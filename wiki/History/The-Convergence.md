# The Convergence

## Status

**Established concept; details still developing.**

The Convergence is the historic settlement that transformed fluid pre-modern frontiers into a more predictable political and economic order.

It did not create the regions from nothing. It **recognized, regularized, and froze many compromises** that had already emerged from centuries of conflict.

## Why it happened

The Convergence followed a period in which:
- trade routes were increasingly dangerous;
- several resource disputes had become economically pointless;
- food crises demonstrated cross-regional dependence;
- piracy and banditry flourished;
- guilds and merchants wanted predictable law;
- rulers wanted recognition of claims they could actually hold.

## Negotiation

High Country is the strongest candidate for the principal congress because of:
- geographic accessibility;
- trade culture;
- existing arbitration traditions;
- relative interest in keeping routes open.

Port likely played an important role in maritime and commercial provisions even if the principal meetings occurred inland.

## What it did

The settlement likely included:

### Border recognition

Not every line was measured precisely. The treaty could define boundaries by:
- rivers;
- ridges;
- roads;
- settlements;
- watersheds;
- named stones;
- old forts.

This creates later ambiguity.

### Trade guarantees

Certain categories of goods gained protected movement.

### Route rules

Passes, major roads, and perhaps sections of The Tunnels received agreed access rules.

### Maritime rules

Neutral shipping and Port access became harder for one kingdom to monopolize.

### Arbitration

Some disputes could be taken to agreed courts or forums rather than immediately fought.

### Neutral Port

Port's special status was formalized or significantly strengthened.

## What it did not solve

- class conflict;
- religious disagreement;
- guild rivalry;
- crime;
- competing interpretations of borders;
- local land disputes;
- old dynastic claims;
- unequal wealth;
- resentment toward outside influence.

## The Council and the Convergence

The Council likely emerged **through** the institutions created by the Convergence rather than appearing fully formed beforehand.

A strong version of the history is:

1. wealthy families helped finance stabilization;
2. rulers increasingly depended on their grain stores, credit, ships, roads, and information;
3. informal coordination among these families prevented crises;
4. coordination became institutional;
5. secrecy grew because open economic rule would provoke resistance;
6. the Council came to believe it was the hidden mechanism keeping the Convergence alive.

This gives the Council a plausible historical reason to think its manipulation is necessary.

## Cultural effect

The Convergence did something paradoxical:

It made regional identities **stronger**.

Once borders and internal peace became more stable:
- governments standardized law;
- schools and religious institutions spread regional norms;
- guilds organized regionally;
- elites cultivated shared histories;
- old mixed frontier identities were sometimes suppressed.

Thus some traditions now presented as ancient "Western Uplands" or "River Country" culture may actually be post-Convergence standardizations of more diverse older practices.
