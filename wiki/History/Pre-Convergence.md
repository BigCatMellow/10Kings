# Pre-Convergence History

## Core rule

The pre-Convergence world was **messy**.

Modern regions should not be projected backward as eternal states with fixed borders. The modern labels describe cultural-geographic zones that emerged from many earlier peoples, towns, kingdoms, confederacies, warbands, guild networks, and migrations.

## General pattern

Before The Convergence:
- borders were poorly surveyed;
- political control followed roads, rivers, fortresses, mines, fields, harbors, and kinship;
- rulers claimed territory they did not effectively govern;
- towns could change allegiance without surrounding countryside doing the same;
- people paid taxes or tribute to multiple authorities;
- marriage could shift a valley more permanently than a battle;
- mercenaries changed sides;
- famine altered alliances;
- raids did not always imply conquest;
- local elites survived regime changes by changing patrons.

## Ironcrest predecessor politics

Ore districts produced:
- mine-holding clans;
- fortified smith towns;
- valley lordships;
- merchant-backed warbands;
- craft alliances.

Wars over ore rarely produced permanent frontiers.

One ruler might seize a mine while losing the road that made it profitable.

## Northwind predecessor politics

Power was divided among:
- coastal clans;
- harbor confederacies;
- island rulers;
- fishing-right alliances;
- raiding fleets.

Maritime rights overlapped political territory.

A defeated clan could lose a town yet continue fishing from allied harbors.

## Greenvale predecessor politics

The lowlands contained:
- estate rulers;
- village leagues;
- river towns;
- grain confederacies;
- irrigation associations.

Crop failures repeatedly destabilized politics.

Greenvale groups sometimes raided neighboring territory for food or attempted expansion into healthier land.

## Highridge predecessor politics

The plateau was divided among:
- toll lords;
- caravan families;
- herding confederacies;
- fortified pass towns;
- merchant coalitions.

Power followed route control rather than continuous land.

## Deepwood predecessor politics

Deepwood was a patchwork of:
- village alliances;
- forest clans;
- river settlements;
- warden networks;
- religious centers;
- hunting territories.

Forest edges expanded and contracted as outsiders cleared land and local groups later reclaimed it.

## Sunplains predecessor politics

The warmer south/east contained:
- city-states;
- irrigation leagues;
- merchant ports;
- noble estates;
- rural client territories.

Waterworks created unusual political maps: a city could control a canal crossing another ruler's land.

## Working conflict traditions

Earlier brainstorming named several conflicts. These are useful as **provisional historical episodes**, not yet hard canon:

- Ore Scar Strife;
- Lowland Famine Raids;
- Coldwave Incursions;
- Ridge Pass Toll Wars;
- Orchard-Lord Feuds;
- Deepwood Edge Skirmishes;
- Iron-Field Conquest;
- Great Coastal Skirmish;
- Highridge Consolidation War.

The final history should revise these into overlapping campaigns with multiple participants, reversals, and local consequences rather than clean one-on-one wars.

## Material traces

Modern landscapes should preserve pre-Convergence history:
- ruined towers;
- roads that lead to vanished markets;
- duplicate fortifications;
- abandoned terraces;
- boundary stones in the wrong kingdom;
- shrines maintained by people now living across the border;
- towns with two legal traditions;
- noble claims based on ancient marriages;
- old canals still controlled under obsolete treaties.

## Why The Convergence became possible

Peace did not occur because everyone suddenly became enlightened.

It became attractive because:
- trade losses accumulated;
- wars repeatedly failed to secure permanent gains;
- famine and blight exposed interdependence;
- guilds wanted predictable markets;
- merchants wanted safe routes;
- rulers wanted borders recognized;
- local populations were exhausted;
- bandits and independent warlords profited from disorder;
- no major power could impose a durable continental settlement alone.

The Convergence was therefore a bargain among tired, self-interested powers that also happened to create real public benefits.
