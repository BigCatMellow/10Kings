# Social Fabric

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level.**

This page owns the setting's cross-cutting social model: how a person's regional, political, religious, professional, familial, class, local, and voluntary identities overlap.

It does **not** make every proposed cult, faction, guild, kingdom, or social movement canon. Specific groups remain owned by their own pages and keep their own status.

## Regional identity is not residence

A person's cultural tradition, birthplace, current residence and political citizenship can all differ.

Examples:

- a North Coast family may have lived in a Old Cities port for four generations;
- a Longwood-derived community may occupy a large forest far outside the Longwood core;
- a Low Rivers milling family may settle along a distant river and retain some inherited practices while adopting local ones;
- a Port-born person may have ancestry from several traditions and identify primarily as Port;
- one village can contain households associated with several regional traditions.

Regional identity should therefore be treated as **one social affiliation among several**, not a coordinate on the map.

## Mixed communities create local culture

Cross-membership is not only coexistence.

A neighborhood containing several cultural traditions can eventually create customs that belong to the neighborhood itself.

Possible examples:

- a mixed market dialect;
- a local marriage custom;
- a work song combining several traditions;
- a new food;
- a shared holiday practice;
- a building layout adapted from several household traditions;
- a local style of dress.

After enough generations, residents may defend the local version against both source cultures.

This is especially likely in:

- Port;
- border towns;
- caravan settlements;
- mining towns;
- river markets;
- migrant farming districts;
- old military settlements.

## Core rule

A person in Two Sons should almost never be explainable by one label.

Someone can simultaneously be:

- born in Low Rivers;
- resident in Port;
- married into a North Coast family;
- member of a shipping guild;
- follower of the Infinite Compass;
- participant in a trans-regional audience movement;
- debtor to an Stone Hills lender;
- veteran of a High Roads caravan company;
- politically loyal to a local city council;
- culturally closer to a border town than to the official identity of either neighboring region.

These identities can reinforce one another, contradict one another, or matter differently depending on the situation.

**Region is not destiny. Institution is not personality. Membership is not total allegiance.**

## The identity stack

The setting currently supports at least these overlapping layers.

| Layer | Typical examples | What it can provide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Place | village, harbor, district, border town, Port neighborhood | daily belonging, accent, local obligations |
| Region | Stone Hills, North Coast, Low Rivers, High Roads, Longwood, Old Cities | broad cultural memory and stereotypes |
| Polity | kingdom, city-state, confederation, clan authority, charter town | law, taxes, military obligations, political legitimacy |
| Kinship | household, lineage, marriage network, clan | inheritance, care, feuds, migration links |
| Class / economic position | laborer, tenant, merchant, owner, noble, debtor | material interests and access |
| Guild / profession | smith, navigator, caravan organization, healer, scribe | training, welfare, contracts, reputation |
| Faith | one of the major trans-regional traditions and its local schools | moral language, ritual, charity, community |
| Voluntary movement | reform circle, audience movement, mutual-aid group, political society | chosen identity, status, activism, belonging |
| Informal network | tavern circle, veterans, smugglers, school cohort, neighbors | trust and information outside formal institutions |
| Generation | pre-Convergence memory, postwar cohort, younger urban generation | different assumptions about what is normal |
| Personal history | debt, migration, grief, friendship, trauma, ambition | why two otherwise similar people act differently |

Not every person needs all of these described. The rule is that several are usually present whether the story foregrounds them or not.

## Known institutional ecology

### Regional cultures

See [Culture and Influence](Culture/Culture-and-Influence.md) and [Regional Social Dynamics](Culture/Regional-Social-Dynamics.md).

Regional identities matter, but they are internally diverse and strongest when contrasted with local, class, professional, religious, and border identities.

### Kingdoms and local government

See [Kingdoms and Politics](Politics/Kingdoms-and-Politics.md).

Visible political authority is fragmented. Regions can contain kingdoms, city-states, autonomous districts, clan systems, charter towns, noble privileges, and contested jurisdictions.

### Religions

See [Religions](Politics/Religions.md).

The nine major traditions cross political borders. Their schools, charities, archives, pilgrim routes, mediators, teachers, and reform movements create social ties that rulers do not fully control.

### Guilds

See [Guilds](Politics/Guilds.md).

Guilds combine profession, welfare, status, regulation, politics, and sometimes coercion. A guild member may disagree with guild leadership while still depending on guild benefits.

### Crime and informal economies

See [Crime and the Underworld](Politics/Crime-and-Underworld.md).

Smuggling, protection, illicit credit, contraband, forged records, and unofficial transport create another layer of practical affiliation.

### Itinerant and pilgrimage networks

See [Nomads](Culture/Nomads.md) and the movement of caravans, sailors, pilgrims, performers, scholars, and seasonal labor.

These networks connect people who may never share government or regional identity.

### Participatory social movements

See [Audience Movements](Culture/Audience-Movements.md).

Some communities are built less around doctrine or profession than around shared participation, memory, personalities, stories, events, and status inside the group.

They can become cult-like or religious without necessarily replacing a person's existing faith.

## Institutions should provide something real

A durable institution survives because membership has benefits.

A major group should normally provide several of the following:

- work;
- training;
- credit;
- legal help;
- food or emergency aid;
- burial or funeral support;
- marriage and friendship networks;
- travel assistance;
- lodging;
- status;
- entertainment;
- identity;
- protection;
- information;
- archives and memory;
- a way to make sense of events.

This is why harmful institutions can remain popular.

People may resent leaders while fearing what disappears if the institution collapses.

## Cross-membership is normal

The same people should recur across institutional systems.

Examples:

- a Harmonious Path mediator is also a caravan-guild accountant;
- a Longwood timber worker belongs to the Eternal Forge through craft lineage;
- a North Coast sailor participates in an Audience movement whose most important gathering is held in Port;
- a Low Rivers farmer has cousins in Old Cities and sells through a guild financed by a Council-linked family;
- a Port smuggler funds a Radiant Path charity because it protected his family;
- a noble heir privately participates in a movement considered vulgar by the court;
- a guild reformer and guild conservative may attend the same temple and marry into the same extended family.

This creates conflicts that cannot be solved by asking which "side" a person belongs to.

## Why people stay — and why they move

Cultural spread does not mean movement is frictionless.

People often remain in difficult places because a place contains **accumulated social and material capital** that cannot simply be packed into a cart.

Reasons to stay can include:

- kin and caregiving networks;
- inherited property or use rights;
- workshops, boats, tools or irrigation access;
- guild standing and professional reputation;
- local credit and trusted customers;
- legal status;
- community obligations;
- faith institutions;
- language;
- childcare;
- knowledge of local routes, weather, soil, water or hazards;
- emotional attachment and historical memory.

Leaving can mean losing several of these at once.

A highly skilled worker may discover that their reputation, certification, customer network or access to specialized infrastructure does not transfer cleanly elsewhere.

A household may remain because an elder depends on relatives nearby, because a lease cannot be sold, because a guild credential is only recognized locally, or because moving requires cash they do not have.

This should **not** become cultural determinism.

People still move because of:

- marriage;
- recruitment;
- trade;
- pilgrimage;
- better wages;
- education;
- debt;
- displacement;
- persecution;
- war;
- ecological loss;
- family reunification;
- simple preference.

Migration is therefore usually a **household and institutional decision under constraint**, not a statement that one culture naturally belongs in one landscape.

This helps explain both:

- why regional cultural cores persist over long periods;
- why diaspora and mixed communities still form throughout the world.

## Social circulation follows physical circulation

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md) for the world-level carrier and propagation model.

The same routes that move goods also move:

- songs;
- rumors;
- religious arguments;
- jokes;
- fashions;
- political slogans;
- cult practices;
- technical ideas;
- scandal;
- reputations;
- marriage connections;
- stories about distant places.

That makes [Port](Places/Port.md) and [High Roads](Regions/Highridge-Plateau.md) social and cultural switching points as well as economic ones. The same road does not carry all information equally: sailors, pilgrims, guild members, migrant workers, itinerant households, merchants, and children transmit different material through overlapping routes.

A caravan route can carry a new prayer. A pilgrimage can spread a labor grievance. A guild dispute can become a popular song. A cult joke can become a political insult in another kingdom. A refugee community can preserve a version of history that the government where it now lives considers subversive.

## Social chokepoints

Potential social chokepoints include:

- guild halls;
- shrines;
- caravanserais;
- inns and public houses;
- docks;
- schools;
- archives;
- festivals;
- courts;
- markets;
- message houses;
- copying shops where available;
- pilgrimage stops;
- famous performers or speakers;
- trusted translators;
- people who can move between otherwise hostile groups.

A person can be a chokepoint.

Someone trusted by two rival movements may matter more than a formal diplomat.

## How institutions change

Durable institutions accumulate history.

A useful lifecycle is:

```text
practical need / charismatic moment
→ recurring participation
→ shared language and stories
→ informal status
→ rituals and offices
→ property / archives / money
→ factions
→ reform movements
→ schisms
→ nostalgia / restoration movements
```

Not every institution completes the sequence.

## Interaction is more important than inventory

The world does not become richer merely by adding more named groups.

New groups should change existing relationships.

Useful questions:

- Which guild employs followers of several rival faiths?
- Which kingdom relies on a religious network based mostly outside its borders?
- Which cult is tolerated because its festivals are economically valuable?
- Which aristocrat has status in a movement that mocks aristocrats?
- Which smuggling route doubles as a pilgrimage route?
- Which border town treats two "foreign" customs as equally local?
- Which reform movement is funded by people who would dislike its ideology but need its practical work?
- Which supposed enemies share friends, marriages, teachers, or business partners?

## Political consequence

Cross-cutting identities can stabilize the world.

A ruler may hesitate to attack a rival because merchants would lose partners, clergy oppose the war, guild branches exist on both sides, families cross the frontier, or a popular social movement treats the rival population as part of the same community.

They can also accelerate conflict by carrying panic, propaganda, boycotts, recruitment, coordinated protest, and vigilante action.

The Villain therefore does not need to invent movements. He can try to redirect existing ones.

The Council likewise cannot simply "control society." It can pressure institutions whose incentives it understands, while informal and overlapping networks remain harder to model.

## Scene test

A scene with several people from the same region should still contain meaningful social differences.

If everyone from Stone Hills speaks, votes, worships, works, and reacts alike, this model has failed.

If two people from different kingdoms sometimes discover that guild, faith, class, neighborhood, shared movement, or family matters more in the immediate situation than nationality, the world is behaving as intended.
