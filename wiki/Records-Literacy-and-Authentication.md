# Records, Literacy, and Authentication

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level. Exact literacy rates, scripts, seal systems, signatures, notarial offices, writing materials, and document forms remain open.**

This page owns the world-level model for:

- who creates records;
- why written records matter;
- how documents are trusted;
- how copies and translations are handled;
- how literacy varies;
- how fraud and record loss work.

It does **not** establish one universal documentary system.

# 1. Core rule

A written record is not automatically true.

Its authority comes from context.

People may trust a document because:

- they know who created it;
- the creator had recognized authority;
- witnesses were present;
- the form is familiar;
- a seal, mark, signature, or other authentication is recognized;
- a copy exists elsewhere;
- it appears in an institutional register;
- later records refer back to it;
- the physical object has remained in trusted custody.

Therefore:

> **documentary authority is institutional, not magical.**

---

# 2. Literacy is not one yes/no category

A person may be able to:

- recognize names;
- read prices;
- read guild marks;
- keep simple accounts;
- read a religious text;
- read formal legal prose;
- compose a letter;
- understand several scripts;
- copy text without fully understanding it.

Those are different skills.

Do not divide the world into simply:

- literate;
- illiterate.

Use **task-specific literacy**.

---

# 3. Practical literacy can be widespread in some professions

Current owners already make written skill especially useful for:

- merchants;
- clerks;
- guild officials;
- scribes;
- lenders;
- warehouse staff;
- route authorities;
- courts;
- religious archives;
- scholars;
- administrators.

A craft worker may be comfortable with:

- measurements;
- marks;
- accounts;
- labels;
- contracts;

without reading literature easily.

A sailor may read:

- cargo marks;
- harbor notices;
- route notes;

without being trained in legal drafting.

---

# 4. Literacy should vary by class, place, and occupation

Likely differences include:

- urban vs remote;
- merchant vs subsistence household;
- guild-trained vs informal worker;
- elite vs poor;
- religious-school access vs no schooling;
- settled vs highly mobile;
- Port/High Roads vs isolated communities.

No continent-wide literacy percentage is established.

Do not invent one until education and polity structure are firmer.

---

# 5. Written records coexist with oral authority

A world with documents still depends on:

- witnesses;
- memory;
- reputation;
- oath;
- oral agreements;
- community knowledge.

Some institutions may prefer writing.

Others may treat living witnesses or customary memory as equally important.

Current law explicitly rejects the assumption that writing always defeats testimony.

# 6. Different institutions keep different records

## Households and kin

Possible records:

- family trees;
- debts;
- marriages;
- births;
- deaths;
- property;
- letters.

Much may also remain oral.

## Towns and polities

Possible records:

- taxes;
- land;
- judgments;
- offices;
- laws;
- census-like counts where locally useful;
- public works.

## Guilds

Likely records:

- apprenticeship;
- certification;
- dues;
- sanctions;
- contracts;
- standards;
- membership.

## Religious institutions

Likely records:

- rites;
- burials;
- marriages where relevant;
- charity;
- genealogy;
- pilgrimage;
- archives;
- teaching texts.

## Merchants and lenders

Likely records:

- accounts;
- debts;
- cargo;
- warehouse claims;
- insurance-like arrangements;
- letters;
- contracts.

## Route and harbor institutions

Likely records:

- tolls;
- cargo;
- arrivals;
- departures;
- route status;

- fees.

Different record systems can overlap without being identical.

---

# 7. Port and High Roads are documentary switching points

Port and High Roads already handle:

- multilingual contracts;
- arbitration;
- dates;
- measures;
- currencies;
- routes;
- foreign merchants.

That creates demand for people who can:

- read several forms;
- compare copies;
- translate;
- recognize trusted marks;
- explain foreign records;
- preserve archives.

Their importance comes from **document compatibility**, not just raw literacy.

---

# 8. Authentication can use several mechanisms

The current world does not yet establish one universal method.

Plausible mechanisms include:

- witnesses;
- named scribes;
- seals;
- stamps;
- signatures;
- personal marks;
- guild marks;
- registered copies;
- counterpart documents;
- official custody;
- distinctive materials;
- tied or bound pages;
- recorded issue numbers.

Each can exist locally.

None should be promoted globally without support.

---

# 9. Witnesses remain important even in written systems

A witness can confirm:

- who agreed;
- whether consent was given;
- whether goods changed hands;
- whether a document was altered;
- whether a seal was applied properly.

This matters when:

- one party cannot read;
- a language translation is involved;
- a document is later lost;
- authenticity is disputed.

Written and oral evidence reinforce each other.

---

# 10. Scribes are not automatically neutral

A scribe may be:

- employee;
- guild member;
- religious official;
- court clerk;
- merchant clerk;
- freelance writer;
- household servant.

Their interests matter.

A record can reflect:

- what the scribe understood;
- what the patron wanted;
- what the institution's form allowed.

Do not treat scribes as invisible recording machines.

---

# 11. A signature is not assumed

The world has not established modern signature practice.

People may authenticate through:

- written name;
- mark;
- seal;
- witness;
- registered scribe;
- guild sign;
- household sign.

Different systems can coexist.

A person who cannot write can still enter enforceable agreements.

---

# 12. A seal is not automatically proof

If seals exist, they can be:

- stolen;
- copied;
- misused;
- applied under coercion;
- used after an office-holder dies.

Therefore trustworthy systems need more than:

> it has the right seal.

Possible supporting checks include:

- witness;
- register;
- known messenger;
- handwriting;
- duplicate copy;
- chain of custody.

Exact systems remain local.

---

# 13. Copies are normal but create problems

Important documents may exist in several copies.

Reasons include:

- each party keeps one;
- a guild keeps one;
- a court registers one;
- a translated copy is required;
- a distant office needs a copy.

Copies can diverge.

Disputes may concern:

- missing lines;
- translation;
- dates;
- spelling;
- seals;
- later additions.

A "copy" is not automatically fraudulent.

---

# 14. Translation creates multiple authoritative texts

See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

A treaty or contract may exist in several languages.

Those versions may not map perfectly.

Possible systems include:

- one legally controlling version;
- equal authoritative versions;
- a neutral reference copy;
- arbitration when versions disagree.

Exact Convergence practice remains open.

The important point is:

> multilingual law creates textual politics.

---

# 15. Dates are part of authentication

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md).

A record may need:

- local date;
- converted date;
- season;
- market cycle;
- office year;
- ruler year;
- witness date.

Calendar ambiguity can make a genuine document difficult to interpret.

A later copy may "modernize" the date and accidentally create confusion.

---

# 16. Names are part of authentication

See [Naming](Culture/Naming.md).

A person or place may have:

- local name;
- translated name;
- legal standard name;
- old name;
- commercial nickname.

Documents can disagree without referring to different entities.

That makes clerks and local knowledge important.

---

# 17. Institutional registers create trust

A standalone document can be easier to forge than a document that also appears in a separate register.

A register might record:

- contract;
- marriage;
- apprenticeship;
- cargo claim;
- land transfer;
- court judgment.

This creates a useful authentication pattern:

~~~text
document held by party
+
entry held by institution
+
witness / mark
→ stronger confidence
~~~

Exact registry systems remain local.

---

# 18. Records can be distributed

No important institution needs one central archive.

Records may be held by:

- local offices;
- guild branches;
- religious houses;
- merchant houses;
- courts;
- Port;
- family archives.

Distributed records can be resilient.

They can also contradict each other.

---

# 19. Archives are vulnerable physical infrastructure

Records can be destroyed by:

- fire;
- flood;
- war;
- damp;
- insects;
- theft;
- deliberate purge;
- simple neglect.

Archive survival should not be assumed.

A legal system can lose proof.

A family can lose genealogy.

A polity can lose tax history.

That creates historical uncertainty naturally.

---

# 20. Record survival is biased

Documents are more likely to survive when created by institutions with:

- money;
- stable buildings;
- trained staff;
- copying habits;
- political protection.

Poor households, migrants, criminals, and marginal communities may leave fewer surviving written records.

This creates a historical bias:

> **what is well documented is not necessarily what mattered most.**

See [Contested Historical Memory](History/Contested-Memory.md).

---

# 21. Religious archives may preserve material governments lose

Religious institutions can survive dynastic or local political changes.

Their archives may preserve:

- births;
- deaths;
- marriages;
- donations;
- disputes;
- pilgrimage;
- local disasters;
- genealogies.

That gives them political value even when they lack coercive power.

It also creates conflict over access.

---

# 22. Guild archives create professional memory

Guild records can preserve:

- who trained whom;
- certifications;
- old techniques;
- contracts;
- disciplinary cases;
- project histories.

A guild may use records to defend:

- monopoly;
- seniority;
- quality claims;
- inherited privilege.

Records are therefore political assets.

---

# 23. Commercial ledgers are power

See [Money, Credit, Exchange, and Measures](Money-Credit-and-Measures.md).

A merchant or lender who knows:

- debts;
- prices;
- defaults;
- inventory;
- delivery history;

possesses leverage.

The Council's information advantage can grow from combining ordinary commercial records across networks.

It does not require magical surveillance.

---

# 24. The Council should have records but not one perfect master archive

See [Economic Council](Politics/Economic-Council.md).

Current Council design supports:

- retained records;
- member-house archives;
- institutional memory;
- compartmentation.

A strong model is:

> each house maintains its own records, while selected summaries or copies circulate through Council coordination.

That preserves:

- secrecy;
- asymmetry;
- vulnerability;
- disagreement.

Do not invent one omniscient central database.

Exact Council record practice remains open.

---

# 25. Access to records is a form of power

Questions include:

- Who may inspect a land register?
- Can a debtor see the lender's account?
- Can a guild apprentice see disciplinary records?
- Can a foreign merchant inspect a Port ruling?
- Can a family read a religious genealogy?

Records can be:

- public;
- private;
- membership-only;
- fee-based;
- sealed;
- restricted by office.

The right to read can matter as much as the right to write.

---

# 26. Literacy creates intermediaries

People who cannot use a particular written register may depend on:

- scribes;
- interpreters;
- clerks;
- priests;
- guild officers;
- merchants;
- family members.

That creates risks:

- dependence;
- fees;
- misinformation;
- exploitation.

It also creates trusted professions and social relationships.

---

# 27. Children and migrants can become document brokers

A multilingual child or younger family member may be asked to read:

- notices;
- letters;
- contracts.

A migrant may understand speech but not the local legal script/register.

This can create a gap between conversational fluency and documentary power.

---

# 28. Public notice does not require universal literacy

Authorities can communicate through combinations of:

- posted text;
- proclamation;
- town criers;
- market announcements;
- guild notices;
- religious gatherings;
- messenger networks.

A written notice can create a durable official version even when many people hear it orally.

---

# 29. Documentary form can signal authority

People learn to recognize:

- layout;
- materials;
- seals;
- colors;
- formulaic openings;
- office titles;
- witnesses.

This can make a document look official even to someone who cannot read every word.

That also makes forged form powerful.

# 30. Forgery is a systemic risk

See [Crime and the Underworld](Politics/Crime-and-Underworld.md).

Possible fraud includes:

- false seals;
- altered dates;
- invented witnesses;
- fake guild certification;
- changed cargo manifests;
- duplicate land claims;
- substituted pages;
- false translations.

Forgery works best where several systems overlap and verification is slow.

---

# 31. Good forgery requires institutional knowledge

A believable forged document needs more than handwriting.

The forger may need to know:

- correct form;
- office-holder;
- calendar;
- terminology;
- language;
- seal;
- expected route;
- corresponding register.

This makes forgery an information problem.

---

# 32. Verification takes time

A suspicious document may need to be checked against:

- distant archive;
- guild branch;
- witness;
- court;
- merchant house.

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

Verification therefore has network latency.

A false document can cause action before it is disproved.

---

# 33. Physical originals matter differently by institution

Some institutions may care strongly about an original.

Others may accept:

- registered copies;
- sworn copies;
- recognized translations;
- guild duplicates.

Exact practice remains open.

Do not impose a modern "original document" concept everywhere.

- marriage;

- customary use.

Therefore land evidence may include:

- deeds;
- tax records;
- oral memory;
- markers;
- maps;

No single document should solve every ancient land dispute.

# 35. Border records are especially political

A border can be described in:

- treaty text;
- map;
- tax records;
- local place names;
- survey markers;
- oral tradition.

Those sources may conflict.

That makes border disputes documentary as well as territorial.

---

# 36. Mobile people create record challenges

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

Mobile households may need records for:

- contracts;
- guild status;
- property;
- births;
- marriages;
- debts;

- in a religious house;

Mobility can complicate documentary identity without implying lawlessness.

---

# 37. Record systems shape identity

A person can exist socially before an institution recognizes them.

Questions such as:

- Is this child recorded?
- Is this marriage recognized?
- Is this apprentice registered?
- Does this migrant appear in the town books?

can affect access to:

- inheritance;
- work;
- courts;
- property;
- citizenship.

Exact identity-document systems are not established.

Avoid modern universal ID assumptions.

---

# 38. Recordkeeping can expand after the Convergence

The Convergence increases demand for:

- treaty copies;
- standardized names;
- routes;
- contracts;
- arbitration records;
- dates;
- recognized borders.

Therefore post-Convergence institutions probably create **more records** than many predecessors.

That does not mean centralized bureaucracy everywhere.

It means cross-border interoperability rewards documentation.

---

# 39. Standardization creates exclusion

A standardized legal form can help trade while disadvantaging people who:

- use another language;
- cannot afford a scribe;
- follow oral custom;
- live far from registry offices.

Administrative improvement can therefore create new inequalities.

This fits the larger post-Convergence pattern.

---

# 40. Historical truth can depend on archives that were never neutral

Current historians may inherit records created by:

- governments;
- guilds;
- wealthy families;
- religious institutions;
- merchants.

Each preserved what mattered to them.

Therefore:

> **the archive is evidence, not omniscience.**

This supports [Contested Historical Memory](History/Contested-Memory.md).

# 44. Design rule

When adding a document, ask:

1. Who created it?
2. Why?
3. In which language/register?
4. Who can read it?
5. How is it dated?
6. How is it authenticated?
7. Who witnessed it?
8. Where is another copy or register entry?
9. Who keeps it?
10. Who is allowed to inspect it?
11. How could it be forged?
12. How could it be lost?
13. What happens if another record disagrees?

Do not begin with:

> it has an official seal.

Begin with:

> **what institution makes people trust this record, and how can that trust fail?**
