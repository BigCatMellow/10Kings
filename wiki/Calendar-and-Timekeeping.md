**Working canon at the structural level. Exact calendar names, month systems, week lengths, eras, and date notation remain open.**

# 1. Core rule

The world does not need one calendar to coordinate.

Different people can organize time through overlapping systems:

- astronomical time;
- agricultural seasons;
- sailing seasons;
- pass and caravan seasons;
- religious observance;
- civic dates;
- legal deadlines;
- market cycles;
- fiscal periods;
- guild schedules;
- household anniversaries.

A single person may use several at once.

The useful distinction is:

> **timekeeping tells people when something is; calendars tell institutions how to name and organize that time.**

Those do not need to be identical everywhere.

# 4. Seasonal thresholds can matter more than fixed dates

Many real activities depend on conditions rather than a printed date.

Useful examples:

- first safe sailing;
- river thaw;
- pass reopening;
- first irrigation release;
- livestock departure;
- harvest readiness;
- storm season;
- first frost;
- navigable water level.

Therefore an obligation can be defined by an event such as:

> payment due at the first major market after the pass reopens

rather than:

> payment due on the twelfth day of a universal month.

This is especially useful where climate varies substantially across the world.

---

# 5. Seasonal time is locally observed

A seasonal threshold may be obvious locally but difficult to use across long distances.

Questions arise:

- Who decides the pass is open?
- Which harbor declares the sailing season?
- Which irrigation authority announces release?
- What counts as the harvest being complete?
- What if two valleys thaw at different times?

This creates institutional roles for:

- route authorities;
- harbor officials;
- irrigation associations;
- market organizers;
- guilds;
- local councils;
- religious observers where custom gives them a role.

Do not assume astronomy alone determines practical seasons.

---

# 6. Cross-border trade requires unambiguous timing

Long-distance contracts need more precision than:

> sometime after winter.

This creates a **derived constraint**:

> **Cross-border institutions need reliable ways to translate dates and deadlines between local systems.**

That does **not** require one universal calendar.

Possible solutions include:

- dual dating;
- commercial date tables;
- local date plus recognized conversion;
- a shared trade-era notation;
- named market cycles;
- numbered days within a fiscal period;
- witnesses or clerks responsible for conversion.

Exact method remains open.

# 8. Legal dates must specify whose calendar applies

See [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

A cross-border contract can fail if two parties interpret a date differently.

Therefore important agreements may need to clarify:

- governing calendar;
- local equivalent;
- deadline trigger;
- place of performance;
- what happens if a seasonal route is closed.

This creates realistic disputes over:

- late payment;
- missed delivery;
- expired claims;
- apprenticeship completion;
- tax deadlines;
- inheritance filings;
- court appearance.

Calendar ambiguity can become legal ambiguity.

---

# 9. Weather can make a legal date physically impossible

The current world retains material travel limits.

A court can order someone to appear on a date that weather makes unreachable.

That creates questions such as:

- Is delay excused?
- Who proves the road was closed?
- Does a contract include a seasonal exception?
- Can a ship delay extend a debt?
- Does a caravan failure count as force beyond control?

Exact legal doctrines remain local.

The structural point is:

> **fixed dates and physical reality can conflict.**

---

# 10. Time standards can spread without replacing local calendars

Merchants may use one date notation while households use another.

A city can publish legal dates in a standardized form while residents continue saying:

- two weeks after first thaw;
- three market days before harvest;
- the night before the local vigil.

This is normal layered culture.

Do not assume standardization means cultural replacement.

# 15. Months are not currently established

Legacy material uses "month" generically.

# 17. Fiscal time creates its own culture

- account closing;
- rent collection;
- tax due dates;

- guild dues.

# 18. Markets can be calendars

A person may remember time through recurring markets.

Examples:

- next cattle market;
- second market after thaw;
- three fairs before winter;
- the Port autumn exchange.

This is especially useful in places where:

- literacy is uneven;
- local calendars differ;
- trade is frequent.

Market-based time can coexist with written legal dating.

# 19. Travel networks create timetable culture

Repeated routes create expectations.

People may know:

- when caravans usually arrive;
- when ships usually depart;
- when pilgrims pass;
- when mobile households return;
- when roads become dangerous.

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

A late caravan is therefore news.

A missing seasonal household can be alarming.

A ship arriving early can move prices before official reports do.

# 20. Mobile circuits are calendars in motion

A circuit-based household organizes the year around:

- work;
- pasture;
- weather;
- kin;
- markets;
- pilgrimage;
- shelter.

Its calendar may therefore be expressed as a sequence of places and obligations.

Example structure:

~~~text
winter quarter
→ spring repair work
→ upland summer pasture
→ late-summer fair
→ harvest labor
→ autumn market
→ winter quarter
~~~

This is a scheduling system even if no one writes it as twelve months.

# 22. Pilgrimage creates synchronization pressure

A major pilgrimage requires people to know:

- when to leave;
- when roads are safe;
- when hospitality sites expect travelers;
- when the central observance occurs.

Therefore large pilgrimages are one of the strongest reasons for cross-regional date coordination.

That does not mean all faiths need universal calendars.

It means a major pilgrimage network must solve the synchronization problem somehow.

A farmer may need:

- daylight;
- weather;
- season.

A harbor may need:

- tides;
- watch changes;
- departure windows.

A court may need:

- a named day;
- a session period.

An astronomer or navigator may need finer observation.

A lender may need an exact due date.

Therefore "what time is it?" has different levels of precision depending on context.

# 34. Diaspora communities can preserve older calendars

A migrant community may preserve:

- an old festival date;
- an old month name;
- an older new-year convention;
- a homeland market cycle.

Meanwhile the place of origin may change.

This can create diaspora traditions that are historically older than "authentic" homeland practice.

---

# 35. Contact zones can become calendar translators

Border towns and mixed cities may routinely manage:

- multiple date systems;
- multiple religious schedules;
- different work seasons.

People there may become unusually comfortable saying:

> that's the third market day in their calendar, but the first week of ours.

Calendar fluency is another form of border competence.

---

# 36. Calendar standardization should have a reason

Do not create one standard calendar merely because authors prefer simplicity.

A shared system should arise only if institutions need it.

Strong reasons include:

- contracts;
- taxation;
- courts;
- shipping;
- major pilgrimages;
- military coordination;
- Convergence administration.

Even then, standardization may apply only to official documents.

---

# 37. Calendar nonstandardization also has costs

Multiple systems create:

- clerical work;
- mistakes;
- disputes;
- fraud;
- missed deadlines;
- outsider disadvantage.

Those costs can become part of ordinary life.

A calendar converter or clerk may be socially boring but economically important.

That is useful middle worldbuilding.

---

# 38. Current best model

The strongest current model is:

~~~text
LOCAL / CULTURAL CALENDARS
for household, religion, civic identity, local work

        +

SEASONAL THRESHOLDS
for agriculture, sailing, passes, herding, rivers

        +

INSTITUTIONAL / COMMERCIAL CONVENTIONS
for contracts, courts, trade, debt, cross-border coordination

        +

DATE CONVERSION
where systems meet
~~~

This produces interoperability without cultural uniformity.

# 39. What is established versus open

## Structurally established

- several overlapping time systems exist;
- seasonal thresholds matter materially;
- work calendars differ by occupation and ecology;
- festivals do not all share one calendar;
- cross-border contracts require unambiguous timing;
- mobile circuits are seasonally coordinated;
- religious and civic observances can use different schedules;
- calendar conversion is a real cross-cultural need.

## Derived constraints

- major trade/legal hubs need some method of translating dates;
- large pilgrimage networks need synchronization;
- treaty-era commerce likely increased pressure for interoperable dating;
- seasonal closure requires contract/legal exceptions somewhere;
- record systems need enough date precision for inheritance, debt, and legal claims.

## Open

- astronomical year length;
- moon system;
- number of months;
- month names;
- month lengths;
- week structure;
- day names;
- start of year;
- intercalation;
- official eras;
- whether a Convergence trade calendar exists;
- exact date notation;
- Port legal calendar;
- High Roads commercial calendar;
- religious calendar details;
- polity-specific civic calendars;
- exact timekeeping technology;
- exact market cadence.

# 40. Design rule

When adding a date or calendar detail, ask:

1. Who needs this date?
2. For what activity?
3. How precise does it need to be?
4. Is it seasonal, religious, civic, legal, or commercial?
5. Which institution maintains it?
6. Does another group use a different system?
7. How are the two converted?
8. What happens when weather breaks the schedule?
9. Who benefits from the chosen standard?
10. What older local system survives underneath it?

Do not begin by naming twelve fantasy months.

Begin with:

> **What coordination problem needs a calendar here?**
