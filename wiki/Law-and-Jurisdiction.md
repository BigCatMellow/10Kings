# Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level.**

This page owns the world-level model for **who has authority over which disputes, why judgments are obeyed, and what happens when jurisdictions overlap**.

It does **not** establish:

- one universal legal code;
- one continent-wide court hierarchy;
- one universal appeal process;
- exact criminal penalties;
- exact citizenship rules;
- exact extradition rules;
- exact marriage or inheritance law;
- exact court names;
- exact legal procedure in every polity.

Those remain local or unresolved unless another owner establishes them.

# 1. Core rule

The world does **not** have one clean ladder of authority.

A person's obligations can come from several places at once:

- local custom;
- village or town authority;
- a ruler or city-state;
- a guild;
- a religious institution;
- a trade contract;
- a route authority;
- Port law;
- a Convergence-era treaty or access guarantee.

Therefore:

> **Jurisdiction depends on the dispute, the people involved, the place where it happened, and the institutions whose cooperation is needed to enforce the result.**

The important question is not simply:

> Who is the highest authority?

It is:

> **Who can plausibly claim this dispute, and whose judgment will the relevant people actually honor?**

---

# 2. The Convergence did not create one legal system

The [Convergence](History/The-Convergence.md) made several kinds of conflict more predictable.

Current owners support that it regularized some combination of:

- borders;
- protected passage;
- trade;
- major routes;
- maritime access;
- arbitration;
- Port neutrality.

It did **not** erase local law.

That means the post-Convergence order is best understood as:

```text
local / political law
+ professional and customary rules
+ cross-border treaty obligations
+ agreed arbitration where needed
```

The Convergence creates a **framework for coexistence**, not a single code that governs every household and crime.

---

# 3. Jurisdiction is layered

A useful structural model is:

## Local authority

Most ordinary disputes begin locally.

Possible authorities already supported by current politics include:

- village institutions;
- town councils;
- local courts;
- clan authorities;
- city councils;
- noble or royal authorities;
- local assemblies.

These are likely to handle many questions involving:

- property;
- neighborhood disputes;
- local violence;
- local debt;
- market behavior;
- family matters;
- land use;
- local taxes.

Exact procedure varies by polity.

## Political authority

Larger governments may claim authority over:

- taxation;
- serious crime;
- military matters;
- land title;
- major infrastructure;
- high-value disputes;
- appeals or review where such systems exist;
- relations with other polities.

The exact division between local and central power varies by region.

## Guild authority

Guilds already regulate:

- apprenticeship;
- professional standards;
- contracts;
- membership;
- market access;
- quality;
- some member welfare.

A guild can therefore have real authority over **professional disputes**, especially where local governments recognize guild privilege.

But guild authority is not universal sovereignty.

A guild cannot automatically decide:

- murder;
- dynastic succession;
- every land dispute;
- crimes by nonmembers;

unless a local polity has explicitly given it such authority.

## Religious authority

Religions already provide:

- mediation;
- moral testimony;
- charity;
- rites;
- records;
- counseling;
- community welfare.

Some communities may voluntarily accept religious mediation.

Some governments may recognize religious records or settlements.

But current canon does **not** establish one universal religious court system.

Religious influence should therefore vary by:

- faith;
- locality;
- polity;
- subject matter;
- whether all parties accept it.

## Treaty / Convergence authority

Cross-border issues may trigger agreed Convergence-era rules or arbitration.

Likely subjects include:

- protected passage;
- route access;
- border interpretation;
- major trade disputes;
- maritime access;
- Port neutrality;
- treaty obligations.

Exact institutions remain open.

## Port authority

Port has unusually strong neutral commercial institutions because many outsiders need to trust transactions there.

Current Port canon supports:

- neutral courts;
- arbitration;
- standardized weights and measures;
- protected foreign merchants;
- warehouse law;
- contract enforcement;
- quarantine and harbor rules.

Port law is important because people choose to transact there.

It is **not** universal law for the whole world.

## Route / High Roads authority

High Roads already contains:

- route authorities;
- caravan guilds;
- toll/customs houses;
- arbitration halls;
- merchant houses;
- local political bodies.

That makes it a strong environment for specialized disputes involving:

- caravans;
- tolls;
- damaged cargo;
- route access;
- guide obligations;
- transport contracts;
- cross-language agreements.

Exact court structures remain local and unresolved.

## Underpass authority

The [Underpass](Places/The-Underpass.md) is explicitly fragmented.

Authority can come from:

- enclave councils;
- guide families;
- mining fraternities;
- local militias;
- trade coalitions;
- criminal organizations;
- surface powers controlling particular entrances.

There is no basis for one Underpass-wide legal authority.

# 4. The subject matter helps determine the forum

A practical rule:

> **The institution most connected to the obligation usually has the strongest first claim.**

Examples:

### Apprentice dispute

Likely starts with:

- guild rules;
- master/apprentice contract;
- local law governing labor or contracts.

### Broken caravan contract

Likely involves:

- caravan or merchant customs;
- guild or route authorities;
- contract forum named by the parties;
- local court if property or violence is involved.

### Harbor cargo dispute

Likely involves:

- Port commercial law;
- warehouse records;
- shipping practice;
- neutral arbitration.

### Village water dispute

Likely starts with:

- local custom;
- irrigation association;
- village or estate authority;
- relevant political law.

### Cross-border route closure

Likely raises:

- local authority;
- treaty rights;
- Convergence obligations;
- route authorities;
- political negotiation.

The point is not that every case has an obvious answer.

The overlap is the worldbuilding.

---

# 5. Place matters

Where something happens changes who can claim authority.

A merchant may be:

- a citizen of one polity;
- a member of a guild headquartered elsewhere;
- carrying goods owned by a third party;
- traveling under a Convergence-protected route agreement;
- contracting in Port.

That can create several plausible forums.

Therefore legal geography follows the same rule as movement:

> **Routes and institutions matter more than simple map distance.**

A dispute between distant merchant houses may be easy to resolve if both regularly use the same Port court.

A dispute between neighboring valleys may be difficult if they recognize different authorities.

---

# 6. Membership matters

Institutions often gain jurisdiction because someone voluntarily or contractually belongs to them.

Examples:

- guild membership;
- merchant association;
- religious community;
- caravan partnership;
- household or kin group;
- town citizenship.

This creates **personal jurisdiction** alongside territorial jurisdiction.

A guild may discipline a member for professional misconduct even when the act occurred far from the guild's home city.

But its ability to enforce that decision may depend on:

- local recognition;
- member fear of losing guild access;
- contracts;
- reputation;
- other branches honoring the sanction.

---

# 7. Contract can choose a forum

Long-distance trade becomes easier when parties know where a dispute will be heard.

Therefore it is structurally plausible that important contracts sometimes specify:

- the governing custom or law;
- the place of arbitration;
- acceptable witnesses or records;
- weights / measures;
- currency or conversion method.

This does **not** require a universal legal drafting tradition.

It follows from the existing need for predictable cross-border trade.

Port and High Roads are especially plausible places for such practices.

Exact document forms remain open.

---

# 8. Dates and deadlines are jurisdictional too

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md).

Cross-border contracts and legal acts need to specify enough time information that different local calendars do not create accidental ambiguity.

Disputes can arise over:

- which calendar controls;
- whether a seasonal threshold occurred;
- whether weather made performance impossible;
- whether a message arrived after an effective date;
- whether a deadline converts cleanly into another local system.

Port, High Roads, and other cross-border legal environments therefore need practical date-conversion conventions even if the world never adopts one universal calendar.

# 9. Why judgments are obeyed

A judgment matters only if someone can make it costly to ignore.

Possible enforcement already supported by the world includes:

- local political authority;
- seizure of property where lawful;
- denial of market or guild access;
- refusal of future contracts;
- loss of credit;
- reputational damage;
- exclusion from a route or professional network;
- treaty retaliation;
- diplomatic pressure.

This produces an important distinction:

> **Legal authority and enforcement capacity are not always the same thing.**

A claimant may be legally right and still struggle to collect.

A weak local court may depend on a guild, ruler, Port institution, or foreign authority to make its judgment meaningful.

---

# 10. Cross-border enforcement depends on recognition

There is no current basis for assuming every polity automatically enforces every foreign judgment.

A cross-border decision is more likely to matter when:

- a treaty requires recognition;
- both parties use the same guild or commercial network;
- Port or another trusted forum issued the decision;
- reciprocity makes refusal costly;
- merchants fear loss of credit or access;
- political authorities want the broader system to keep functioning.

This makes **recognition** as important as judgment.

---

# 11. Arbitration is central because sovereignty is fragmented

Current sources repeatedly support arbitration as part of the world, especially through:

- the Convergence;
- High Roads traditions;
- Port's commercial role.

Arbitration is useful because parties can accept a forum without surrendering broader sovereignty.

A ruler can say:

> We accept this trade ruling.

without saying:

> This foreign court rules our kingdom.

That makes arbitration a natural tool in this world.

---

# 12. Arbitration should not become a magic solution

Arbitration can fail when:

- one party refuses to appear;
- evidence is disputed;
- translators disagree;
- enforcement is weak;
- a ruler rejects the outcome;
- guild politics interfere;
- the issue involves violence or sovereignty beyond the arbitrator's mandate;
- the parties disagree about which forum has authority.

Therefore the world should contain:

- respected arbitration;
- ignored arbitration;
- competing arbitrators;
- forum shopping;
- settlements made because continued conflict costs more than compromise.

---

# 13. Evidence follows the information system

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

Evidence can include:

- witnesses;
- contracts;
- account books;
- toll records;
- shipping logs;
- land markers;
- maps;
- guild records;
- religious records;
- letters;
- seals or other authentication where established.

But evidence has the same weaknesses as information:

- delay;
- translation;
- forgery;
- missing records;
- biased witnesses;
- conflicting copies;
- political suppression.

A written document should not automatically defeat living testimony everywhere.

Different institutions can value evidence differently.

---

# 14. Language and translation are legal problems

See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

The High Roads and Port are multilingual.

That means disputes can turn on:

- what a promise meant;
- whether a word implied obligation or intention;
- which measurement system was used;
- how a name was recorded;
- whether a translation is authoritative.

This creates practical importance for:

- interpreters;
- scribes;
- bilingual merchants;
- notaries or equivalent record specialists where they exist.

Exact professional structures remain open.

---

# 15. Borders create jurisdictional ambiguity

The Convergence stabilized borders without making every frontier perfectly measured.

Current history explicitly allows borders defined by:

- rivers;
- ridges;
- roads;
- settlements;
- watersheds;
- named stones;
- old forts.

These can move, disappear, or be interpreted differently.

Therefore border law naturally produces disputes over:

- grazing;
- timber;
- mines;
- water;
- tolls;
- tax collection;
- criminal pursuit;
- settlement jurisdiction.

A river changing course can create a legal dispute even if nobody moved.

---

# 16. Cultural borders and political borders do not match

See [Kingdoms and Politics](Politics/Kingdoms-and-Politics.md) and [Social Fabric](Social-Fabric.md).

A person may culturally belong to one tradition while politically living under another government.

Do not assume law follows culture.

This creates situations where:

- minority communities retain customary practices;
- governments attempt standardization;
- guild or faith institutions bridge the gap;
- border towns develop mixed practice;
- people disagree about which authority is legitimate.

Exact minority-rights arrangements remain local.

---

# 17. Guild privilege is negotiated

Guilds can have meaningful legal autonomy without becoming states.

Possible recognized powers already consistent with current guild owners include:

- certification;
- apprenticeship discipline;
- professional standards;
- internal fines or sanctions;
- contract arbitration;
- market access.

The extent of those powers should depend on local political agreements.

A strong guild in one city may possess privileges that the same guild lacks elsewhere.

That helps preserve regional political variation.

---

# 18. Religious mediation is socially powerful even without coercive jurisdiction

A faith can resolve a dispute because people trust it.

That is different from having state coercive power.

Religious mediation may be especially important in:

- family conflict;
- community reconciliation;
- burial;
- moral testimony;
- charity;
- local peace-making.

Whether governments legally recognize such settlements remains local.

Current canon does **not** establish universal sanctuary immunity or church courts.

Those remain open.

---

# 19. Mobile circuits and jurisdiction

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

Mobile households repeatedly cross political, guild, toll, and customary jurisdictions.

That creates recurring legal questions about:

- grazing;
- stopping places;
- market access;
- temporary work;
- property left with settled kin;
- taxes and tolls;
- contracts made in one place and performed in another;
- births, marriages, deaths, and inheritance across jurisdictions;
- accusations raised after a household has moved on.

Current canon does **not** establish one trans-regional nomad law or universal stopping right.

Instead, access should usually come from the same mechanisms already used elsewhere:

- custom;
- contract;
- lease;
- hospitality;
- kinship;
- guild privilege;
- religious institutions;
- local toleration;
- political recognition.

The legal status of the same household may therefore change from stop to stop.

This is a feature of the setting's layered jurisdiction, not an inconsistency.

# 20. Criminal networks exploit jurisdictional gaps

See [Crime and the Underworld](Politics/Crime-and-Underworld.md).

Criminal organizations benefit when:

- one authority cannot pursue across a border;
- documents differ between jurisdictions;
- Port neutrality limits foreign enforcement;
- officials disagree about ownership;
- Underpass routes cross claims;
- guild sanctions do not bind outsiders.

They may also provide their own:

- arbitration;
- debt enforcement;
- protection;
- transport;
- forged documentation.

That does not make criminal judgments legitimate.

It makes them enforceable inside certain networks.

---

# 21. Port is a legal switching point

Port is structurally important because it lets people from different systems interact under a relatively predictable neutral framework.

Its legal advantage comes from:

- repeated use;
- commercial trust;
- neutral institutions;
- multilingual administration;
- recognized measures;
- records;
- enforceable local judgments;
- the cost to outside powers of undermining its neutrality.

Port therefore behaves like a **legal switching point** in the same way it behaves as a trade and information hub.

A Port ruling may matter elsewhere because merchants, lenders, insurers, guilds, and governments want Port transactions to remain trustworthy.

That is network enforcement, not world sovereignty.

---

# 22. High Roads is an arbitration environment

The High Roads has deep historical reasons to value negotiated obligation.

Its pre-Convergence history included:

- pass lords;
- caravan families;
- toll towns;
- merchant confederations;
- overlapping route control.

Modern High Roads culture already emphasizes:

- obligation;
- evidence;
- quotation;
- weights;
- dates;
- routes;
- exceptions.

That makes specialized arbitration a natural continuation of existing history rather than a newly invented institution.

It should still contain:

- corrupt arbiters;
- competing authorities;
- local exceptions;
- people who reject decisions.

---

# 23. The Underpass is a jurisdictional stress test

The Underpass demonstrates why political maps are incomplete.

A single journey may pass through:

- a surface-claimed entrance;
- an independent enclave;
- a mining fraternity's corridor;
- a criminal-controlled junction;
- an unclaimed stretch;
- another polity's exit.

The question:

> Which law applies?

may have no single answer.

People may rely instead on:

- guide agreements;
- local toll rules;
- reputation;
- escort;
- negotiated safe passage;
- force.

That uncertainty is part of the Underpass's political value.

---

# 24. Appeals are not universal

Do not assume every judgment can be appealed up a neat hierarchy.

Depending on the polity or institution, a dissatisfied party may:

- appeal internally;
- petition a ruler;
- seek another arbitration;
- invoke a treaty;
- pressure through a guild;
- renegotiate;
- ignore the ruling and accept consequences;
- escalate politically.

Exact appeal systems belong to individual polities and institutions.

---

# 25. Forum shopping should exist

Where several authorities overlap, people will choose the forum that benefits them.

A merchant may prefer:

- Port arbitration;
- a guild hearing;
- a local court;
- a High Roads forum;

depending on:

- law;
- cost;
- speed;
- evidence;
- likely enforcement;
- political sympathy.

Institutions will resent this.

That creates ordinary legal politics without inventing new grand conflict.

---

# 26. Jurisdiction produces class differences

Access to law is not equal.

A wealthy merchant may:

- hire interpreters;
- travel to Port;
- preserve records;
- use guild lawyers or equivalent specialists;
- delay proceedings;
- pressure creditors.

A poor worker may depend on:

- a local guild branch;
- religious mediation;
- neighborhood testimony;
- a patron;
- local custom.

This means the same legal system can feel very different by class.

---

# 27. Jurisdiction produces cultural bleed

Legal practice also travels.

A successful contract form, arbitration custom, or evidence practice can spread through:

- merchants;
- guilds;
- Port;
- High Roads;
- migration.

But adoption need not be uniform.

A local community can borrow one useful legal practice while rejecting the institution that originated it.

This fits the wider world rule that cultural exchange is selective and generative.

---

# 28. The Council and law

See [The Economic Council](Politics/Economic-Council.md).

The Council does not need its own universal court system to exercise power.

It can influence legal outcomes through existing structures:

- contracts;
- credit;
- guild relationships;
- political clients;
- information;
- arbitration choices;
- funding litigation or negotiation;
- making settlement more attractive than continued dispute.

The Council is strongest when it can use legitimate institutions rather than replace them.

Do not invent a secret Council judiciary unless a future world problem requires one.

---

# 29. The Villain and jurisdiction

The Villain can exploit jurisdiction without controlling it.

Useful pressure points include:

- contradictory maps;
- disputed records;
- overlapping authority;
- forged documents;
- delayed messages;
- border incidents;
- conflicting guild and political obligations;
- forum shopping;
- a ruling that one side refuses to recognize.

The strongest manipulation makes each actor's response locally reasonable.

---

# 30. Worked example — damaged cross-border cargo

**Mechanism example, not a canon event.**

A Low Rivers merchant sends grain over the High Roads to a North Coast buyer.

The cargo is damaged after a bridge failure.

Possible claims:

- the merchant blames the carrier;
- the carrier blames the bridge authority;
- the bridge authority says unusual weather caused the failure;
- the buyer refuses payment;
- the lender says the debt remains due;
- the guild points to contract terms;
- the political authority wants the road reopened immediately.

Possible forums:

- merchant/guild arbitration;
- a High Roads route forum;
- a local court near the failure;
- Port, if the financing contract specified Port arbitration.

No forum automatically owns the entire dispute.

Resolution may require several linked settlements.

That is the kind of legal complexity the world should produce.

---

# 31. Worked example — moving river border

**Mechanism example, not a canon event.**

A Convergence-recognized boundary follows a river.

Over decades, the river shifts.

Now:

- farms lie on the opposite bank;
- one polity taxes them;
- another cites the old boundary;
- irrigation rights follow the current watercourse;
- old maps disagree;
- families have intermarried across the boundary.

Possible evidence:

- old treaty language;
- maps;
- stone markers;
- tax records;
- oral testimony;
- irrigation records;
- physical traces of the old channel.

The dispute cannot be solved by saying:

> the border is the river.

The whole problem is what that phrase means after history changes the landscape.

# 32. What is established versus open

## Established structurally

- authority is layered rather than singular;
- political systems vary across regions;
- the Convergence created cross-border rules and arbitration without abolishing local autonomy;
- Port has neutral commercial courts/arbitration and contract enforcement;
- High Roads has strong arbitration/route-law traditions;
- guilds regulate professions and contracts;
- religions can mediate and maintain records;
- criminal networks exploit jurisdictional gaps;
- Underpass authority is fragmented;
- enforcement depends on institutions and networks, not abstract legal correctness alone.

## Derived constraints

- cross-border judgments require some form of recognition or reciprocal interest to matter;
- overlapping jurisdictions create forum shopping;
- multilingual trade requires interpretation of contracts/evidence;
- moving borders and mixed populations create recurring legal ambiguity;
- wealthy actors generally have more access to distant or specialized forums.

## Open

- exact Convergence arbitration institutions;
- whether there are permanent treaty courts;
- exact appeal structures;
- Port citizenship;
- extradition;
- sanctuary;
- marriage recognition across borders;
- inheritance across jurisdictions;
- precise maritime law;
- standard notarial/seal practices;
- exact criminal procedure;
- exact guild legal privilege by polity;
- whether specific faiths have formally recognized courts;
- how Underpass judgments are recognized outside enclaves.

# 33. Standard jurisdiction test

For any dispute, ask:

1. Where did it happen?
2. Who are the parties?
3. What memberships or contracts bind them?
4. What kind of dispute is it?
5. Which institutions can plausibly claim authority?
6. Which forum do the parties prefer?
7. What evidence exists?
8. In what language?
9. Who can enforce the result?
10. Will another jurisdiction recognize it?
11. Who can afford to keep fighting?
12. What happens if one party simply refuses?

If these questions produce only one obvious authority every time, the world is probably too centralized.

---

# 34. Design rule

When adding legal detail:

```text
existing authority
→ existing obligation
→ overlapping claim
→ forum
→ evidence
→ enforcement
→ recognition
→ consequence
```

Do not invent a new court merely because a dispute needs somewhere to go.

First ask whether an existing:

- local authority;
- guild;
- religious mediator;
- route institution;
- Port court;
- treaty forum;

already has a reason to hear it.

The goal is a world where law grows out of the institutions already doing the work.
