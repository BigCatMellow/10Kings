# Money, Credit, Exchange, and Measures

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level. Exact currencies, denominations, minting systems, exchange rates, units, interest norms, tax forms, and financial instruments remain open.**

This page owns the world-level model for:

- physical money;
- credit and debt;
- account settlement;
- currency exchange;
- weights and measures;
- pricing;
- collateral;
- large commercial finance;
- everyday informal credit.

It does **not** establish one universal currency or one world banking system.

# 1. Core rule

The world should not be treated as if every transaction uses the same coin.

Exchange can occur through several overlapping systems:

- coin or other physical money;
- ledger/account balances;
- credit;
- debt;
- goods in payment;
- labor obligations;
- warehouse or cargo claims;
- locally recognized tokens or instruments where established.

The important distinction is:

> **money is one way of settling obligation; it is not the only way value moves.**

Long-distance commerce may depend more on trusted credit and account relationships than on physically moving large quantities of coin.

---

# 2. Physical money exists

Legacy material repeatedly assumes:

- coins;
- counterfeit coins;
- gold/copper as ordinary figurative references;
- currency exchange.

That is enough to support **physical money** as part of the world.

It is **not** enough to establish:

- one currency;
- one metal standard;
- one denomination system;
- one mint;
- one fixed coin-to-coin ratio.

Those remain open.

---

# 3. Do not assume one world currency

The political world is fragmented.

Therefore several possibilities are structurally plausible:

- polity-issued coinage;
- city coinage;
- widely accepted merchant coin;
- older coins still circulating;
- foreign coins accepted by weight;
- local tokens used only in limited settings.

The exact mix remains open.

A merchant in Port should plausibly encounter money issued elsewhere.

That creates a reason for:

- exchange;
- weighing;
- testing;
- discounting;
- trusted dealers.

---

# 4. Face value and accepted value may differ

A coin can say it is worth one thing while a merchant treats it as worth another.

Reasons include:

- unfamiliar issuer;
- debasement;
- clipping;
- wear;
- counterfeit risk;
- political instability;
- shortage of trusted currency;
- local legal rules.

Therefore:

> **money has issuer trust as well as material value.**

This is especially important across borders.

---

# 5. Coin by weight is structurally plausible

Where foreign coinage circulates, merchants may care about:

- weight;
- metal;
- fineness;
- wear;
- recognized mint.

That makes scales and reference weights important.

Exact metallurgical practice remains open.

Do not assume every ordinary market transaction involves assay.

The level of scrutiny should rise with:

- value;
- unfamiliar currency;
- risk;
- distance.

---

# 6. Counterfeit and bad coin should exist

Legacy sources explicitly mention counterfeit coin.

Possible fraud includes:

- base-metal copies;
- clipped coins;
- false plating;
- altered marks;
- underweight pieces;
- imitation foreign coin.

This creates ordinary work for:

- merchants;
- money changers;
- mints;
- courts;
- market inspectors.

Counterfeit money should be a financial crime, not a fantasy plot device requiring special magic.

---

# 7. Currency exchange is a real profession or service

Current and legacy worldbuilding already supports currency exchange in major commercial centers.

Port and High Roads are the strongest natural hubs.

Exchange may be offered by:

- merchant houses;
- banking houses;
- specialist money changers;
- guild-linked offices;
- warehouse/finance firms.

Exact institutional forms remain open.

The core mechanism is:

~~~text
different accepted monies
+ repeated cross-border trade
→ conversion service
~~~

---

# 8. Exchange rates move

Do not treat conversion as permanent arithmetic.

Rates can change because of:

- confidence in an issuer;
- war;
- trade imbalance;
- metal availability;
- seasonal demand;
- political crisis;
- counterfeit scares;
- local shortages.

That means currency information can itself become valuable market information.

---

# 9. Credit is structurally central

The current world repeatedly relies on:

- merchant credit;
- farm debt;
- infrastructure finance;
- emergency loans;
- warehouse claims;
- large contracts.

Credit allows exchange before final payment.

A basic chain can be:

~~~text
trust / collateral / guarantee
→ goods or money advanced
→ obligation recorded
→ future settlement
~~~

This does not require a modern banking system.

---

# 10. Long-distance trade cannot sensibly be all cash

Moving large amounts of physical money is:

- heavy;
- risky;
- expensive;
- vulnerable to theft;
- awkward across currencies.

Therefore major merchants need some way to settle obligations without carrying all value physically.

Structurally plausible methods include:

- accounts between merchant houses;
- letters or orders to pay;
- clearing through trusted partners;
- offsetting mutual debts;
- warehouse claims;
- credit guaranteed by known institutions.

Exact financial instruments remain open.

Do not import modern checks or bills of exchange by name until the world develops its own forms.

---

# 11. Account money can exist without a universal bank

Two merchant houses can keep reciprocal accounts.

A Port lender can settle against a High Roads partner.

A guild branch can record dues or obligations.

This allows:

> **value to move through records even when coin does not physically move the same distance.**

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

---

# 12. Clearing is a natural consequence of repeated trade

If merchants repeatedly owe one another, they can cancel opposite obligations.

Example:

~~~text
House A owes House B 100
House B owes House A 70
→ only 30 must actually be settled
~~~

This reduces physical transfer.

It also increases dependence on:

- trusted records;
- agreed dates;
- recognized values;
- dispute resolution.

---

# 13. Credit depends on reputation

A borrower can receive better terms because of:

- known family;
- guild membership;
- past repayment;
- property;
- reliable trade;
- guarantor;
- political status;
- institutional backing.

Poor reputation can mean:

- higher cost;
- smaller advances;
- more collateral;
- refusal.

Reputation is therefore an economic asset.

---

# 14. Poor people use credit too

Do not reserve debt for merchant houses.

Ordinary credit can include:

- shop tabs;
- rent arrears;
- wage advances;
- seed loans;
- food advanced until harvest;
- tools bought over time;
- family loans;
- employer housing debt.

Informal credit may be socially enforced rather than documented formally.

This makes debt part of everyday life.

---

# 15. Credit can be communal

A loan may be secured by more than one individual.

Possible guarantors:

- kin;
- guild;
- village cooperative;
- merchant association;
- religious institution;
- patron.

That can spread risk.

It can also spread consequences when one person defaults.

---

# 16. Collateral varies with the economy

Possible collateral can include:

- land;
- livestock;
- goods;
- cargo;
- warehouse stock;
- tools;
- future harvest;
- business share;
- contract revenue.

Exact legality varies by polity.

Do not introduce debt slavery or human collateral without deliberate separate worldbuilding.

---

# 17. Debt is jurisdictional

See [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

A debt raises questions:

- which law governs;
- what counts as default;
- what collateral can be seized;
- which court hears the dispute;
- whether another jurisdiction recognizes the judgment;
- whether a seasonal delay excuses performance.

Cross-border debt therefore depends heavily on trusted forums.

---

# 18. Debt is also temporal

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md).

Credit needs:

- maturity;
- payment schedule;
- seasonal trigger;
- renewal;
- renegotiation.

A harvest loan and a shipping loan may use different schedules.

Legacy "Ledger Closing" remains useful as a **fiscal-cycle mechanism**, not a fixed universal festival.

---

# 19. Default does not have one universal consequence

Possible consequences include:

- extension;
- renegotiation;
- higher interest/cost;
- collateral seizure;
- loss of guild standing;
- loss of market access;
- reputational damage;
- court judgment;
- bankruptcy-like local procedure where established.

Exact systems remain local.

Do not assume every unpaid debt leads to prison.

---

# 20. Interest norms remain open

Current canon supports lending.

It does not establish:

- standard interest rates;
- universal compound interest;
- legal maximums;
- religious prohibition;
- one accepted word for interest.

Different faiths, polities, guilds, and communities may regulate lending differently.

Develop those rules only when specific institutions require them.

---

# 21. Banking is a function before it is an institution

The world supports banking-like activities:

- deposits;
- lending;
- exchange;
- account settlement;
- merchant finance;
- storage of valuables.

These functions may be performed by:

- merchant houses;
- specialist banks;
- guild institutions;
- wealthy families;
- religious institutions where appropriate.

Do not assume one standardized "bank" form across the world.

---

# 22. High Roads is an exchange and credit hub

See [The High Roads](Regions/Highridge-Plateau.md).

Its structural advantages include:

- caravan convergence;
- arbitration;
- multilingual clerks;
- repeated merchant traffic;
- toll systems;
- cross-border contracts.

That naturally supports:

- money changing;
- lending;
- account settlement;
- conversion of measures;
- credit information.

High Roads is not "the banking region."

Its finance role grows from route traffic.

---

# 23. Port is an exchange and settlement hub

See [Port](Places/Port.md).

Port concentrates:

- foreign merchants;
- currencies;
- warehouses;
- lenders;
- shipping;
- arbitration;
- records.

Therefore Port is a natural place for:

- currency exchange;
- settlement of accounts;
- cargo finance;
- warehouse-backed claims;
- insurance-like contracts.

Its neutrality increases the value of financial institutions trusted by outsiders.

Exact banks and exchange houses remain open.

---

# 24. Port and High Roads need not use the same conventions

Both are exchange hubs for different networks.

Port may develop conventions around:

- maritime cargo;
- foreign coin;
- warehouse claims;
- shipping finance.

High Roads may develop conventions around:

- caravan credit;
- tolls;
- pack-animal loads;
- route risk;
- inland settlement.

Standards can converge without becoming identical.

---

# 25. Weights and measures are political infrastructure

Trade requires agreement about quantities.

Important measures may include:

- weight;
- volume;
- length;
- area;
- cargo capacity;
- grain measures;
- cloth length;
- metal weight.

Local systems can vary.

This creates conversion work.

---

# 26. Port supports standardized local measures

Current Port canon already supports standardized weights and measures.

This should mean:

> **transactions inside Port can refer to recognized Port standards.**

It does not mean Port's system is universal.

Foreign merchants may convert their own measures into Port forms for trade and court purposes.

---

# 27. High Roads needs reliable commercial reference measures

High Roads arbitration and contracts depend on:

- weights;
- dates;
- routes;
- obligations.

Therefore major High Roads markets likely maintain recognized reference measures.

Exact units remain open.

These may be local standards that merchants convert against, not continent-wide units.

---

# 28. Reference standards must exist physically or institutionally

A standard measure can be maintained through:

- official weights;
- measuring rods;
- standard containers;
- certified scales;
- guild reference pieces;
- market inspectors.

Exact practice remains open.

The principle is:

> **measurement trust must attach to something people can check.**

---

# 29. False measures are ordinary fraud

A seller can cheat through:

- light weights;
- false-bottom containers;
- stretched measures;
- inaccurate scales;
- misleading quality grades.

Markets therefore have incentives for:

- inspection;
- guild enforcement;
- public standards;
- reputational sanctions.

Measurement fraud should be part of ordinary commercial life.

---

# 30. Quality can matter as much as quantity

A unit of:

- grain;
- metal;
- oil;
- cloth;
- timber;

is not identical in value across quality grades.

Contracts may therefore specify:

- grade;
- origin;
- moisture;
- purity;
- condition;
- inspection standard.

Exact quality systems remain industry-specific.

---

# 31. Commodity payment remains plausible

Not every transaction requires coin.

People may pay with:

- grain;
- livestock;
- fuel;
- cloth;
- labor;
- services;
- locally useful goods.

This is especially plausible where:

- coin is scarce;
- markets are local;
- obligations are customary.

But do not describe the whole economy as barter.

Credit, money, and account systems clearly exist.

---

# 32. Taxes need not all be paid in coin

A polity may collect some obligations as:

- coin;
- grain;
- labor;
- toll;
- livestock;
- material.

Exact taxation belongs to individual polities.

The existence of money does not require complete monetization.

---

# 33. Wages can also be mixed

Workers may receive:

- coin;
- food;
- lodging;
- guild benefit;
- share of catch/cargo;
- seasonal bonus;
- credit at a company or household store where locally established.

Mixed payment can create dependency.

Do not assume modern cash wages everywhere.

---

# 34. Price is local information

There is no single "price of grain."

Price depends on:

- location;
- season;
- quality;
- transport cost;
- risk;
- supply;
- storage;
- currency accepted;
- credit terms.

Therefore numeric prices should not be canonized casually.

A price only makes sense with context.

---

# 35. Credit price and cash price can differ

A merchant may offer:

- lower price for immediate trusted payment;
- higher price on credit;
- discount for bulk;
- penalty for risky currency.

This creates social information inside prices.

A cheap good may still be expensive to a buyer with weak credit.

---

# 36. Insurance-like arrangements remain structurally supported

Current owners already use insurance-like concepts for shipping and risk.

The exact form is open.

Possible mechanism:

~~~text
many contributors
→ pooled or distributed risk
→ compensation after verified loss
~~~

Or risk may be shared through merchant partnerships and guarantees instead of a separate insurer.

Do not assume modern insurance companies.

---

# 37. Risk changes finance before goods move

See [Couriers, Posts, and Message Networks](Couriers-Posts-and-Message-Networks.md). Commercial prices and credit can react to carried reports before goods physically reroute; urgent dispatch is therefore an economic service as well as an information service.

A rumor of:

- piracy;
- war;
- pass closure;
- crop disease;

can cause lenders to:

- raise cost;
- refuse finance;
- demand more collateral;
- shorten terms.

That can reduce trade before the physical route actually fails.

Finance is therefore part of the information feedback loop.

# 38. Currency and measures are translation problems too

See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

Financial communication needs standardized or carefully translated terms for:

- numbers;
- denominations;
- weight;
- quality;
- interest;
- debt;
- guarantee;
- due date.

A mistranslated number or measure can create a real legal dispute.

---

# 39. Financial records are evidence

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Finance depends on:

- ledgers;
- receipts;
- contracts;
- account statements;
- cargo records;
- collateral records.

Their authority depends on:

- who kept them;
- authentication;
- comparison with other records;
- witnesses.

A ledger is evidence, not infallible truth.

# 40. Exchange houses accumulate information

Someone exchanging currencies repeatedly learns:

- which money is trusted;
- who is defaulting;
- where coin is scarce;
- which routes are risky;
- which governments are unstable.

That information itself has value.

Money changing can therefore overlap with:

- merchant intelligence;
- lending;
- political influence.

# 41. The Council is not a central bank

See [Economic Council](Politics/Economic-Council.md).

The Council's Finance and Exchange portfolio may coordinate:

- credit;
- emergency financing;
- large loans;
- currency exchange relationships;
- investment;
- settlement of large obligations.

It does **not** currently:

- issue the world's currency;
- set universal interest rates;
- control every bank;
- dictate exchange rates by law;
- own all minting.

Its power comes from access to capital and networks, not monetary sovereignty.

---

# 42. The Council can still influence financial conditions

Council-linked houses can change:

- who receives large loans;
- which projects get funded;
- whether emergency credit is available;
- which merchant failures are rescued;
- how quickly capital moves between crises.

Those actions can influence market conditions without formal control of money.

That is more consistent with the current Council model.

---

# 43. Financial power creates class differences

A wealthy actor may have:

- several lenders;
- collateral;
- records;
- access to Port;
- access to High Roads exchange;
- legal help.

A poor actor may depend on:

- employer credit;
- kin;
- shop tabs;
- local lender;
- guild welfare;
- religious charity.

The same nominal interest or price can therefore have very different consequences.

---

# 44. Mobile households use finance differently

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

A mobile household may prefer:

- portable coin;
- trusted merchant credit;
- kin-held deposits;
- goods that retain value;
- accounts with repeated market partners.

Its financial relationships may follow the circuit.

Exact systems remain group-specific.

---

# 45. Coin shortage does not mean economic collapse

If trusted coin becomes scarce, people may shift toward:

- credit;
- local accounts;
- commodity payment;
- foreign coin;
- deferred settlement.

That can create friction without stopping exchange entirely.

Do not make coin supply the only measure of economic activity.

---

# 46. The Convergence may standardize conversion rather than currency

The Convergence increased cross-border commerce.

That creates pressure for:

- recognized reference measures;
- reliable currency conversion;
- standardized contract language;
- accepted settlement forums.

Current sources do **not** establish a single Convergence currency.

A better current model is:

> **interoperability before monetary unification.**

Exact treaty provisions remain open.

---

# 47. Writing-the-sequel consequence: financial infrastructure becomes ordinary

If cross-border trade has been stable for generations, people should inherit institutions whose origins they may barely notice.

Possible accumulated results:

- money changers near major markets;
- standard scales;
- price boards or regular quoted rates where literacy permits;
- clerks trained in conversion;
- customary credit terms;
- established merchant correspondent relationships;
- inherited arguments about dishonest lenders;
- old fiscal dates whose origin is forgotten.

These are consequences to derive locally, not a command that every place has them.

---

# 48. A financial crisis should propagate through relationships

Useful chain:

~~~text
merchant failure
→ lender loss
→ credit tightens
→ buyers cancel contracts
→ producer inventory accumulates
→ wages fall
→ debt defaults rise
→ distressed property changes hands
→ political anger grows
~~~

The crisis spreads through obligations, not magic.

# 52. Design rule

When adding a financial detail, ask:

1. What obligation is being settled?
2. Why is this payment form trusted?
3. Is payment physical or recorded?
4. Which currency or unit is used?
5. Who converts it if needed?
6. Which measure determines quantity?
7. Who checks the measure?
8. What happens if payment is delayed?
9. What collateral or guarantee exists?
10. Which court or network enforces the debt?
11. What record proves it?
12. How can the system be cheated?
13. Who has easier access because of class or connections?

Do not begin with:

> What should the gold coin be called?

Begin with:

> **How do these two people actually settle what one owes the other?**
