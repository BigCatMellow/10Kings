# Stone Hills

## Status

**Working canon.** Stone Hills is the best-known mining and metalworking region, but it is not a nation of miners. It contains farms, villages, upland herders, river towns, merchants, religious communities, poor districts, old estates, guild centers, and border populations.

## Cultural reach

Stone Hills is a **cultural core and historical concentration**, not an exclusive homeland. Western-derived craft families, mining communities, guild traditions and dialects can exist far outside the western uplands, especially around mines, workshops and trade cities. Likewise, the geographic Stone Hills contains communities whose strongest cultural ties are Low Rivers, High Roads, Longwood, Port, religious, guild or locally mixed.

## Geography

Stone Hills occupies much of the western upland country. It includes mineral-rich hills, valleys cut by streams, wooded slopes, pasture, workable farmland, and increasingly dense settlements around older mining and metalworking centers.

Its famous ore deposits explain why mining and metalcraft became unusually important, not why nothing else exists there.

The eastern and southeastern edges grade toward Low Rivers, High Roads, and parts of Longwood through foothills, old roads, and forest margins.

## Historical identity

Before The Convergence, Stone Hills was not one unified state. Smith-chiefs, mine holders, fortified towns, valley rulers, merchant families, and warbands fought over ore seams, roads, charcoal country, rivers, and grain access.

Control shifted repeatedly:
- a mine could be taken while the valley below remained independent;
- towns changed allegiance through marriage or debt;
- Low Rivers rulers periodically held western lowlands;
- Stone Hills powers periodically pushed into food-producing country;
- forest margins changed hands repeatedly.

The modern region remembers this as both achievement and warning. Its political culture values reliable agreements partly because its own history demonstrates how ruinous resource warfare can become.

## Cultural influences

Useful inspiration pools:
- Scottish, Welsh, and Appalachian mining communities;
- Andean highland mining settlements;
- Ethiopian and Yemeni upland stone architecture;
- Japanese and Chinese craft-lineage traditions;
- Balkan and Central European mountain towns.

The result should not resemble any one of these directly.

## Cultural character

Stone Hills culture places unusually high value on:
- demonstrated skill;
- accountability for work;
- reputation over generations;
- apprenticeship;
- mutual aid in dangerous trades;
- practical literacy such as contracts, weights, measures, and records.

There is also a long tension between craft pride and concentrated ownership. Mine owners, guild masters, workers, small smiths, merchants, and rural communities do not necessarily want the same future.

## Community differentiation — industrial districts

Focused community work now supports **major industrial mine / civic districts** as one Stone Hills community type.

See:

- [Community Analogue Research — Wave 2](../Reference/Community-Analogue-Research-Wave-2.md);
- [Community Profiles — P1 Wave 1](../Reference/Community-Profiles-P1-Wave-1.md);
- [Community Hold Resolutions — V1](../Reference/Community-Hold-Resolutions-V1.md).

Such districts can plausibly contain:

- older mine / craft households;
- internal Stone Hills migrants from smaller valleys / workings;
- cross-regional skilled / casual workers;
- worker-built mutual-aid / education / recreation institutions;
- guild halls;
- boarding districts;
- owner / administrative neighborhoods.

This does **not** make every Stone Hills mining town culturally identical.

The important structural result is that dangerous industrial work can create:

- civic institutions;
- migration layers;
- neighborhood politics;
- occupational folklore

rather than only workplace conflict.

No single faith is assigned to industrial districts. Religious charities, craft institutions, worker organizations and guild services can overlap.

## Language

See [Language and Thought](../Culture/Language-and-Thought.md).

Stone Hills languages may habitually distinguish between something being:
- ordered;
- attempted;
- completed;
- inspected;
- guaranteed.

This encourages close attention to responsibility and proof without making every Stone Hills person blunt or emotionless.

## Settlement and architecture

### Cities

Older urban cores may contain dark local stone, narrow lanes, fortified compounds, cisterns, market courts, warehouses, guild halls, and neighborhoods built in layers around mines or river crossings.

Industrial districts are dirty because furnaces, charcoal, animals, and bulk transport are dirty — not because Stone Hills has modern factories.

Large workshops rely on:
- water wheels where possible;
- bellows;
- cranes and pulleys;
- hand and animal power;
- kilns;
- charcoal and coal where technically suitable.

### Towns

Small towns usually exist for multiple reasons: a mine plus farming, a ford plus repair shops, a market plus nearby forest work.

Architecture often combines:
- local stone bases;
- timber or mud-plaster upper walls;
- shared yards;
- workshops attached to homes;
- storage cellars;
- public wells.

### Rural homes

A miner's family may also keep goats and a garden. A smallholder may repair tools for neighbors. A smith may farm part of the year.

Homes should therefore show mixed economies rather than single occupations.

## Food

Stone Hills cuisine is shaped by upland grains, legumes, roots, dairy, preserved meat, mushrooms, orchard goods from lower valleys, and imported Low Rivers staples.

Typical distinctions:
- workers favor portable, filling food;
- towns have breads, stews, roasted roots, sausages, cheeses, pickles, and fried market foods;
- elites have greater access to imported spices, fruit, wine, and coastal foods;
- border food blends strongly with Low Rivers and Longwood.

Dark ales and grain spirits are plausible, but no single beverage defines the region.

## Politics

Modern Stone Hills rulers must manage:
- mine ownership;
- labor;
- guild privileges;
- food imports;
- forest/fuel rights;
- border roads;
- old land claims;
- Council-linked credit.

See:

- [Stone Hills Functional Governance Test](../Reference/Stone-Hills-Functional-Governance-Test.md)
- [Stone Hills Polity Vertical Slice — V1](../Reference/Stone-Hills-Polity-Vertical-Slice-V1.md) for the current provisional SH-A / SH-M concrete-state test.

The strongest current structural conclusion is that Stone Hills is **more territorially consolidated than its pre-Convergence past without becoming economically centralized**.

Larger rulers can plausibly command soldiers, tax, regulate major roads, intervene in strategic metal flows, and conduct diplomacy. They still depend on mine owners, skilled workers, guilds, towns, lenders, food, fuel, and transport.

Mine ownership, civic authority, guild authority, and sovereign authority should therefore remain distinct.

A ruler can command soldiers but cannot simply order the economy to function.

## Current pressures

- labor unrest in mining and metalworking;
- covert high-quality metal shipments;
- pressure to expand access to Tunnels routes;
- fears of weapon stockpiling;
- Council efforts to prevent Stone Hills from converting economic strength into unchecked military power;
- Villain efforts to turn guild grievances and elite rivalries into strategic disruption.

## Story function

Stone Hills lets the story examine the difference between **productive capacity** and **who controls it**.

It should never be presented simply as "the weapon region." It is a society in which work, ownership, skill, debt, and political power have become inseparable.
