# Highridge Plateau

## Status

**Working canon.**

Highridge is a highland crossroads, not simply "the merchant region." Its importance comes from geography: routes that avoid or cross parts of The Spine naturally converge here.

## Geography

The plateau contains:
- exposed uplands;
- sheltered valleys;
- steep passes;
- seasonal streams;
- grazing country;
- caravan corridors;
- defensible heights;
- towns clustered around water and route junctions.

The environment favors people who understand movement, timing, weather, animals, and negotiation.

## Historical identity

Pre-Convergence Highridge was highly fragmented.

Power belonged to:
- pass lords;
- caravan families;
- toll towns;
- herding groups;
- fortress communities (one became the town of [Seven Hill](../Places/Seven-Hill.md));
- merchant confederations.

Borders followed control of roads more often than neat territory.

A ruler could control a pass but not the valley below it. A merchant league could effectively govern a route crossing several political jurisdictions.

The historical shift from toll warfare toward negotiated passage is one of the foundations of modern Highridge identity.

## Cultural inspirations

Reference pools include:
- Tibetan and Himalayan plateau communities;
- Andean highlands;
- Central Asian caravan societies;
- Persian/Silk Road caravanserai networks;
- Caucasus trade communities;
- mountain markets across Asia and South America.

## Language

Highridge is probably the most multilingual settled region. Its plateau speech is the most mixed member of the Heartland family ([Language and Thought](../Culture/Language-and-Thought.md#language-families)).

Common trade forms emphasize:
- condition;
- obligation;
- evidence;
- quotation;
- weights;
- dates;
- routes;
- exceptions.

People accustomed to bargaining across languages become attentive to what a promise actually commits someone to.

## Settlement and architecture

### Cities

Highridge cities are layered around:
- major markets;
- caravan yards;
- stables;
- cisterns;
- temples;
- public meeting halls;
- courts/arbitration halls.

Terraces and retaining walls are common where terrain requires them.

Dense neighborhoods should show foreign influence because merchants have settled here for generations.

### Towns

Pass towns often have:
- one or two fortified gates;
- inns;
- animal yards;
- repair shops;
- warehouses;
- shrines;
- public scales;
- toll or customs houses.

### Rural homes

Herders and small upland farmers use stone, earth, timber, woven material, and animal products according to local availability.

## Food

Highridge cuisine is unusually mixed because caravans bring ingredients.

Local staples may include:
- barley or other hardy grains;
- dairy;
- goat/sheep/yak-like livestock;
- legumes;
- preserved meat;
- roots;
- tea/herbal infusions.

Markets introduce dried fruit, spices, oils, noodles, rice, wine, fish, and sweets from elsewhere.

There should be strong differences between local household food and cosmopolitan market food.

## Politics

Highridge political power is distributed among:
- route authorities;
- old families;
- merchant houses;
- caravan guilds;
- urban councils;
- religious institutions;
- herding communities.

The region's reputation for reasoned debate is partly ideal and partly institutional necessity.

## Current pressures

- caravan attacks;
- manipulated route closures;
- accusations of corrupt militia or toll officials;
- intellectual disputes tied to actual commercial patrons;
- contested infrastructure projects;
- Council interference with route costs;
- Villain efforts to redirect flows without appearing to control them.

## Story function

Highridge turns logistics into politics.

Whoever controls movement does not need to own everything that moves.
