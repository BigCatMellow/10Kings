# North Coast

## Status

**Working canon.**

North Coast is colder than most of the known world and unusually maritime, but its people are not uniformly fishers or stoic seafarers. The region includes coastal towns, sheltered inland valleys, grazing country, forests in milder zones, craft centers, small farms, shipping ports, islands, and old clan territories.

## Cultural reach

North Coast is a **cultural core and historical concentration**, not the only place where North Coast people live. Maritime families, harbor communities and clan-descended groups can persist in distant ports and coastal settlements. The geographic North Coast also contains inland, migrant and mixed communities that do not share one cultural identity.

## Geography

North Coast occupies much of the Northern Continent and associated coasts and islands.

Important environmental facts:
- cold seas;
- strong seasonal storms;
- shorter growing seasons;
- rocky coasts;
- sheltered coves;
- some partially freezing waters;
- inland routes toward High Roads.

Its settlements cluster where geography offers a reason to stay: a good harbor, a protected bay, a navigable river, a reliable fishery, pasture, timber, or a route south.

## Historical identity

North Coast's pre-Convergence politics were fragmented among:
- coastal clans;
- fishing confederacies;
- harbor towns;
- island rulers;
- raiding bands;
- inland chiefs.

Control of land mattered, but control of **water and seasonal access** often mattered more.

A clan might lose a harbor yet retain boats, kin alliances, and offshore rights. A political border on shore did not necessarily define who fished a bank or used a winter anchorage.

This history explains why modern North Coast politics remain sensitive to:
- access rights;
- clan obligation;
- shared rescue;
- maritime law;
- outsiders buying control of harbors.

## Cultural inspirations

Research pools include:
- Scottish and Irish island communities;
- Icelandic, Faroese, Scandinavian, and North Atlantic maritime cultures;
- Ainu, Japanese, Korean, Aleut, Inuit, and other northern/coastal societies;
- fishing societies built around seasonal risk, preservation, and cooperative labor.

Avoid generic "Viking culture."

## Language

North Coast languages may include:
- inclusive/exclusive "we";
- strong evidential markers;
- directional vocabulary tied to coast, wind, and slope;
- future forms that emphasize conditions.

This can make conversations sensitive to who is included, who witnessed something, and how certain a plan really is.

## Settlement and architecture

### Cities

Large ports contain:
- stone breakwaters;
- dense dock districts;
- timber warehouses;
- fish markets;
- smokehouses;
- boatyards;
- public halls;
- shrines and meeting houses;
- neighborhoods built above flood or storm lines.

Buildings need not all have the same roof shape. Older districts, wealthier merchant districts, island-influenced neighborhoods, and migrant quarters should differ.

### Towns

Fishing towns often combine boat access with inland food production.

A typical town may contain:
- small docks;
- communal work yards;
- drying racks;
- smokehouses;
- gardens;
- storage pits;
- animal shelters;
- steep-roofed or earth-insulated homes depending on local climate.

### Rural homes

Remote families build for insulation, repairability, and available material. Driftwood, stone, turf, timber, clay, hide, reeds, and imported iron may all appear.

## Food

North Coast food should show both scarcity management and trade.

Common techniques:
- drying;
- salting;
- smoking;
- fermentation;
- pickling;
- cold storage.

Foods may include:
- fish and shellfish;
- sea greens;
- hardy grains;
- roots;
- dairy where herding is possible;
- berries;
- preserved meats;
- imported Low Rivers flour and Old Cities oils or fruit in wealthier ports.

Alcohol can include grain ales, berry ferments, mead-like drinks, and imported spirits.

## Politics

North Coast's political tensions often revolve around:
- fishing rights;
- harbor control;
- convoy protection;
- privateering/piracy;
- clan autonomy;
- merchant investment;
- food dependence;
- Council influence over shipping and finance.

## Current pressures

- declining or shifting fish stocks;
- rising piracy;
- debate over convoy agreements with Port;
- accusations that some clans cooperate with raiders;
- Council attempts to stabilize shipping without allowing a single maritime bloc to dominate;
- Villain efforts to make defensive actions look like aggression.

## Story function

North Coast lets the story ask: **when survival requires cooperation, who gets to define the group?**

Its social strength can become political vulnerability when false information changes who people believe threatens that group.
