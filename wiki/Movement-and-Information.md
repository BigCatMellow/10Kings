# Movement and Information

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level.**

This page owns the world-level model for how people, messages, rumors, records, and intelligence move through the setting.

It does **not** establish exact travel times, literacy rates, a universal postal service, a single courier guild, or precise route schedules. Those remain open until map scale, language families, and local institutions are firmer.

# 1. Core rule

Information does not float across the map.

It rides **people, institutions, and routes**.

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md) for the scheduling layer behind ships, markets, pilgrimages, pass openings, seasonal labor, and mobile circuits. Repeated movement depends not only on a route but on knowing **when** that route or institution is expected to function.

The same physical road can carry very different information depending on who is traveling it:

- a sailor carries harbor news, prices, slang, and ship gossip;
- a merchant carries prices, credit conditions, demand, route risk, and contracts;
- a pilgrim carries religious argument, family news, hospitality knowledge, and stories from distant communities;
- a guild member carries technical practice, certification news, wage disputes, and professional reputation;
- an itinerant household carries route knowledge, songs, prices, marriages, local stories, and missing-person information;
- a state messenger carries orders and official reports;
- a smuggler carries information about enforcement gaps and illicit demand;
- a spy carries selected information to a selected recipient.

Therefore:

> **The carrier matters as much as the route.**

This extends the carrier-specific cultural-transmission model already supported by [Social Fabric](Social-Fabric.md) and the Phase 7 transmission tests.

# 2. Information speed is network latency

Until exact map scale and travel times are fixed, information speed should be modeled structurally rather than with invented day counts.

A message's total delay is approximately:

```text
departure delay
+ physical travel
+ transfer between carriers or hubs
+ copying / translation
+ verification / authentication
+ political or institutional friction
= effective propagation time
```

## Departure delay

A dedicated messenger can leave immediately.

A merchant report may wait for the next ship, caravan, market run, or agent.

A pilgrim story may wait until someone happens to travel the relevant road.

This means two messages traveling the same distance can arrive at very different times.

## Physical travel

Travel remains bounded by:

- roads;
- rivers;
- sea conditions;
- animal endurance;
- mountain passes;
- seasonal closure;
- weather;
- available boats;
- Underpass capacity;
- border restrictions.

## Transfer delay

Long-distance information often changes hands.

A message may move:

```text
village witness
→ market trader
→ caravan
→ High Roads broker
→ Port agent
→ ship
→ foreign merchant house
```

Every handoff creates delay and an opportunity for distortion.

## Verification delay

Reliable information is often slower than repeatable information.

A rumor can be repeated immediately.

A court, guild, merchant house, ruler, or archive may require:

- a second witness;
- a signed copy;
- a seal;
- a known messenger;
- corroborating prices;
- a ship manifest;
- a guild mark;
- a translated document;
- a formal hearing.

That creates a recurring setting principle:

> **Rumor can propagate faster than verification without physically traveling faster than people.**

## Political friction

Information may also be delayed by:

- censorship;
- strategic silence;
- bribery;
- confiscated letters;
- closed borders;
- guild secrecy;
- diplomatic protocol;
- fear of punishment;
- deliberate misinformation.

# 3. Relative propagation classes

These are **relative classes**, not fixed travel-time promises.

## A. Local immediate

Within a settlement, harbor, camp, market, or work district, oral information can move very quickly through face-to-face contact.

Typical carriers:

- neighbors;
- workers;
- market sellers;
- taverns and public houses;
- docks;
- guild halls;
- shrines;
- family networks.

Best for:

- accidents;
- arrests;
- fires;
- market shortages;
- scandal;
- local route trouble;
- public announcements.

Weakness:

high speed does not imply accuracy.

## B. Dedicated dispatch

Where a ruler, merchant house, guild, military force, or other institution pays someone specifically to carry a message, that message can move faster than ordinary social circulation because it does not wait for unrelated travel.

Likely uses:

- urgent orders;
- contract changes;
- military warning;
- high-value commercial intelligence;
- diplomatic correspondence.

Important constraint:

the setting does **not yet have a canon universal courier service or postal network**.

Dedicated messengers are structurally plausible and supported by legacy material, but their exact institutional form remains open.

## C. Commercial cadence

Merchants, ships, caravans, warehouse agents, and guild branches create repeated information movement along economically active routes.

This is likely the fastest **routine** long-distance network for:

- prices;
- shortages;
- credit conditions;
- route reliability;
- shipping losses;
- demand;
- large contracts;
- political events that affect trade.

Commercial information is fast where traffic is frequent and slow where trade is thin.

## D. Social-network cadence

Pilgrims, itinerant households, migrant laborers, performers, students, sailors, and voluntary movements carry information widely but according to their own circuits.

This can be slower than a dedicated dispatch while reaching communities formal institutions neglect.

Best for:

- family news;
- religious argument;
- reputations;
- stories;
- labor grievances;
- local warnings;
- social movements;
- missing-person information;
- cultural change.

## E. Seasonal / circuit movement

Some information follows seasonal movement rather than direct routes.

An itinerant household may know conditions across a wide territory but revisit particular places only at certain times.

This network can have:

- broad geographic memory;
- strong local trust;
- excellent route knowledge;
- poor immediacy for places off the current circuit.

## F. Archival circulation

Records, copied texts, treaties, maps, account books, scholarly collections, and religious archives usually spread more slowly than rumor.

Their strength is different:

- persistence;
- comparability;
- provenance;
- accumulated contradiction;
- recovery after living witnesses are gone.

Archives can therefore become politically important long after faster information has disappeared.

# 4. Major carrier networks

## Merchants and commercial agents

Strongest information:

- prices;
- supply;
- credit;
- contracts;
- route conditions;
- port delays;
- political risk.

Strengths:

- repeated routes;
- financial incentive to be early;
- agents in multiple cities;
- contact with transport, storage, and lenders.

Weaknesses:

- commercial secrecy;
- incentives to manipulate expectations;
- poor reach into places with little profitable trade.

## Sailors and harbor workers

Strongest information:

- ship arrivals and losses;
- storms;
- piracy;
- harbor restrictions;
- foreign prices;
- coastal politics;
- sailor rumor and slang.

North Coast maritime communities and Port make this an especially important trans-regional network.

Strengths:

- long geographic reach;
- repeated port-to-port contact;
- dense informal exchange at docks.

Weaknesses:

- weather;
- sailing schedules;
- information may leap between ports while bypassing inland areas.

## Caravan workers and High Roads networks

Strongest information:

- overland route conditions;
- tolls;
- pass closures;
- banditry;
- market demand;
- caravan security;
- political conditions along roads.

The High Roads function as a major overland switching point because many routes, languages, merchants, and arbitration traditions converge there.

Strengths:

- cross-language brokerage;
- repeated interior routes;
- practical route knowledge.

Weaknesses:

- season;
- animal and road capacity;
- route closures;
- information may be excellent along corridors and poor only a short distance away.

## Guild branches and professional networks

Strongest information:

- technical changes;
- standards;
- certification;
- apprenticeship;
- contracts;
- wages;
- professional reputation;
- supply problems.

Guilds can transmit information across political borders because professional membership overlaps with regional and political identity.

Exact guild dispatch systems remain open.

## Pilgrims and faith networks

Strongest information:

- events in distant communities;
- moral interpretation;
- religious disputes;
- hospitality conditions;
- family and community news;
- reports of suffering or local crisis.

The Infinite Compass is especially associated with pilgrimage, maps, hospitality, travelers, and road shrines. Other faiths maintain schools, archives, charities, mediation, and local communities that can also carry information.

Strengths:

- trans-regional trust;
- access to communities outside commercial centers;
- recurring lodging and hospitality infrastructure.

Weaknesses:

- information may be interpreted through religious frameworks;
- routes follow sacred/social destinations rather than commercial efficiency.

## Itinerant households and mobile peoples

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

Mobile peoples are now modeled primarily through **repeatable circuits**, not random wandering. A circuit may connect work, pasture, markets, kin, pilgrimage sites, winter quarters, fairs, or route junctions. Because those circuits repeat, they create durable relationships with settled communities and can function as long-lived social infrastructure without a central organization.

Strongest information:

- local route conditions;
- prices;
- marriages and family links;
- missing people;
- songs and stories;
- practical warnings;
- conditions in small communities.

Strengths:

- repeated local contact;
- difficult-route knowledge;
- social trust based on reputation;
- reach outside major institutions;
- inherited memory of routes, hosts, tolls, and recurring stops.

Weaknesses:

- seasonal cadence;
- fragmented coverage;
- route closure;
- access disputes;
- outsiders may distrust them even when their information is good.

Different circuits can intersect at markets, fairs, Port, High Roads caravan yards, and shrines, allowing information to relay from one mobile network into another.

## Migrants and refugees

Strongest information:

- conditions in places they left;
- family networks;
- labor opportunities;
- repression or violence;
- household practice;
- local political memory.

Migration can preserve information that official institutions suppress, but trauma, distance, and partial knowledge can also shape what survives.

## States and political authorities

Strongest information:

- official orders;
- taxation;
- law;
- military reports;
- diplomatic communication;
- administrative records.

A state can make information authoritative without making it fast.

Some governments may maintain excellent messenger systems; others may depend on local officials, merchants, guilds, or religious networks.

There is no canon assumption that governments are always the fastest network.

## Criminal and smuggling networks

Strongest information:

- enforcement patterns;
- border weaknesses;
- illicit demand;
- corrupt officials;
- hidden routes;
- forged documents;
- unofficial transport.

Strengths:

- bypass formal closures;
- operate across jurisdictional gaps;
- strong incentives to know enforcement conditions.

Weaknesses:

- compartmentation;
- deliberate deception;
- information may stay inside trusted circles.

## Spies and intelligence networks

Legacy sources strongly support spies, informants, guild contacts, religious observers, and Council data gathering.

Current canon supports the Council as a hidden coordination system that depends on information.

Intelligence should therefore be treated as:

- selective;
- targeted;
- often compartmented;
- expensive;
- vulnerable to interpretation error.

A spy network is not an all-seeing map.

## Scribes, scholars, notaries, and archives

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Current material supports practical literacy, commercial records, libraries, schools, archives, and record-centered religious institutions in parts of the world.

These networks are best at:

- durable records;
- legal proof;
- contracts;
- copying;
- comparison;
- long memory.

Exact literacy rates and universal document conventions remain open.

# 5. Information hubs

## Port — maritime and commercial aggregation

Port is likely one of the world's fastest places for **aggregation**, not necessarily the first place to learn every event.

It concentrates:

- ships;
- warehouses;
- merchants;
- lenders;
- insurers and insurance-like arrangements;
- guild offices;
- diplomatic agents;
- multilingual clerks;
- foreign communities;
- criminals;
- intelligence brokers.

A remote event may be known locally before Port hears of it.

But once information reaches Port, Port can redistribute it through many networks at once.

That makes Port an amplifier.

## The High Roads — overland aggregation

The High Roads perform a complementary role for inland movement.

Markets, caravan yards, route authorities, interpreters, courts, toll systems, and merchant communities can concentrate information from multiple overland corridors.

Port and the High Roads therefore gather **different samples of the world**.

Neither should be omniscient.

## Markets and border towns

Markets and contact settlements are local switching points where:

- travelers exchange route reports;
- prices become visible;
- languages mix;
- rumors are compared;
- foreign news is translated;
- local people decide what outside information matters.

Border towns can know both sides of a dispute unusually well while still being distrusted by both.

## Guild halls, shrines, caravanserais, inns, docks, schools, and archives

These are recurring social chokepoints.

They matter because information moves where people repeatedly gather, not only where governments place offices.

# 6. Information has a last-mile problem

A major city can know something while nearby rural communities do not.

A harbor can know maritime news that an inland court has not yet heard.

A village on a pilgrimage road can know distant religious news while knowing little about a neighboring valley off the route.

A mine may receive metal prices before it receives a new legal interpretation.

Therefore information geography should be modeled as **corridors and nodes**, not evenly spreading circles.

Likely information shadows include:

- remote valleys;
- minor islands;
- deep forest settlements;
- settlements off main rivers;
- routes closed by winter;
- communities with weak language overlap;
- politically isolated districts;
- Underpass sections disconnected from major junctions.

---

# 7. Different information has different fastest networks

| Information type | Often reaches first through | Structural weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local accident / disturbance | witnesses, neighbors, workers, markets | fast but noisy |
| Price / shortage | merchants, warehouses, Port agents, guilds | manipulation and commercial secrecy |
| Route closure / danger | travelers, caravan workers, sailors, guides | corridor-specific |
| Official law / decree | political messengers, clerks, local officials | authority can outrun comprehension; local arrival varies |
| Technical / craft change | guild and apprenticeship networks | may remain professionally restricted |
| Religious controversy | pilgrims, clergy, hospitality networks | interpretation varies by school/community |
| Family / migration news | kin, migrants, itinerants, travelers | irregular cadence |
| Scandal / rumor | markets, docks, inns, social networks | verification lags |
| Criminal enforcement changes | smugglers, corrupt contacts, underworld networks | compartmented and deceptive |
| Political intelligence | spies, diplomats, merchant agents, Council-linked networks | selective and costly |
| Historical evidence | scribes, archives, scholars, community collections | slow to circulate but durable |

No network is universally fastest.

# 8. Rumor, verification, and correction

A useful propagation chain is:

```text
event
→ witness
→ first telling
→ repeated retelling
→ hub amplification
→ action based on partial knowledge
→ verification attempt
→ correction / competing version
```

Rumors spread well because they have low transmission cost.

They require:

- no special material;
- little institutional permission;
- no guarantee of accuracy;
- only a person willing to repeat them.

Verification often requires more.

That means a false explanation can produce real consequences before anyone proves it false.

Examples:

- merchants hoard;
- travelers avoid a road;
- a guild pauses work;
- a crowd gathers;
- a ruler mobilizes;
- a faith issues a warning;
- criminals exploit panic.

The later correction does not automatically reverse those actions.

This supports the existing [information feedback loop](World-System.md#8-feedback-loops).

# 9. Records are a different kind of power

Oral information dominates many ordinary situations, but durable records change what can be proved later.

Records can include:

- contracts;
- account books;
- shipping logs;
- toll records;
- land claims;
- court decisions;
- guild certifications;
- letters;
- maps;
- religious archives;
- scholarly copies;
- family or community records.

Important distinctions:

## Fast information

Useful for acting now.

Often incomplete.

## Durable information

Useful for proving, comparing, inheriting, or contradicting later.

Often slower.

## Secret information

Useful only if it reaches the correct recipient without being exposed.

May deliberately move more slowly or through fewer people.

This is why an archive can threaten a powerful institution even when it was never the fastest source of news.

# 10. Translation is part of the network

The setting is multilingual. See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md) for the structural model; exact family trees and languages remain unresolved.

Therefore long-distance information often depends on:

- interpreters;
- bilingual merchants;
- guild clerks;
- religious travelers;
- mixed households;
- Port-born multilingual communities;
- High Roads trade registers.

Translation can change:

- certainty;
- legal meaning;
- tone;
- status;
- responsibility;
- who supposedly witnessed something.

A translated message is not automatically a corrupted message, but translation adds another human decision point.

# 11. Political consequences

Information asymmetry creates power.

Actors who know something earlier can:

- buy before prices change;
- move goods;
- close a route;
- recruit labor;
- flee;
- mobilize security;
- spread a preferred explanation;
- destroy records;
- warn allies;
- create a shortage simply by expecting one.

This gives the Council believable influence without omniscience.

The Council can gain advantage by aggregating information across otherwise separate systems.

But the world already contains non-Council observation networks:

- merchants;
- guild branches;
- Port institutions;
- High Roads route networks;
- faiths;
- itinerants;
- sailors;
- local authorities;
- criminal networks.

The Council's advantage may therefore be less **having all the raw information** than:

- combining it;
- comparing it;
- acting across domains;
- financing responses;
- keeping coordination private.

# 12. The Underpass as an information bypass

See [The Underpass](Places/The-Underpass.md).

The Underpass can move information when surface routes are:

- closed;
- watched;
- taxed;
- militarized;
- politically dangerous.

But it is not a magic shortcut.

Information moving through it inherits the Underpass's:

- fragmented authority;
- unstable routes;
- guide dependence;
- danger;
- capacity limits;
- criminal overlap.

That makes it especially useful for:

- covert messages;
- smuggler intelligence;
- refugee news;
- local warnings;
- political information someone wants to keep off official roads.

---

# 13. Movement changes information

Movement is not only a delivery system.

People interpret what they carry.

A traveler may:

- shorten a story;
- translate it;
- attach a moral lesson;
- remove technical detail;
- combine two reports;
- exaggerate danger;
- suppress a name;
- turn a price change into a political theory.

This is why different networks can carry different versions of the same event without anyone centrally fabricating them.

---

# 14. A practical propagation test

For any important event, trace at least five questions:

1. **Who knows first?**
2. **Who has a reason to carry it?**
3. **What route do they actually use?**
4. **Who hears it before verification?**
5. **Who receives the durable or authoritative version later?**

Then test at least three audiences:

- a major hub;
- a place on the same route but outside the hub;
- a place geographically nearby but poorly connected.

If all three learn the same thing at the same time, the information network is probably too flat.

# 15. Worked structural examples

These are mechanism examples, not newly canonized events.

## Example A — a North Coast ship fails to arrive

```text
destination harbor notices absence
→ sailors / ship owners / families know first
→ Port agents notice missed cargo or credit obligation
→ merchant houses adjust risk
→ rumor becomes piracy / storm / defection depending on audience
→ inland buyers learn through commercial routes
→ official investigation may arrive after markets have already reacted
```

## Example B — a High Roads pass closes

```text
guides / travelers / nearby settlements know first
→ caravan organizations redirect traffic
→ merchants change prices and schedules
→ itinerant and pilgrim networks carry local explanations outward
→ governments receive formal reports
→ Port may learn through missing or redirected cargo rather than direct observation
```

## Example C — evidence of Council manipulation appears

```text
local clerk / merchant / scholar sees one contradiction
→ professional or archival network preserves it
→ rumor produces an exaggerated theory
→ spies and Council-linked actors notice interest
→ separate records from another region provide corroboration
→ reliable proof emerges much later than suspicion
```

These examples show why plot-relevant information should not move as one package.

# 16. What is established versus open

## Established structurally

- information moves through physical and social networks;
- transmission is carrier-specific;
- Port is a major maritime/commercial aggregation point;
- the High Roads are a major overland aggregation point;
- guilds, faiths, itinerants, sailors, merchants, states, criminals, and spies can carry different information;
- rumors can spread through informal networks;
- records and archives preserve durable evidence;
- the Underpass can function as a bypass when surface routes are restricted;
- route frequency and institutional trust matter as much as straight-line distance.

## Still open

- exact travel times between hubs;
- exact messenger speeds;
- whether dedicated courier guilds exist and where;
- whether any polity operates a public postal service;
- literacy rates by place/class;
- standard seals, notarial rules, or document formats;
- exact language families and translation burden;
- exact schedules of ships, caravans, pilgrimages, and itinerant circuits;
- exact censorship regimes;
- exact signal systems such as beacons, flags, bells, or messenger birds;
- whether any rare magical communication exists at all.

Legacy writing guides mention messenger birds, enchanted messengers, scrying, and speculative courier guilds. Those remain **legacy proposals**, not current canon.

# 17. Worldbuilding rule

When writing or designing an event, do not ask only:

> Who knows?

Ask:

> **Which network knows, in what form, after what delay, and with what reason to believe it?**

That is the information equivalent of the world's broader rule:

> **Routes matter more than straight-line distance.**
