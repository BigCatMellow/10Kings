# Medicine and Healing

## Status

**Working structural owner — new synthesis prompted by legacy gap analysis.**

Legacy Volume 13 flagged medicine, healthcare access, practitioners and common treatment as underdeveloped categories but did not provide a coherent setting-specific medical system.

This owner is grounded in:

- [Daily Life and Customs](Culture/Daily-Life-and-Customs.md)
- [Religions](Politics/Religions.md)
- [Guilds](Politics/Guilds.md)
- [Magic Baseline](Magic-Baseline.md)

No confirmed magical healing is assumed.

---

# 1. Core rule

Illness, injury, childbirth and disability remain materially consequential.

Care exists on several layers:

- household care;
- local experienced healers;
- midwives / birth attendants;
- herbal specialists;
- barbers / surgeons or analogous practical specialists;
- guild-trained practitioners where supported;
- religious charity;
- wealthy private physicians;
- military / ship / caravan medical experience.

These categories can overlap.

---

# 2. Household care is the base layer

Most sickness begins at home.

Households provide:

- rest;
- food;
- fluids;
- warming / cooling;
- wound cleaning;
- simple splinting;
- isolation where custom supports it;
- familiar remedies.

The quality of care depends on:

- knowledge;
- time;
- food;
- clean water;
- space;
- money.

---

# 3. Expertise is uneven

A practitioner may be especially skilled in:

- childbirth;
- wounds;
- bones;
- herbs;
- fever;
- dentistry;
- animal medicine;
- occupational injury.

There does not need to be one unified "doctor" profession everywhere.

---

# 4. Training

Medical knowledge can be learned through:

- apprenticeship;
- family line;
- religious institution;
- guild / professional association;
- military service;
- shipboard experience;
- scholarly texts;
- practical mentorship.

Exact credentialing remains open.

---

# 5. Religion and healing

Faith institutions may provide:

- charity;
- shelter;
- nursing;
- food;
- hospice;
- burial preparation;
- counseling;
- practitioner training.

This does **not** prove supernatural healing.

A religious healer can be effective because of:
- experience;
- cleanliness;
- routine;
- social trust;
- access to stored supplies;
- community support.

---

# 6. Access is political

Care depends on:

- cost;
- distance;
- class;
- guild membership;
- patronage;
- charity;
- language;
- reputation.

A wealthy merchant can summon a specialist.

A remote laborer may rely on household knowledge or whoever is nearby.

---

# 7. Public health exists before modern medicine

Communities can learn practical patterns without germ theory.

Possible measures include:

- isolating the obviously sick;
- protecting water sources;
- removing waste;
- closing contaminated wells;
- regulating burial location;
- cleaning wounds;
- ventilating dangerous work areas;
- quarantine of ships / caravans when disease is feared.

Exact scientific explanations may be wrong while practices still help.

---

# 8. Occupational medicine

Different work creates different knowledge.

Examples:

- miners know crush injury / bad air;
- sailors know exposure / drowning;
- builders know falls;
- caravan workers know dehydration / animal injury;
- farmers know cuts / infection / heat;
- soldiers know trauma.

This practical knowledge may circulate through guilds.

---

# 9. Medicines and trade

Medicinal materials can include:

- herbs;
- alcohol;
- oils;
- resins;
- mineral preparations;
- cloth / bandaging;
- imported ingredients.

Do not assume every traditional remedy works.

Some may be:
- effective;
- partially effective;
- useless;
- harmful;
- culturally important anyway.

Trade disruptions therefore affect medicine.

---

# 10. Surgery and invasive treatment

Exact capability remains open.

Grounding rules:

- pain;
- bleeding;
- infection;
- cleanliness;
- skill;
- recovery time

must matter.

No modern-equivalent surgery should be introduced casually.

---

# 11. Childbirth

Childbirth should be treated as an important medical and social system.

Questions include:

- who attends;
- who is allowed in the room;
- what happens during complications;
- who cares for parent / infant afterward;
- which faith or household rites overlap with care;
- how wealth changes outcomes.

---

# 12. Disability and chronic illness

See [Daily Life and Customs](Culture/Daily-Life-and-Customs.md).

People with disability remain part of:

- households;
- work;
- guilds;
- religion;
- public life.

Avoid treating cure as the only meaningful outcome.

---

# 13. Epidemics and institutions

A major outbreak affects:

- markets;
- travel;
- labor;
- burial;
- religion;
- law;
- shipping;
- migration;
- rumor.

Institutions may disagree about:
- quarantine;
- trade closure;
- public gatherings;
- burial practice.

This creates real political pressure.

---

# 14. Fraud and uncertainty

Medicine is a high-trust field.

The world can contain:

- skilled practitioners;
- sincere but mistaken healers;
- charlatans;
- miracle sellers;
- dangerous remedies;
- counterfeit medicine.

Religious or magical claims remain subject to [Magic Baseline](Magic-Baseline.md).

---

# 15. Medical records

Where literacy supports it, practitioners / institutions may keep:

- recipe books;
- case notes;
- birth records;
- death records;
- supply lists;
- epidemic logs.

These can become valuable historical evidence.

---

# 16. Open questions

Still unresolved:

- named medical professions;
- training length;
- medical guilds / associations;
- surgical capability;
- common effective remedies;
- public hospitals / infirmaries;
- maternal mortality;
- epidemic history;
- sanitation by city;
- burial-health interactions.

---

# 17. Scene test

For a medical situation, ask:

1. Who notices first?
2. Who provides first care?
3. What does the household believe is happening?
4. Is a specialist available?
5. How far away?
6. What does treatment cost?
7. What supply is needed?
8. What can go wrong?
9. What happens to work / income?
10. Does faith overlap with care?
11. What does the practitioner actually know?
12. What remains uncertain?
