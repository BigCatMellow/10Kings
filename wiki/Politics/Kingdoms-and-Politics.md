# Kingdoms and Politics

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level.** Exact constitutions, titles, borders, treaty offices, and succession rules remain unresolved where noted.

## Principle

The six regions should not necessarily share identical political systems.

"Region" is a cultural-geographic term. A region can contain a kingdom, confederation, city-states, autonomous districts, chartered towns, religious lands, and old privileges.

The post-Convergence world is therefore **layered rather than neatly hierarchical**.

## Layers of authority

Different institutions govern different parts of life, and their jurisdictions overlap.

### Local authority

Examples include:

- villages and town councils;
- harbor authorities;
- clan institutions;
- irrigation associations;
- forest or grazing communities;
- pass settlements;
- neighborhood bodies;
- local courts and customary law.

They may control practical matters such as:

- land and water use;
- local markets;
- maintenance;
- policing;
- common storage;
- minor disputes;
- seasonal access;
- community welfare.

Local institutions can survive changes in rulers because people still need roads repaired, water allocated, disputes settled, and food stored.

### Regional and state authority

Crowns, confederations, city-states, leagues, assemblies, and other larger governments deal with some combination of:

- taxation;
- warfare and mobilization;
- diplomacy;
- major law;
- large infrastructure;
- border claims;
- recognized currencies or standards;
- treaties;
- appointments;
- regional courts.

Their authority is real but not total.

A ruler who formally controls a territory may still need guild cooperation, local compliance, credit, religious legitimacy, or treaty access for policy to work.

### Trans-regional institutions

Some important institutions cross political borders.

These include:

- guilds;
- merchant and caravan networks;
- religious institutions;
- pilgrimage systems;
- banks and credit houses;
- scholarly and archival networks;
- criminal organizations;
- Port's commercial and legal institutions;
- Convergence-era arbitration or treaty mechanisms where retained.

They matter because many problems do not fit one state's borders.

A caravan may cross several jurisdictions. A faith may serve communities in every region. A guild standard may matter to buyers in multiple kingdoms. A debt may be enforceable through merchant networks even where one ruler dislikes it.

### Hidden economic coordination

The [Economic Council](Economic-Council.md) operates through dependencies that cut across all of the visible layers.

Its influence comes from:

- food;
- materials;
- routes;
- infrastructure;
- finance;
- information.

This is not a higher legal government.

A king can legally reject Council pressure. A guild can resist it. A city can break a contract.

The Council's power comes from the cost of doing so.

## The Convergence did not erase overlapping sovereignty

The [Convergence](../History/The-Convergence.md) regularized borders, routes, trade, arbitration, and Port's special role without turning the known world into one state.

That means several kinds of authority can be valid at the same time.

A person might simultaneously be:

- a subject or citizen of a regional government;
- a resident of a chartered town;
- a member of a guild;
- part of a clan or extended kin network;
- bound by a religious institution;
- indebted to a merchant house;
- protected by a trade or pilgrimage agreement.

Political conflict often begins when those obligations point in different directions.

## Working political tendencies

### Ironcrest

Likely relatively consolidated compared with its pre-Convergence past, but power is divided among:

- crown/state institutions;
- mine owners;
- guilds;
- town governments;
- labor organizations;
- old landed families.

Its recurring political problem is the relationship between **productive capacity, ownership, labor, and military power**.

### Northwind

Likely preserves strong clan, harbor, and local rights even under larger regional leadership.

Maritime law may be partially separate from inland law.

Its recurring political problem is **who has authority over shared risk**: fisheries, harbors, convoys, rescue, shipping, and seasonal access.

### Greenvale

Could combine:

- regional monarchy or central authority;
- estate power;
- village councils;
- irrigation associations;
- cooperatives;
- market towns.

Its recurring political problem is **who controls abundance and vulnerability**: land, seed, storage, water, debt, and distribution.

### Highridge

Could be a federation/confederation of:

- cities;
- route districts;
- caravan houses;
- herding territories;
- old pass communities.

Its political legitimacy may come from negotiated compacts rather than a single dynasty.

Its recurring political problem is **who can set the terms of passage without owning everything that passes**.

### Deepwood

Likely the least centralized.

Possible layers:

- local councils;
- forest wardens;
- regional assemblies;
- sacred jurisdictions;
- market towns;
- hereditary authorities in some districts.

Its recurring political problem is **who has the right to define stewardship, access, and extraction**.

### Sunplains

Most explicitly plural:

- city-states;
- leagues;
- estates;
- ports;
- rural territories.

The Convergence may recognize the region externally even though internally it contains several sovereign or semi-sovereign governments.

Its recurring political problem is **how independent polities cooperate without creating a bloc strong enough to alarm their rivals**.

### Port

Port is politically distinct enough to require its own owner page: [Port](../Places/Port.md).

Its recurring political problem is **how a place can govern itself while its neutrality, trade, and security depend on agreements with outside powers**.

## Political behavior

Every government responds to:

- domestic legitimacy;
- old borders;
- guild pressure;
- food;
- credit;
- military readiness;
- religion;
- public rumor;
- elite family interests.

This is why the Villain can push states toward conflict even when the Council wants peace: rulers cannot always follow Council preferences without appearing weak, corrupt, or disloyal to their own populations.

## Common jurisdiction conflicts

Useful political tensions should often come from overlapping authority rather than simple rebellion.

Examples:

- a crown approves a road that local communities reject;
- a guild contract conflicts with town law;
- a religious sanctuary protects someone a ruler wants arrested;
- a Port treaty grants access that local merchants resent;
- a clan fishing right conflicts with a regional naval policy;
- an irrigation league ignores a city-state's political boundary;
- a Council-backed loan requires policies a ruler can legally refuse but cannot easily afford to reject.

These conflicts make institutions feel real because each side can possess legitimate authority over part of the problem.

## How war can begin against Council wishes

A plausible escalation chain:

1. a real grievance exists;
2. evidence appears to confirm the rival caused it;
3. local politics punish leaders who seem passive;
4. mobilization begins as deterrence;
5. mobilization is interpreted as preparation for attack;
6. border forces act without full central control;
7. deaths create public demand for retaliation;
8. Council de-escalation efforts appear suspicious;
9. leaders fear losing legitimacy if they compromise;
10. limited conflict becomes self-sustaining.

The Villain's strength is making each step locally rational.

## What remains unresolved

The structural model is stronger than the constitutional detail.

Still open:

- exact constitutional form of each region;
- what the Convergence created as permanent interregional institutions;
- how interregional arbitration is staffed and enforced;
- whether rulers can lawfully close protected trade routes;
- exact Port citizenship and treaty status;
- how military obligations interact with local/clan/guild rights;
- which legal systems govern foreigners, mixed-border communities, and Underpass enclaves.
