# The Economic Council

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level.**

The Council is one of the setting's core institutions, but several implementation details remain deliberately open:

- exact number of seats;
- seat titles;
- member-house names;
- succession rules;
- exact age relative to the Convergence;
- exact public facade;
- exact meeting practice;
- how much any ruler, guild, faith, or ordinary citizen knows.

The current six-domain model is useful as a **working portfolio structure**, not proof that six people literally own or control six sectors of civilization.

## Core concept

The Economic Council is a **private interregional coordination layer** built around a small number of extremely wealthy and well-connected houses whose influence crosses political borders.

Its members do not represent Stone Hills, North Coast, Low Rivers, High Roads, Longwood, Old Cities, Port, or any one polity.

Their power comes from occupying important positions in networks that many different societies already depend on:

- credit;
- storage;
- shipping;
- caravan trade;
- large contracts;
- mines and workshops;
- infrastructure finance;
- emergency supply;
- information;
- trusted commercial relationships.

Individually, the member houses are powerful.

Collectively, they can compare problems across domains and coordinate responses that no single kingdom, guild, merchant house, faith, or city can easily organize on its own.

That **coordination advantage** is the heart of the Council.

---

# 1. Source disposition

The legacy Council material contains several generations of the idea.

Early versions describe an almost omnipotent shadow government that can decide who prospers, who starves, and which rulers survive.

Later source files — especially **The Council - Reimagined** and **The Council - Stress Test** — move toward a more grounded model:

- merchant and banking families;
- supply-chain leverage;
- credit;
- distribution;
- contracts;
- front organizations;
- guild relationships;
- crisis response;
- long-lived institutional memory;
- subtle influence rather than direct rule.

The current owner follows that later direction.

Preserved legacy source:

- [Volume 08 — Power: The Council, Guilds & Underworld](../../legacy-notes/2026-09-22/consolidated-package/08_Power_The_Council_Guilds_Underworld.md)

The older "six people secretly control everything" model is **not** current canon.

---

# 2. Why the Council exists

See [The Convergence](../History/The-Convergence.md).

The Convergence created a more predictable interregional order without creating one central government.

That produced a structural gap.

The world became more dependent on:

- long-distance food movement;
- shared routes;
- maritime access;
- caravan security;
- credit;
- large infrastructure;
- arbitration;
- emergency response;
- cross-border contracts;
- reliable information.

But no single public institution was responsible for coordinating all of those things together.

A plausible Council history is therefore:

```text
pre-Convergence merchant / land / credit / transport houses
→ finance stabilization and reconstruction
→ repeatedly coordinate emergencies
→ discover that private cross-border cooperation works
→ accumulate privileged information and relationships
→ become increasingly indispensable
→ formalize private coordination
→ hide more of that coordination as its political implications grow
→ come to believe that the public order depends on them
```

The Council did not need to invent interdependence.

It grew powerful because interdependence already existed.

---

# 3. The coordination gap

The Council is strongest where a problem crosses several jurisdictions or economic systems at once.

Examples:

### Food crisis

A poor harvest can require:

- reserve grain;
- merchant credit;
- shipping or caravan capacity;
- warehouse space;
- political permission;
- price management;
- information about where shortages are worst.

No single village, guild, ruler, or merchant can necessarily coordinate all of those pieces.

### Route failure

A closed pass can affect:

- caravan schedules;
- substitute roads;
- warehouse demand;
- food prices;
- credit;
- military planning;
- Port shipping;
- local employment.

### Credit panic

A failed merchant house can affect:

- warehouse claims;
- ship finance;
- farm loans;
- construction projects;
- wages;
- public revenue.

The Council's real strategic asset is therefore not any single mine, granary, spy, or bank.

It is the ability to see several systems **together** and move resources between them.

---

# 4. What the Council is not

The Council is **not**:

- a world government;
- a replacement for kings, city councils, clans, guilds, faiths, or local law;
- an owner of every major business;
- an organization with perfect information;
- an organization capable of forcing any ruler to obey instantly;
- a supernatural conspiracy;
- a single unified mind;
- the source of every crisis;
- the only trans-regional institution in the world.

Its power is strongest when other institutions voluntarily continue using systems in which Council-linked houses are deeply embedded.

---

# 5. Public power versus secret coordination

The Council works best if its **components are visible while the pattern is hidden**.

A powerful merchant house, creditor, shipping family, warehouse network, landholder, patron, or investor may be publicly known.

What is less visible is:

- how closely certain houses coordinate;
- which decisions are jointly planned;
- which crises are being treated as one system;
- which apparent market decisions are actually political interventions;
- how much information is being pooled;
- which rival institutions are quietly being pressured by several Council members at once.

This creates several levels of knowledge.

### Ordinary people

May know:

- famous families;
- major creditors;
- merchant houses;
- local rumors about "the Council";
- stories about suspicious coincidences.

They generally do not know the full coordination structure.

### Merchants, guild leaders, senior clerks, and local elites

May know that certain houses cooperate unusually closely.

Some may believe there is a private consortium without knowing its membership or reach.

### Rulers and senior political figures

Knowledge can vary.

Some may:

- know a Council exists;
- know only the house they deal with;
- know several members but underestimate their coordination;
- deliberately cooperate while denying it publicly;
- treat the Council as a useful myth;
- suspect far more than they can prove.

### Council insiders

Even insiders need not know everything.

Compartmentation protects the institution and creates internal information asymmetry.

---

# 6. The member houses

The Council should be understood as a meeting of **member houses or equivalent economic networks**, not six isolated individuals with magical portfolios.

A member house can contain:

- family leadership;
- heirs;
- marriage alliances;
- factors and commercial agents;
- clerks;
- accountants;
- warehouse interests;
- lenders;
- ship or caravan investments;
- guild patrons;
- local political clients;
- information sources;
- retired insiders;
- rival branches of the same family.

The person occupying a Council seat represents a much larger network.

This matters because killing, exposing, or replacing one Council member does not automatically destroy the system behind the seat.

## Internal structure

See [Economic Council — Internal Structure](Economic-Council-Internal-Structure.md).

The current world and preserved Council material support only a limited set of internal facts:

- Council membership has generational continuity beyond individual representatives;
- succession need not work identically for every seat;
- members are nominal peers even when their practical leverage differs;
- each member operates through a larger family / commercial / guild / agent network;
- members retain their own domain organizations rather than functioning as departments of one central bureaucracy;
- they meet privately, exchange information, negotiate conflicts, and reach compromises;
- member aides, secretaries, accountants, spies, and other operatives exist;
- Council and house records preserve some institutional memory;
- members have strong incentives to keep internal disagreement from exposing or destroying the institution.

A few additional constraints follow from the existing world:

- coordinated action requires the relevant member networks to actually cooperate;
- a member whose network is essential to a plan naturally has bargaining leverage;
- secrecy makes a large centralized hidden bureaucracy harder to justify.

Details such as a shared secretariat, common treasury, formal voting rules, vetoes, regencies, sanction ladders, expulsion procedures, or one centralized Council archive are **not current canon**. They remain open design options until the world gives us a reason to need them.

The internal-structure page tracks these distinctions explicitly.

# 7. The six working portfolios

The six current portfolios describe **coordination responsibilities and leverage**, not exclusive monopolies.

A Council house may have interests in several domains.

The portfolio tells the Council who takes lead responsibility when a problem reaches that system.

## 1. Food and Biological Supply

Working title: **Harvest / Provision**

Concerned with:

- grain storage;
- seed networks;
- livestock movement;
- orchard and breeding stock;
- mills;
- emergency reserves;
- bulk food contracts;
- food transport coordination;
- some medicinal or biological supply where relevant.

Typical leverage comes from:

- finance;
- storage;
- contracts;
- reserve access;
- distribution relationships;
- advance purchasing;
- creditor relationships.

It does **not** own every farm or control every seed.

## 2. Materials and Production

Working title: **Materials / Metal**

Concerned with:

- mines;
- ore contracts;
- fuel for production;
- timber inputs;
- large workshops and foundries;
- strategic materials;
- construction metal;
- weapon-grade production;
- high-value craft capacity.

This portfolio is especially entangled with Stone Hills production, Longwood timber/fuel questions, North Coast shipbuilding, High Roads repair systems, Port workshops, and Old Cities construction.

It does not make Stone Hills "the Council's mining territory."

## 3. Routes and Transport

Working title: **Routes / Navigation**

Concerned with:

- maritime shipping;
- caravan capacity;
- major pass access;
- road use;
- warehouses;
- convoy arrangements;
- river transport where important;
- Underpass connections where available;
- shipping and caravan scheduling.

Its leverage often comes from **timing and cost**.

A route does not need to be closed to become politically consequential.

Making it:

- slower;
- riskier;
- more expensive;
- harder to insure;
- lower priority;

may be enough.

## 4. Infrastructure

Working title: **Works / Infrastructure**

Concerned with:

- bridges;
- roads;
- canals;
- irrigation works;
- harbor improvements;
- fortifications;
- large repairs;
- major construction contracts.

Its leverage is unusually long-term.

A road, bridge, canal, warehouse district, or irrigation system changes future economic geography.

This portfolio therefore creates constituencies as well as infrastructure.

See [World Synthesis — E/I](../Reference/World-Synthesis-EI.md#candidate-24--infrastructure-creates-constituencies).

## 5. Finance and Exchange

Working title: **Treasury / Commerce**

Concerned with:

- credit;
- debt;
- merchant finance;
- currency exchange;
- large loans;
- insurance-like arrangements;
- investment;
- emergency financing;
- settlement of large obligations.

This portfolio can connect otherwise separate Council interventions because most large responses require financing.

It should not be treated as a universal central bank unless later worldbuilding explicitly creates one.

## 6. Knowledge and Information

Working title: **Knowledge / Intelligence**

Concerned with:

- commercial records;
- market reports;
- maps;
- technical knowledge;
- guild information;
- political intelligence;
- scribes and specialist correspondents;
- blackmail where used;
- archival comparison.

See [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md).

This portfolio does **not** control the world's information.

Its strongest advantage is aggregation:

```text
merchant reports
+ route reports
+ price movements
+ guild information
+ political contacts
+ archives
+ selective intelligence
→ cross-domain picture
```

Merchants, pilgrims, guilds, sailors, itinerants, states, faiths, criminals, and local communities all possess information the Council does not create and cannot fully control.

The Council's advantage is often that it can compare those signals and finance a response.

# 8. How a Council intervention actually works

A useful operating loop is:

```text
OBSERVE
→ COMPARE
→ PRIORITIZE
→ CHOOSE LEVERS
→ DELEGATE
→ WATCH EFFECTS
→ ADJUST
```

## Observe

Council-linked networks receive incomplete reports from:

- merchants;
- warehouses;
- lenders;
- guild contacts;
- local officials;
- ship agents;
- caravan interests;
- political clients;
- spies and informants;
- archives and account books.

## Compare

The Council's value comes from putting information next to information that normally lives in separate institutions.

Example:

> grain prices rise + ships are delayed + a creditor is overextended + a pass may close early.

Each fact alone may look manageable.

Together they can imply a major shortage.

## Prioritize

The Council cannot fix everything.

It decides:

- what threatens system stability;
- what can be ignored;
- where intervention is cheapest;
- which local harm is acceptable;
- which political actor must be pressured;
- which crisis can be used to solve another problem.

This is where its moral logic becomes dangerous.

## Choose levers

Possible levers include:

- credit terms;
- bulk purchases;
- contract timing;
- insurance-like rates;
- investment;
- warehouse release;
- shipping priority;
- route substitution;
- infrastructure scheduling;
- patronage;
- selective disclosure;
- quiet political pressure.

More coercive tools can include:

- blackmail;
- bribery;
- engineered exclusion;
- deliberate information delay;
- coordinated commercial retaliation.

Those should be consequential choices, not the Council's default answer.

## Delegate

Most Council actions are carried out by people who may not know they are serving a Council plan.

They may believe they are:

- protecting a guild;
- making a profitable loan;
- prioritizing a shipping client;
- enforcing a contract;
- repairing the most important bridge;
- reducing risk;
- following a patron's request.

This keeps the Council grounded in ordinary incentives.

## Watch and adjust

Council interventions create new conditions.

They can fail.

A grain release may stabilize one city while collapsing prices elsewhere.

A rerouted caravan may save a trade flow while impoverishing a pass town.

An emergency loan may prevent panic while creating long-term dependency.

The Council must react to its own side effects.

---

# 9. How the Council governs without governing

The Council rarely needs to say:

> Obey us.

Its preferred form of power is to change the **cost of available choices**.

A ruler may remain legally free to reject a proposal.

But refusal can interact with:

- scarce credit;
- unfavorable contract terms;
- delayed infrastructure;
- lost shipping priority;
- more expensive insurance-like arrangements;
- guild resistance;
- alternate buyers receiving better terms;
- reduced access to emergency reserves.

This is not mind control.

The ruler still has agency.

The Council's power lies in making some choices materially harder than others.

---

# 10. Why people cooperate

Council influence cannot survive on fear alone.

Its networks must provide real benefits.

Possible benefits include:

- emergency credit;
- access to distant markets;
- crisis shipping;
- reserve supplies;
- reliable contracts;
- infrastructure finance;
- risk sharing;
- mediation between competing commercial interests;
- information aggregation.

A ruler, merchant, guild, or city can resent Council influence while still fearing the loss of those functions.

This makes cooperation morally and politically complicated.

---

# 11. Funding and material base

The Council should not possess an unexplained infinite treasury.

Its interventions draw from the wealth and productive positions of its member houses.

Likely sources include:

- lending;
- trade profits;
- storage and warehousing;
- land or resource interests;
- shipping or caravan investments;
- infrastructure contracts;
- insurance-like arrangements;
- shares in workshops or merchant ventures;
- fees associated with services its networks provide.

Whether the Council itself maintains:

- a pooled reserve;
- common emergency funds;
- shared property;
- a formal treasury;

remains open.

That decision should be made when succession, meetings, and institutional legal status are developed.

---

# 12. The Council and information

The Council should be powerful at information without being omniscient.

See [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md).

Its information advantage comes from:

1. **breadth** — reports from several economic domains;
2. **comparison** — seeing connections separate institutions miss;
3. **memory** — family and commercial archives across generations;
4. **access** — relationships with elites, merchants, guilds, and specialists;
5. **resources** — ability to pay for targeted information;
6. **action** — ability to finance a response after seeing the pattern.

Its weaknesses include:

- delayed messages;
- bad translation;
- false reports;
- self-interested agents;
- missing rural information;
- deliberate deception;
- compartmentation;
- internal politics;
- unknown informal networks.

A rumor can surprise the Council.

A local community can know something before it does.

An archive can contain evidence Council members have never seen.

---

# 13. The Council's limits

The Council becomes less believable if every obstacle proves it was secretly in control.

Its power has real limits.

## It cannot create resources

A failed harvest is still a failed harvest.

Credit can move food but cannot manufacture grain.

## It cannot erase geography

Storms, bad roads, closed passes, distance, and capacity still matter.

## It cannot command every institution

Guild branches, faiths, rulers, local councils, Port institutions, criminal groups, itinerants, and merchants have their own interests.

## It cannot guarantee compliance

A ruler may accept economic damage rather than comply.

A guild may strike.

A city may default.

A merchant may take a dangerous alternative route.

## It cannot observe everything

Information arrives unevenly.

## It cannot intervene without side effects

Every adjustment creates winners, losers, new dependencies, and opportunities for rivals.

## It can be bypassed

Possible bypasses include:

- secondary ports;
- local credit;
- guild finance;
- religious mutual aid;
- alternate routes;
- Underpass connections;
- direct political agreements;
- new suppliers;
- smuggling.

Bypasses may be expensive, unreliable, or politically dangerous.

That is enough to make them important.

## It can become too visible

Overt coercion threatens the Council's legitimacy and secrecy.

The more openly it acts like a government, the harder it becomes to claim it is merely commerce.

---

# 14. Why the Council remains secret

Secrecy protects more than the names of members.

It protects the **political meaning of coordination**.

Public knowledge that one merchant house owns warehouses is ordinary.

Public proof that several major houses secretly coordinate:

- grain availability;
- credit;
- shipping;
- infrastructure;
- political pressure;

would make them look less like independent commercial actors and more like an unelected government.

Secrecy therefore protects:

- legitimacy;
- contracts;
- political relationships;
- member houses from retaliation;
- the appearance of independent markets;
- the belief that rulers retain full economic sovereignty.

---

# 15. Internal politics

The Council is not one mind.

Different portfolios face different incentives.

Examples:

- Provision may support releasing reserves while Finance fears losses.
- Routes may want trade reopened while Materials benefits from scarcity.
- Infrastructure may favor expensive long-term redundancy while Finance resists the cost.
- Knowledge may see warning signs that other members dismiss.
- A member house may benefit privately from a crisis the Council collectively wants solved.

Each member house can also contain:

- heirs;
- conservative elders;
- ambitious younger relatives;
- professional managers;
- creditors;
- clients;
- allies by marriage;
- rival branches.

Council decisions should therefore involve bargaining.

A "Council decision" may be:

- unanimous;
- a compromise;
- a temporary coalition;
- an action one member tolerates in exchange for another concession;
- a policy some insiders actively undermine.

---

# 16. Institutional memory

One reason the Council can outlast rulers is continuity.

A throne, city council, guild master, or minister may change quickly.

Council-linked houses can preserve:

- account books;
- contracts;
- diaries;
- old alliances;
- debt histories;
- route records;
- crisis precedents;
- family memory.

That does not mean their records are complete or truthful.

It means they can remember patterns political regimes forget.

This is one reason the Council may sincerely believe it is more responsible than governments.

# 17. Relationship to the major regions and Port

The Council is trans-regional.

No portfolio should map one-to-one onto one culture.

However, different places naturally matter to different Council networks.

### Stone Hills

Important for:

- metals;
- workshops;
- dangerous labor;
- industrial credit;
- fuel;
- strategic production.

### North Coast

Important for:

- shipping;
- fisheries;
- ports;
- shipbuilding;
- convoy risk;
- maritime information.

### Low Rivers

Important for:

- bulk food;
- land credit;
- storage;
- seed;
- river transport;
- agricultural volatility.

### High Roads

Important for:

- caravan movement;
- route information;
- arbitration;
- brokerage;
- multilingual contracts;
- overland alternatives.

### Longwood

Important for:

- timber;
- fuel;
- medicinal and forest goods;
- stewardship disputes;
- difficult-to-monitor local access.

### Old Cities

Important for:

- urban merchant networks;
- irrigation;
- coastal trade;
- city-state finance;
- civic and commercial rivalry.

### Port

Port is especially useful to Council-linked networks because many systems meet there:

- shipping;
- storage;
- finance;
- contracts;
- foreign agents;
- repair;
- arbitration;
- information.

Port should **not** simply be "the Council's city."

Its neutrality, local identity, competing merchant houses, guilds, criminal networks, foreign communities, and political importance make complete Council control implausible.

# 18. Relationship to guilds, faiths, and other networks

## Guilds

See [Guilds](Guilds.md).

The Council can:

- lend to guilds;
- sponsor contracts;
- cultivate leaders;
- use guild standards;
- hire members;
- favor one branch over another.

But guilds have their own:

- members;
- factions;
- welfare obligations;
- political interests;
- regional branches.

A guild can cooperate with one Council house and oppose another.

## Religions

See [Religions](Religions.md).

Faith networks can provide:

- charity;
- mediation;
- archives;
- schools;
- travel support;
- moral legitimacy;
- trans-regional communication.

The Council may cultivate religious institutions but should not control them as a bloc.

Their independent social networks are one reason the Council never possesses the whole information environment.

## Criminal networks

See [Crime and the Underworld](Crime-and-Underworld.md).

Council-linked actors may sometimes:

- buy illicit information;
- use smugglers;
- bribe intermediaries;
- tolerate contraband when useful.

Criminal organizations remain independent actors and may profit from Council restrictions.

## Informal networks

Family, neighborhood, migration, itinerant, audience, and friendship networks are difficult to map completely.

These are important blind spots.

# 19. A worked structural example

This example illustrates the Council's mechanism. It does not establish a specific historical event.

## A major Low Rivers crop failure

### What happens without the Council

- local prices rise;
- merchants compete for remaining grain;
- rulers seek emergency imports;
- lenders fear defaults;
- North Coast and Port shippers demand higher rates;
- High Roads caravans redirect capacity;
- neighboring buyers hoard;
- rumors make the shortage worse.

### What the Council can see

Provision sees:

- reserve levels;
- storage contracts;
- likely harvest deficit.

Routes sees:

- available ships and caravans;
- closure risks;
- warehouse congestion.

Finance sees:

- which merchant houses may fail;
- which governments cannot pay immediately.

Knowledge sees:

- whether panic exceeds the physical shortage;
- where reports conflict.

Infrastructure sees:

- whether a damaged bridge or canal is restricting movement.

Materials may see:

- secondary effects on tools, fuel, construction, or labor.

### Possible intervention

The Council might:

1. finance an emergency purchase elsewhere;
2. guarantee part of the risk to shippers;
3. redirect some transport capacity;
4. pressure a warehouse network to release reserves;
5. extend credit to prevent a merchant collapse;
6. quietly discourage a neighboring polity from hoarding.

No one action "controls the world."

The power lies in coordinated timing.

### Cost

The intervention can still:

- raise prices elsewhere;
- privilege Council clients;
- deepen future debt;
- harm local merchants;
- create resentment;
- teach rulers that the Council will rescue them.

Stabilization can create dependence.

# 20. The Council's self-justification

The Council's strongest ideology is not:

> We deserve to rule because we are rich.

It is:

> Someone has to see the whole system.

Members can sincerely believe:

- rulers think too locally;
- guilds protect members rather than the whole;
- merchants chase immediate profit;
- crowds react to rumor;
- wars begin because nobody coordinates the dependencies underneath politics;
- the Convergence survives because someone quietly prevents cascading failure.

This belief can coexist with:

- profit;
- arrogance;
- nepotism;
- coercion;
- fear of losing privilege;
- genuine public service.

The Council is more interesting if some of its members are right about the problem and wrong about the legitimacy of their solution.

---

# 21. The moral problem

The central problem is not simply that the Council is secret.

It is:

> **Does being good at coordination create the right to make consequential decisions for people who never consented to that authority?**

The Council can prevent a famine and still:

- choose which city receives food first;
- decide which debt is protected;
- sacrifice one trade route to save another;
- protect institutions that preserve its own power;
- hide information to prevent panic;
- deny communities the ability to make informed choices.

Its useful functions and its illegitimate authority are deliberately entangled.

---

# 22. Relationship to the Villain

The Villain attacks the Council's coordination model.

He does not need to overpower every member house.

He can:

- make information unreliable;
- create simultaneous demands;
- force Council portfolios into conflict;
- create politically unacceptable costs for intervention;
- build or exploit bypass networks;
- make rulers see coordination as coercion;
- force the Council to act openly.

His strongest attack is to make the Council choose between:

- maintaining secrecy;
- maintaining stability.

---

# 23. Relationship to Wurdren

Wurdren encounters the consequences of Council decisions at human scale.

A Council action described internally as:

> redirect transport capacity

may mean:

- a village loses its normal market;
- workers are dismissed;
- a family cannot afford grain;
- a caravan town empties;
- a repair project is postponed.

Wurdren can also encounter cases where Council coordination genuinely prevented worse harm.

That keeps the conflict from becoming:

> secret group bad / rebellion good.

The question is what kind of order can replace private coordination without recreating the same problem in another form.

---

# 24. Main vulnerabilities

A Council crisis can come from:

- inaccurate information;
- simultaneous shocks;
- member-house rivalry;
- succession dispute;
- overextended credit;
- a new route bypassing an old bottleneck;
- a guild coalition;
- a powerful polity building independent infrastructure;
- public exposure;
- loss of trust in Council-backed contracts;
- a member pursuing private advantage against Council policy;
- independent trans-regional institutions coordinating without it.

The Council is strongest when coordination appears indispensable.

It becomes weakest when the world proves that indispensable functions can be performed elsewhere.

---

# 25. Still open

The following should remain unresolved until later passes:

- final number and names of seats;
- final member-house names;
- exact assent thresholds for routine, coercive, and constitutional action;
- whether any seat has a formal review right beyond functional leverage;
- exact common-reserve assessment;
- exact meeting location and cadence;
- exact public facade;
- archive locations and authentication practices;
- precise relationship to surviving Convergence institutions;
- how many rulers know it exists;
- how common Council conspiracy theories are;
- founding compact / charter name and wording;
- whether a house has ever been expelled or replaced;
- current seat-holders and internal family factions.

These are now **institutional questions**, not gaps in the Council's basic purpose.
