# Guilds

## Core role

Guilds are part economic institution, part professional community, part political lobby, part welfare system, and sometimes part coercive cartel.

They matter because pre-modern governments cannot directly administer every profession and service.

## Types

### Production and craft

Examples:
- smiths;
- builders;
- millers;
- shipwrights;
- weavers;
- potters;
- masons.

### Trade and transport

Examples:
- merchants;
- caravan masters;
- navigators;
- warehouse operators;
- river pilots.

### Service and learned professions

Examples:
- healers;
- scribes;
- teachers;
- surveyors;
- interpreters.

### Armed and clandestine organizations

Earlier notes include:
- Warrior's Guild;
- Assassin's Guild;
- Spy Guild.

These should be developed carefully so they function as plausible institutions rather than game classes.

## What guilds want

No guild wants only "more power."

Typical institutional goals:
- preserve monopoly or standards;
- keep member income stable;
- control apprenticeship;
- prevent outsiders undercutting rates;
- gain legal privileges;
- protect trade secrets;
- influence taxation;
- secure routes;
- obtain contracts;
- maintain member welfare;
- stop rival guild expansion.

## Current schemes

### Metalworking guild factions

Some want controlled production and traditional apprenticeship.

Others want broader output and new methods.

Council-linked investors may fund one faction to restrain another.

### Agricultural guilds/cooperatives

Fighting over:
- seed control;
- storage;
- land consolidation;
- pricing;
- new crop strains.

### Navigator and shipping guilds

Debating:
- convoy systems;
- piracy response;
- Port influence;
- route exclusivity.

### High Country caravan organizations

Competing over:
- safe-route certification;
- tolls;
- militia protection;
- maps.

### Builders and infrastructure guilds

Seeking long contracts and legal control over standards.

Their delays can become political weapons even when caused by ordinary disputes.

## Guild interaction with the Council

The Council does not "own the guilds."

It influences them through:
- loans;
- contracts;
- access;
- leadership patronage;
- legal favors;
- information;
- insurance;
- selective enforcement.

Guilds sometimes knowingly manipulate Council families in return.

## Guild interaction with the Villain

The Villain exploits legitimate guild grievances.

He may:
- fund reformers;
- expose corruption;
- arrange shortages that make one faction look incompetent;
- create demand for guild security;
- encourage strikes at strategically chosen moments;
- leak real documents mixed with misleading conclusions.

## Guild interaction with Wurdren

Wurdren encounters guilds as people:
- an apprentice denied work;
- a caravan team abandoned after a contract dispute;
- a healer unable to obtain supplies;
- workers threatened by owners;
- owners threatened by a strike.

He sees why the institution exists and why it can become harmful.

## Rule

Every major guild should have:
- leadership;
- ordinary members;
- regional branches;
- internal reformers;
- a funding model;
- benefits members would lose if the guild disappeared;
- abuses that outsiders resent.


## Guilds inside the wider social fabric

See [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md).

Guild membership is not total identity. Guild branches contain people of different regions, faiths, classes, political loyalties and voluntary movements.

This matters because a dispute that looks like "guild versus kingdom" from above may divide guild members internally through family ties, religion, local politics or participation in other social networks.

The provisional [Audience Movements](../Culture/Audience-Movements.md) model also creates possible non-guild coordination channels: a movement may mobilize workers, couriers, performers or mutual aid faster than formal guild leadership can.
