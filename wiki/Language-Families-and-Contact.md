# Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level. Exact language families, language names, scripts, sound systems, and family trees remain open.**

**Current topology test:** [Language and Naming Topology — V1](Reference/Language-and-Naming-Topology-Test-V1.md) now uses abstract W1 / N1 / R1 / P1 / F1 / S1 cluster handles to test the minimum historical continuity and contact structure required by the political map. The IDs are design tools, not language names or promoted family trees.

This page owns the world-level model for:

- language relatedness;
- dialect continua;
- mutual intelligibility;
- multilingualism;
- trade and legal registers;
- contact varieties;
- language shift;
- translation;
- prestige standards.

It does **not** establish one language for each cultural region.

# 1. Core rule

The six cultural-geographic regions are **not six language boxes**.

Languages should follow older human geography:

- valleys;
- rivers;
- islands;
- harbors;
- migration;
- conquest;
- kin networks;
- trade;
- towns;
- religious centers;
- old political borders;
- mobile circuits.

Therefore:

> **A map of languages should be messier than the modern regional map.**

Some regional cores may contain several unrelated languages.

Some language families may cross several cultural regions.

---

# 2. Source reconciliation

The preserved linguistics material contains useful and obsolete layers.

## Useful mechanisms

Legacy sources support:

- dialect pockets;
- border blending;
- code-switching;
- multilingual trade centers;
- professional jargon;
- a possible interregional trade register;
- Port contact speech;
- language variation by class, settlement, and migration history.

These mechanisms fit the current world.

## Obsolete packaging

Legacy material often assumes:

- one language per old region;
- one themed "accent" reflecting regional economy;
- named regional languages such as old Ironcrest-language concepts;
- accents defined by forge noise, forest hush, cold wind, or other theme-first logic;
- exaggerated phonetic spelling.

Those are **not current canon**.

Do not restore them merely because they are detailed.

---

# 3. Language families remain open

The world clearly contains linguistic diversity.

The exact number and relationships of language families are not yet established.

A future family model should explain:

- old settlement patterns;
- major migration;
- conquest;
- islands and coastlines;
- river corridors;
- mountain isolation;
- long-standing trade routes;
- pre-Convergence political geography.

Do not begin with:

> six regions, therefore six language families.

Begin with:

> **which populations were actually in repeated contact across centuries?**

---

# 4. Relatedness and intelligibility are different questions

Two languages can be historically related but difficult to understand.

Two unrelated languages can be easy to use together if speakers have long contact.

Therefore distinguish:

## Genealogical relationship

Did the languages descend from a common earlier language?

## Mutual intelligibility

Can ordinary speakers understand one another without study?

## Contact familiarity

Have speakers learned each other's vocabulary, pronunciation, or structures through repeated interaction?

## Register compatibility

Do both speakers know the same:

- trade register;
- guild terminology;
- religious register;
- legal formula;
- maritime vocabulary?

These dimensions should not be collapsed.

---

# 5. Mutual intelligibility should be a gradient

Useful levels include:

## High

Ordinary conversation is possible with adjustment.

## Partial

Basic topics are manageable; abstract or legal discussion is difficult.

## One-way asymmetric

One population understands the other better due to:

- education;
- migration;
- media/performance;
- trade exposure;
- political prestige.

## Register-specific

Speakers can negotiate cargo or craft work but cannot discuss family life easily.

## Low / none

An interpreter is normally required.

This is more believable than declaring languages simply "the same" or "different."

---

# 6. Dialect continua should exist

Neighboring communities may change gradually in speech.

A useful pattern:

~~~text
village A
↔ village B
↔ market town C
↔ river town D
↔ city E
~~~

A and B understand each other easily.

D and E understand each other easily.

A and E may struggle.

Political authorities may still label all five as one language or split them into several.

Language boundaries are partly social decisions.

---

# 7. Political borders can cut through dialect continua

The Convergence stabilized political borders.

It did not move every speaker.

Therefore a border may divide communities that:

- speak closely related varieties;
- share kin;
- attend the same markets;
- use the same songs;
- have different official written standards.

Over generations, schools and administration can make the two sides diverge further.

This is one way political borders slowly become cultural borders without beginning that way.

---

# 8. One polity can contain several languages

A political unit may need to govern populations that do not share a household language.

Possible responses include:

- multilingual clerks;
- translated proclamations;
- local administration in local languages;
- one prestige written language;
- bilingual courts;
- interpreters;
- language requirements for office.

Exact policy remains local.

Do not assume political centralization produces immediate linguistic uniformity.

---

# 9. One cultural region can contain several languages

Regional identity may persist across linguistic differences.

People can share:

- food;
- historical memory;
- political institutions;
- architecture;
- religion;
- work systems;

while speaking different languages.

Likewise, two communities can share a language while identifying with different cultural regions.

Language is one identity layer among several.

---

# 10. The Convergence creates standardization pressure

See [The Convergence](History/The-Convergence.md).

Treaties, maps, arbitration, contracts, weights, dates, and border descriptions create demand for:

- reliable translation;
- stable written forms;
- consistent names;
- defined technical vocabulary;
- interpreters;
- copies in multiple languages.

This supports a post-Convergence expansion of prestige and professional registers.

It does **not** require one universal spoken language.

---

# 11. A professional trade/legal register can spread without becoming anyone's home language

The strongest current candidate is an interregional register associated with:

- contracts;
- weights and measures;
- dates;
- cargo;
- tolls;
- arbitration;
- route conditions;
- credit.

High Roads and Port are natural centers for such a register.

This register may be:

- simplified;
- highly conventional;
- multilingual;
- borrowed from several languages;
- learned by profession.

Exact grammar and vocabulary remain open.

Do not automatically call it a "Trade Tongue" as if one complete lingua franca is already canon.

---

# 12. Registers can be mutually intelligible while home languages are not

Two merchants may conduct business effectively because both know:

- numbers;
- measures;
- contract phrases;
- shipping vocabulary.

Outside work, they may need an interpreter.

This creates an important worldbuilding distinction:

> **functional communication is not the same thing as fluent shared language.**

---

# 13. High Roads is a multilingual switching zone

See [The High Roads](Regions/Highridge-Plateau.md).

The region's route history supports:

- interpreters;
- mixed pass-town speech;
- caravan terminology;
- multilingual merchant households;
- legal/arbitration registers;
- loanwords from many directions.

High Roads should not have one uniform "merchant language."

Its linguistic importance comes from **translation competence and repeated contact**.

---

# 14. Port develops native contact speech

See [Port](Places/Port.md).

Port is not merely a place where outsiders temporarily mix.

Generations of settlement create locally born language forms.

A plausible process:

~~~text
multilingual harbor contact
→ repeated trade registers
→ stable mixed neighborhoods
→ children acquire local contact varieties natively
→ distinct Port speech
→ Port slang and commercial vocabulary spread outward
~~~

Exact classification remains open.

Some Port varieties may become:

- creoles;
- mixed languages;
- koine-like urban dialects;
- ordinary descendant languages with heavy borrowing.

Do not choose the label before the history is built.

---

# 15. Border towns create stable mixed speech

See [Border Towns](Places/Border-Towns.md).

A border community can develop speech that is not merely "half one accent, half another."

Over generations it may develop:

- its own grammar;
- local vocabulary;
- stable code-switching rules;
- mixed naming conventions;
- distinct politeness patterns.

The mixed form can become a community's first language.

That makes contact zones cultural producers, not linguistic leftovers.

---

# 16. Mobile circuits create linguistic bridges

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

Mobile households may know:

- several languages;
- route-specific registers;
- greetings and bargaining language;
- kin varieties spread across jurisdictions.

Children raised on circuits may acquire different repertoires from settled neighbors.

Mobile groups can therefore preserve:

- old vocabulary;
- minority languages;
- route jargon;

while also spreading loanwords.

---

# 17. Guilds create trans-regional terminology

A guild can standardize words for:

- tools;
- grades;
- measurements;
- contracts;
- techniques;
- defects;
- certifications.

Those words can cross language boundaries.

A craft term may be borrowed worldwide while ordinary speech remains different.

This is especially plausible for:

- metallurgy;
- shipping;
- finance;
- caravan work;
- irrigation;
- specialized medicine;
- recordkeeping.

---

# 18. Religions create another layer of shared language

Faiths can spread:

- prayers;
- ritual formulas;
- philosophical vocabulary;
- titles;
- textual languages;
- pilgrimage phrases.

A religious language can be:

- prestigious;
- archaic;
- partially understood;
- translated locally.

Do not assume the language of ritual is the community's everyday language.

---

# 19. Class affects language

Elite and non-elite speakers may differ in:

- literacy;
- access to prestige standards;
- multilingual education;
- legal register;
- pronunciation;
- vocabulary.

A wealthy merchant may speak several trade languages.

A rural worker may be monolingual but fluent in a highly localized technical vocabulary.

Neither is linguistically deficient.

They have different repertoires.

---

# 20. Written standards can be newer than spoken languages

Post-Convergence institutions can standardize:

- spelling;
- legal forms;
- maps;
- place names;
- contracts.

This may create a "proper" written standard that no village historically spoke exactly.

That standard can gain prestige through:

- courts;
- schools;
- government;
- guilds;
- religion;
- trade.

Modern written unity may therefore hide deep spoken diversity.

---

# 21. Scripts remain open

The current world does not establish:

- one script;
- several scripts;
- script direction;
- alphabetic vs syllabic systems;
- standardized punctuation.

Those choices should follow:

- language history;
- record traditions;
- religious transmission;
- trade;
- conquest.

Do not invent scripts merely for visual fantasy flavor.

---

# 22. Translation is institutional labor

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

Translation can be performed by:

- merchants;
- clerks;
- diplomats;
- guild members;
- religious travelers;
- bilingual locals;
- professional interpreters where such roles develop.

Important translation work includes:

- contracts;
- court testimony;
- maps;
- treaties;
- letters;
- records;
- technical manuals;
- religious texts.

Translation should be visible as work.

---

# 23. Translation changes legal meaning

See [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

Legal translation can create disputes over:

- obligation;
- certainty;
- ownership;
- dates;
- names;
- evidence;
- status.

A translated treaty may not map perfectly onto another language's categories.

This is especially important where one language distinguishes something another leaves implicit.

Do not use "the translator made a mistake" as the only mechanism.

Sometimes both translations are defensible.

---

# 24. Names are linguistic evidence

See [Naming](Culture/Naming.md).

Place names can preserve older language layers after everyday speech changes.

A location may contain:

- substrate name;
- translated name;
- conquest name;
- official standardized form;
- local pronunciation;
- commercial shorthand.

Therefore place names can reveal:

- migration;
- political history;
- old language boundaries;
- elite standardization.

They should not all match the modern cultural region.

---

# 25. Exonyms and endonyms should exist

Different language communities may call the same place or people different things.

A treaty may standardize one international form.

Locals may continue using another.

Using one form can signal:

- outsider status;
- political allegiance;
- age;
- class;
- profession.

Exact endonyms and exonyms remain open until language histories are developed.

---

# 26. Naming can survive language death

A language can disappear from daily speech while surviving in:

- river names;
- mountain names;
- family names;
- legal phrases;
- prayers;
- market terminology.

This gives naming history depth without requiring every old language to remain spoken.

---

# 27. Language shift should happen unevenly

A community may shift languages because of:

- marriage;
- schooling;
- trade;
- urbanization;
- political pressure;
- prestige;
- migration.

Different domains can shift at different speeds:

- household;
- market;
- religion;
- law.

A family can speak one language at home and another in public for generations.

---

# 28. Language revival can become political

Old languages or dialects can be revived through:

- schools;
- songs;
- religious practice;
- political movements;
- naming campaigns.

A revival can be sincere cultural preservation and also a political statement.

Do not assume "older" means more authentic.

Revival often creates new standardized forms.

---

# 29. Language loss has practical consequences

When a language declines, knowledge can be lost or made harder to access.

Possible affected domains:

- route names;
- land claims;
- oral history;
- medicinal vocabulary;
- technical terminology;
- religious texts.

This can create disputes where an old record survives but nobody fully agrees on its meaning.

---

# 30. Interpreters can hold power

An interpreter does not merely repeat words.

They choose:

- equivalents;
- register;
- politeness;
- ambiguity;
- emphasis.

In high-stakes settings, this can give interpreters real influence.

Examples:

- court;
- diplomacy;
- trade negotiation;
- treaty drafting;
- interrogation.

That influence can produce:

- prestige;
- suspicion;
- professional ethics;
- corruption;
- political patronage.

Exact institutions remain open.

---

# 31. Children can become linguistic brokers

Children in:

- mixed households;
- migrant families;
- Port neighborhoods;
- border towns;

may acquire languages faster than adults.

That can put young people in awkward roles as:

- household translators;
- market intermediaries;
- legal helpers.

This is a human-scale consequence of multilingual systems.

---

# 32. Language and information speed

A message does not merely need transport.

It may need:

- translation;
- copying;
- interpretation;
- verification.

Therefore language differences contribute to network latency.

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

A rumor may cross language boundaries through rough paraphrase faster than an official report can be translated carefully.

---

# 33. Mutual intelligibility can be political propaganda

Governments may claim:

- "we all speak one language";
- "their speech is only a corrupt dialect";
- "our language is completely distinct";

for political reasons.

Linguists, merchants, and ordinary speakers may disagree.

Do not treat official labels as neutral scientific facts.

---

# 34. Prestige language can create class resentment

A court or guild may require a standardized register that:

- rural speakers find unnatural;
- migrants must learn;
- elites master through education.

That can create:

- exclusion;
- comedy;
- resentment;
- code-switching;
- hypercorrection.

Language policy can therefore become ordinary politics.

---

# 35. The Underpass should be linguistically fragmented

See [The Underpass](Places/The-Underpass.md).

The Underpass contains:

- enclaves;
- guides;
- miners;
- traders;
- smugglers;
- surface migrants.

Therefore it should contain:

- enclave languages;
- mixed market speech;
- route jargon;
- borrowed terms from several surface languages.

Do not create one universal Underpass language unless later history strongly justifies it.

---

# 36. Religious, guild, and trade multilingualism can overlap

One person may know:

- home language;
- local prestige form;
- trade register;
- guild terminology;
- religious liturgical phrases.

That repertoire is more useful than declaring them "bilingual" or "monolingual" without context.

Language competence should be task-specific.

---

# 37. Writing rule

Do not represent language difference mainly through eye dialect or exaggerated phonetic spelling.

Prefer:

- vocabulary;
- syntax;
- register;
- what must be stated explicitly;
- evidentiality;
- politeness;
- code-switching;
- translation difficulty;
- naming choice.

This preserves readability while making linguistic difference meaningful.

# 39. What is established versus open

## Established structurally

- linguistic geography predates modern regional labels;
- cultural regions and languages do not map one-to-one;
- dialect continua and multilingual contact zones exist;
- High Roads and Port are major translation/contact hubs;
- professional registers cross language boundaries;
- Port develops native local contact speech;
- border communities can develop stable mixed varieties;
- post-Convergence institutions increase standardization pressure;
- translation affects law and information flow.

## Derived constraints

- cross-border law/trade requires interpreters or shared professional registers;
- multilingual states require local accommodation of some kind;
- official language labels may simplify real continua;
- place names preserve older language history;
- mixed households and migration create code-switching and language shift.

## Open

- number of language families;
- family trees;
- language names;
- sound systems;
- exact grammar;
- mutual-intelligibility matrix;
- scripts;
- literacy by language;
- prestige standards;
- trade/legal register structure;
- Port contact-language classification;
- High Roads local languages;
- Underpass language distribution;
- religious/liturgical languages;
- exact language policy by polity.

# 40. Design rule

When adding a language or dialect, ask:

1. Where did its speakers historically live?
2. Which populations were nearby?
3. Which routes connected them?
4. Which migrations or conquests affected them?
5. Which languages are related?
6. Which unrelated languages influenced it?
7. How mutually intelligible are neighboring varieties?
8. What professional registers do speakers know?
9. Is there a written prestige standard?
10. What does the language call itself?
11. What do outsiders call it?
12. What older place names survive from previous languages?

Do not begin with:

> What fantasy accent does this region have?

Begin with:

> **What contact history produced the speech used here?**
