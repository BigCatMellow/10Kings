# Couriers, Posts, and Message Networks

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level. Exact courier guilds, postal services, rates, routes, uniforms, relay stations, address systems, and legal privileges remain open.**

This page owns the practical movement of **deliberately sent messages**:

- letters;
- dispatches;
- orders;
- contracts;
- notices;
- reports;
- copied records;
- urgent warnings.

It does not replace [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md), which owns the wider circulation of rumor, news, records, and social information.

# 1. Core rule

There is no need for one universal postal service.

The setting already contains enough institutions to support several overlapping message systems:

- state messengers;
- merchant-house dispatch;
- guild branch correspondence;
- religious correspondence;
- caravan and ship carriage;
- paid private couriers;
- local runners;
- informal hand-carrying by trusted travelers.

Therefore:

> **A message network is usually attached to the institution or route that has a reason to move it.**

The world can have excellent message service along some corridors and almost none a short distance away.

---

# 2. Dispatch and post are different

A **dispatch** is sent because one message matters enough to justify a carrier.

A **post** or scheduled message service exists because enough messages repeatedly travel the same route to justify regular carriage.

That distinction matters.

## Dedicated dispatch

Useful for:

- military warning;
- diplomatic orders;
- high-value contracts;
- urgent commercial intelligence;
- legal summons;
- emergency requests.

Advantages:

- leaves quickly;
- can choose route for speed;
- may use a trusted carrier.

Costs:

- expensive;
- limited capacity;
- vulnerable to one carrier's failure.

## Scheduled carriage

Useful where:

- merchant traffic is frequent;
- institutions have branches;
- markets recur;
- ships or caravans depart predictably.

Advantages:

- cheaper;
- routine;
- can carry many messages.

Weakness:

- waits for the next departure.

---

# 3. The world has several message layers

A useful model is:

~~~text
LOCAL RUNNERS
inside settlements and nearby districts

        +

INSTITUTIONAL MESSENGERS
states, guilds, faiths, merchant houses

        +

COMMERCIAL CARRIAGE
ships, caravans, wagons, river traffic

        +

SPECIALIZED PAID COURIERS
where demand supports them

        +

INFORMAL TRUSTED CARRIAGE
travelers carrying letters for people they know
~~~

No layer covers everything.

---

# 4. Local delivery is its own problem

Getting a letter to a city is not the same as getting it to a person.

The final delivery may depend on knowing:

- household;
- workshop;
- guild hall;
- warehouse;
- ship;
- inn;
- neighborhood;
- patron;
- market stall;
- religious house.

A modern street-number system is **not established**.

In many places, an address may function more like:

> deliver this to the cooper working near the east market, through the guild hall.

This makes local knowledge valuable.

---

# 5. Addresses should follow actual settlement structure

Possible addressing references include:

- household name;
- family;
- guild;
- employer;
- district;
- landmark;
- market;
- street where established;
- estate;
- village;
- religious house;
- ship or caravan;
- official office.

Dense cities may develop more standardized address conventions.

Rural or mobile populations may rely more on social identification and known stops.

Exact systems remain local.

---

# 6. State messengers exist where states need them

Polities with:

- tax systems;
- military forces;
- courts;
- distant officials;
- border posts;

need some method of official communication.

That supports government messengers.

It does **not** mean all states operate public mail.

A ruler's courier service may carry only:

- orders;
- reports;
- tax records;
- military messages;
- diplomatic correspondence.

Exact government networks vary by polity.

---

# 7. State messengers may receive limited privileges

A polity may give official messengers rights such as:

- priority horses;
- access to government lodging;
- reduced tolls;
- permission through restricted gates;
- protection from ordinary search.

None of these are universal canon.

Any privilege should be derived from:

- how important dispatch speed is;
- who pays for the system;
- how easy the privilege is to abuse.

---

# 8. Merchant houses are natural long-distance correspondents

Major merchants already maintain:

- agents;
- warehouses;
- lenders;
- branch relationships;
- route knowledge.

Therefore merchant correspondence can move through:

- company riders;
- caravan agents;
- ships;
- trusted commercial carriers;
- partner houses.

This may be one of the fastest routine message systems along profitable routes.

It may ignore places with little commercial value.

---

# 9. Guild branches create another correspondence network

See [Guilds](Politics/Guilds.md).

Guilds may need to send:

- certification records;
- apprenticeship information;
- sanctions;
- technical notices;
- contract disputes;
- membership news.

A guild with many branches may maintain:

- its own messengers;
- contracted couriers;
- scheduled bundles through merchant traffic.

Exact guild dispatch systems remain open.

Do not create a universal Courier Guild merely because guilds exist.

---

# 10. Faith networks can carry messages beyond commercial corridors

See [Religions](Politics/Religions.md).

Religious houses and pilgrimage routes can transmit:

- letters;
- charitable requests;
- records;
- community news;
- religious decisions;
- family messages carried by pilgrims.

Their strength is access to communities commercial networks may neglect.

Their weakness is that message movement follows:

- pilgrimage;
- hospitality;
- institutional cadence;

rather than pure speed.

---

# 11. Ships naturally carry mail without being postal ships

A merchant or passenger vessel already moves between ports.

It can carry:

- letters;
- account copies;
- orders;
- legal papers;
- family correspondence.

Possible arrangements include:

- captain accepts letters;
- merchant house reserves packet space;
- harbor agent bundles outgoing correspondence;
- passenger carries private letters.

This creates maritime mail without requiring a dedicated postal fleet.

---

# 12. Caravans naturally carry message packets

The same logic applies overland.

A caravan can carry:

- merchant letters;
- guild packets;
- government papers;
- private correspondence.

High Roads is especially suited to organized handoff because routes converge there.

But messages can be delayed if:

- the caravan waits for cargo;
- weather closes a pass;
- guards refuse a risky route;
- the caravan changes destination.

---

# 13. Paid private couriers are structurally plausible

Where people frequently need faster delivery than normal carriage, paid courier services can emerge.

Likely customers:

- merchants;
- courts;
- guilds;
- wealthy households;
- political offices;
- foreign envoys.

A private courier may specialize in:

- one city;
- one corridor;
- one language community;
- high-value documents;
- difficult terrain.

Exact organizations remain open.

---

# 14. Message houses can emerge at transfer points

A message house is useful where many messages change carriers.

Functions can include:

- accepting packets;
- holding letters for pickup;
- bundling messages by destination;
- hiring onward carriers;
- recording arrival/departure;
- collecting fees;
- confirming delivery where possible.

Natural locations include:

- Port;
- High Roads caravan centers;
- major markets;
- border towns;
- important river ports.

This does **not** make message houses universal.

---

# 15. A message house can be several businesses at once

The same establishment may also be:

- inn;
- warehouse office;
- money changer;
- merchant factor;
- guild branch;
- caravan yard;
- shipping agent.

That is often more plausible than inventing a specialized post office everywhere.

Existing infrastructure can absorb communication functions.

---

# 16. Relays improve speed but require infrastructure

A courier who changes:

- horse;
- boat;
- runner;
- guide;

can move faster than one traveler carrying the message end to end.

But relays require:

- known stops;
- available animals/boats;
- trusted staff;
- payment;
- route continuity.

Therefore relay systems are easiest for:

- states;
- wealthy merchant networks;
- dense commercial corridors.

Exact relay-station systems remain open.

---

# 17. Handoff creates chain-of-custody questions

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Every transfer raises:

- who received the packet;
- whether it was opened;
- whether something was removed;
- whether the correct person received it;
- whether a copy was substituted.

High-value correspondence may therefore use:

- seals or marks;
- wrappers;
- witnesses;
- receipts;
- paired copies;
- known carriers.

Exact authentication remains local.

---

# 18. Delivery receipts are useful but not universal

A sender may want proof that a message arrived.

Possible confirmation:

- recipient mark;
- witness;
- return note;
- entry in a message-house ledger;
- verbal confirmation through the returning carrier.

The more valuable the message, the more likely someone pays for proof.

Ordinary private letters may have none.

---

# 19. Privacy is not guaranteed

A letter may pass through:

- scribes;
- carriers;
- message houses;
- translators;
- clerks.

Some recipients may need another person to read it aloud.

Therefore "private letter" does not mean nobody else sees its contents.

This creates social conventions around:

- discretion;
- trusted scribes;
- sealed packets;
- coded language;
- reputation.

---

# 20. Literacy does not determine access to correspondence

A person who cannot write may:

- dictate to a scribe;
- ask a relative;
- use a guild clerk;
- use a religious house;
- send a verbal message with a trusted traveler.

A person who cannot read may have a letter read aloud.

Correspondence can therefore reach beyond the fully literate population.

---

# 21. Translation can occur during carriage

See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

A message may need translation:

- before departure;
- at a hub;
- at destination.

That creates delay.

It also creates questions over which version is authoritative.

High Roads and Port are natural translation points.

---

# 22. Timekeeping determines service reliability

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md).

A scheduled service needs:

- departure day;
- market cycle;
- ship window;
- route-opening season;
- expected arrival range.

Exact clocks are unnecessary.

A practical schedule can be:

> the first caravan after the south pass opens.

Service can be regular without being daily or precisely timed.

---

# 23. Price depends on urgency and route

See [Money, Credit, Exchange, and Measures](Money-Credit-and-Measures.md).

Message cost can vary by:

- distance;
- urgency;
- route risk;
- weight;
- privacy;
- number of handoffs;
- proof of delivery;
- translation.

A routine packet carried with a caravan should cost less than a rider sent only for one letter.

Exact rates remain open.

---

# 24. Institutions may subsidize correspondence

A guild, faith, employer, or government may carry some messages cheaply or free because communication benefits the institution.

Examples:

- guild certification;
- military orders;
- religious charity correspondence;
- branch records.

The sender's cost does not always equal the system's cost.

---

# 25. Emergency messages compete for priority

A network may need to choose between:

- military warning;
- court order;
- famine report;
- merchant price news;
- private family letter.

Priority can be determined by:

- payment;
- office;
- law;
- institutional rules;
- personal corruption.

This creates politics inside communication infrastructure.

---

# 26. Interception is ordinary intelligence work

See [Movement and Information](Movement-and-Information.md).

A message can be:

- stolen;
- copied;
- delayed;
- destroyed;
- redirected;
- opened and resealed.

Spies do not need magical communication if they can penetrate:

- courier networks;
- inns;
- docks;
- guild offices;
- message houses.

Communication security is therefore partly social.

---

# 27. Censorship is easier at chokepoints

Authorities can inspect messages more effectively at:

- gates;
- ferries;
- ports;
- border posts;
- caravan stations.

But aggressive inspection can push senders toward:

- smugglers;
- private travelers;
- alternate routes;
- code.

No government can necessarily inspect every message.

---

# 28. Smugglers can carry messages too

Illicit networks may carry:

- criminal instructions;
- political correspondence;
- banned religious texts;
- family messages across closed borders.

They may be faster than legal networks in some corridors and less trustworthy in others.

Do not make illicit carriage universally superior.

---

# 29. Mobile households can provide trusted local carriage

See [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](Culture/Nomads.md).

A household that repeatedly visits the same communities may carry letters between known families.

Advantages:

- personal trust;
- knowledge of recipients;
- access to small communities.

Weakness:

- seasonal schedule;
- limited destinations;
- route disruption.

This can be socially important without becoming a formal post.

# 32. Border towns are handoff zones

Border towns can sit between:

- languages;
- currencies;
- legal systems;
- courier networks.

A message may need:

- translation;
- new carrier;
- new address form;
- new payment;
- new seal recognition.

This can make a small border town disproportionately important to communication.

---

# 33. The Underpass can bypass message controls

See [The Underpass](Places/The-Underpass.md).

The Underpass can provide:

- alternate carriage;
- hidden handoff;
- winter bypass where routes permit.

But fragmented authority and dangerous branches make reliability uneven.

Do not turn it into a secret high-speed postal network.

---

# 34. Failed delivery is normal

Messages can fail because:

- recipient moved;
- ship never arrived;
- caravan rerouted;
- road closed;
- address was vague;
- carrier died;
- letter was stolen;
- recipient refused it;
- institution dissolved.

A believable communication system needs dead letters, missing packets, and delayed correspondence.

---

# 35. A returned letter is information

Failure itself can tell the sender:

- the recipient moved;
- a route closed;
- a merchant house failed;
- a town changed control;
- a ship was lost.

Communication networks therefore generate information even when delivery fails.

---

# 36. Correspondence creates social habits

Reliable letter carriage can produce:

- long-distance courtship;
- merchant family management;
- distant apprenticeship ties;
- scholarly correspondence;
- religious debate;
- political organizing.

Poor service changes those relationships.

The existence of communication infrastructure should alter society.

---

# 37. Writing the Sequel: routes accumulate communication customs

Long-lived message routes can generate:

- trusted courier families;
- inns known for holding letters;
- shorthand address conventions;
- customary fees;
- old privileges;
- jokes about slow routes;
- institutional feuds;
- inherited expectations of confidentiality.

These details should emerge after the route has existed for generations.

---

# 38. The Convergence likely increased communication interoperability

The Convergence created more:

- diplomacy;
- trade;
- arbitration;
- border administration;
- protected movement.

That creates pressure for:

- recognizable official dispatch;
- reliable treaty correspondence;
- cross-border forwarding;
- translation conventions.

Current sources do **not** establish a Convergence postal service.

Safe conclusion:

> **The Convergence made cross-border messaging more useful and more regular without necessarily centralizing it.**

---

# 39. There is no need for instant communication

Current world systems depend on:

- delay;
- physical movement;
- partial information;
- rumor;
- verification.

Do not introduce:

- magical messaging;
- universal signal systems;
- continent-wide rapid post;

without re-auditing:

- politics;
- markets;
- war;
- Council secrecy;
- story timing.

See [Magic Baseline](Magic-Baseline.md).

# 40. What is structurally established

- dedicated messengers exist;
- routine messages can ride ships, caravans, and institutional networks;
- states, merchants, guilds, and faiths have reasons to maintain correspondence;
- paid private couriers are plausible where demand is high;
- Port and High Roads are natural message-transfer hubs;
- message handoff creates authentication and delay;
- final delivery depends on local knowledge;
- informal letter carriage remains important.

---

# 41. Derived constraints

- major hubs need some place or agent to receive and forward messages;
- valuable correspondence benefits from chain-of-custody practices;
- scheduled routes require calendar coordination;
- multilingual correspondence requires translators;
- public communication can exist without universal literacy;
- message interception is easiest at institutional chokepoints;
- dense corridors can develop better service than nearby peripheral places.

# 43. Design rule

When sending a message in-world, ask:

1. Who needs it sent?
2. How urgent is it?
3. Who already travels toward the destination?
4. Is there a scheduled departure?
5. Who pays?
6. How many handoffs occur?
7. Does it need translation?
8. How is the packet authenticated?
9. How is the recipient located?
10. Is proof of delivery required?
11. Who could intercept it?
12. What happens if the route fails?
13. What slower or illicit alternatives exist?

Do not begin with:

> where is the post office?

Begin with:

> **who already has a reason to carry this message, and what would make a dedicated service worth paying for?**
