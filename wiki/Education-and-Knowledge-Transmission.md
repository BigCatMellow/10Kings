# 1. Core rule

There is **no single universal school system**.

Knowledge is transmitted through overlapping institutions:

- households;
- apprenticeships;
- guilds;
- religious communities;
- tutors;
- scribes;
- local schools;
- archives and libraries;
- courts and administrative offices;
- caravan / maritime training;
- specialist professional networks.

Access depends on:

- class;
- place;
- profession;
- faith;
- language;
- gender / household custom where locally relevant;
- wealth;
- whether a child can be spared from labor.

---

# 2. Childhood learning

Most children first learn through household and community participation.

Possible early learning includes:

- speech and local etiquette;
- counting used in trade / household work;
- chores;
- seasonal knowledge;
- family stories;
- religious practice;
- craft imitation;
- basic navigation / route knowledge where locally useful;
- songs / games carrying memory.

Formal schooling is **one route**, not childhood itself.

---

# 3. Literacy is uneven

Literacy should be treated as a spectrum.

A person may be able to:

- recognize names / marks;
- keep simple tallies;
- read contracts slowly;
- read religious text;
- write letters;
- keep accounts;
- compose legal documents;
- copy manuscripts;
- work across multiple scripts / languages.

"literate / illiterate" is often too simple.

---

# 4. Apprenticeship is education

Guild apprenticeship is one of the strongest existing educational systems.

It can teach:

- craft;
- mathematics;
- measurement;
- materials;
- contracts;
- professional vocabulary;
- safety;
- reputation;
- law / custom relevant to the profession.

Apprenticeship also creates dependency.

A poor household may struggle with:

- entry fees;
- unpaid early training;
- tools;
- lodging;
- sponsorship;
- travel requirements.

Who gets access to skilled work is therefore political.

---

# 5. Religious education

Several faiths already support:

- schools;
- archives;
- libraries;
- moral teaching;
- interpretation;
- mediation;
- manuscript preservation.

Religious education can include:

- literacy;
- scripture;
- history;
- law / ethics;
- copying;
- rhetoric;
- charity administration.

Different branches of the same faith may disagree about:
- who should be taught;
- which texts are dangerous;
- whether knowledge should be public;
- whether teaching is charity or authority.

---

# 6. Specialist schools

Some places can support specialist institutions without implying modern universities.

Possible forms:

- advanced guild schools;
- scribal schools;
- legal / administrative teaching;
- medical training houses;
- navigation schools;
- religious academies;
- merchant accounting schools.

These institutions should require:
- teachers;
- funding;
- books / materials;
- students with time to attend;
- a reason the knowledge cannot be learned entirely through apprenticeship.

---

# 7. Libraries and archives

A library is not automatically public.

Collections may belong to:

- faiths;
- guilds;
- merchant houses;
- courts;
- city governments;
- wealthy families;
- schools.

Questions:

- who may enter;
- who may copy;
- what is indexed;
- what is secret;
- what language is used;
- who pays scribes;
- what survives fire / war / migration.

The Illuminated Circle is a strong trans-regional participant in this layer but should not monopolize knowledge.

---

# 8. Oral knowledge remains important

Written culture does not replace:

- songs;
- family teaching;
- route memory;
- work demonstration;
- oral legal custom;
- storytelling;
- recitation;
- memorized religious teaching.

Some highly skilled people may have little formal literacy.

Some literate people may know very little practical work.

---

# 9. Language creates access barriers

See [Language Families and Contact](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

Education can require:

- learning a prestige language;
- learning a professional register;
- translation;
- code-switching;
- learning a script different from home speech.

This creates brokers:

- interpreters;
- tutors;
- clerks;
- multilingual apprentices.

It can also reproduce class advantage.

---

# 10. Knowledge travels

Knowledge moves through:

- apprentices;
- migrants;
- marriage;
- traveling teachers;
- guild branches;
- pilgrims;
- merchants;
- sailors;
- refugees;
- copied texts;
- stolen texts;
- correspondence.

A technique can spread faster than the institution that created it.

---

# 11. Knowledge can be controlled

Institutions may restrict:

- recipes;
- technical processes;
- route maps;
- legal forms;
- religious texts;
- medical knowledge;
- financial records.

Control may rely on:

- secrecy;
- licensing;
- language;
- cost;
- apprenticeship rules;
- physical custody;
- social exclusion.

No institution can perfectly prevent leakage.

---

# 12. Education and class

Education can produce mobility while also protecting hierarchy.

Questions:

- can a poor child enter a skilled apprenticeship;
- can women / outsiders / migrants join a school locally;
- can debt bind a student to a sponsor;
- do elite children learn governance / languages unavailable to others;
- can military service provide training;
- can religious charity provide literacy.

Do not answer universally.

---

# 13. Education and political power

People who can:

- read contracts;
- keep accounts;
- write petitions;
- interpret laws;
- translate;
- preserve records

possess practical political power.

This matters to:
- guilds;
- rulers;
- courts;
- the Council;
- reform movements.

---

# 14. Crisis effects

War, shortage or migration can:

- close schools;
- scatter teachers;
- create emergency apprenticeships;
- destroy archives;
- move books;
- create refugee teachers;
- accelerate knowledge transfer;
- reduce children's time for formal learning.

Education is part of the world system, not insulated from it.

---

# 15. Numeracy and practical competence

Literacy is only one educational axis.

Trade, craft, agriculture, navigation, administration and debt create demand for:

- counting;
- proportion;
- measurement;
- area;
- time;
- account arithmetic;
- currency and measure conversion.

A person may be highly numerate while only partly literate.

Likewise, a skilled practical worker may know far more about a technical system than a broadly educated clerk.

See [Money, Credit, Exchange, and Measures](Money-Credit-and-Measures.md).

---

# 16. Education follows seasons, mobility and adulthood

Formal learning does not need to follow one uninterrupted childhood school calendar.

Instruction may expand during:

- winter;
- agricultural off-seasons;
- periods between voyages;
- winter quarters of mobile circuits;
- temporary residence with settled kin.

Adults also learn through:

- migration;
- new work;
- guild advancement;
- military service;
- religious change;
- language contact.

For mobile households, continuity can come from kin, work, songs, route practice, seasonal teachers, religious houses and repeated winter stops rather than one fixed school.

---

# 17. Credentials are jurisdictional

Training recognized in one place may be:

- accepted;
- discounted;
- retested;
- rejected;

elsewhere.

A skilled migrant can therefore lose **recognized status** without losing actual competence.

Professional recognition may depend on:

- guild records;
- a master's letter;
- examination;
- completed work;
- reputation;
- local patronage.

This connects education directly to [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](Law-and-Jurisdiction.md) and [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Formal credentials can protect standards while also becoming class and migration barriers.

---

# 18. Knowledge networks extend beyond schools

Learning can be financed or transmitted through:

- patronage;
- correspondence;
- traveling teachers;
- military institutions;
- merchant houses;
- guild branches;
- religious networks.

Reliable correspondence can create scholarly communities whose members rarely meet in person.

See [Couriers, Posts, and Message Networks](Couriers-Posts-and-Message-Networks.md).

Patrons can expand access while also influencing which questions are safe or valuable to pursue.

Do not create one university-like institution merely because advanced learning exists.

---

# 19. Apprenticeship contains power as well as training

A master may control:

- housing;
- work;
- certification;
- reputation;
- future employment.

Possible abuse includes:

- excessive labor;
- unsafe work;
- withheld instruction;
- delayed advancement;
- debt;
- favoritism.

Guild regulation may protect apprentices, masters, or both depending on the local institution.

This makes apprenticeship a labor relationship as well as an educational one.

# 20. Open questions

Still unresolved:

- approximate literacy and numeracy rates by place / class / occupation;
- dominant scripts;
- who teaches basic reading;
- whether any city has a broadly accessible school;
- how medical training works;
- how legal clerks are trained;
- the cost and duration of apprenticeship;
- whether examinations exist outside some guilds;
- seasonal school patterns;
- cross-border recognition of credentials;
- education arrangements for mobile households;
- patronage and private teaching;
- whether any university-like institution develops;
- how books / paper / parchment are produced and priced.

---

# 21. Scene test

For a character's education, ask:

1. Who taught them?
2. Who paid?
3. What did they need to learn?
4. What could they not learn?
5. Which language / script?
6. Did work compete with schooling?
7. What institution recognizes the training?
8. Who was excluded?
9. What practical advantage does the knowledge create?
10. How could it travel to someone else?
