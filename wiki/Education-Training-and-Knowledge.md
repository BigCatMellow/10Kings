# Education, Training, and Knowledge Transmission

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level. Exact schools, curricula, ages, attendance rates, examinations, degrees, teacher institutions, and academic centers remain open.**

This page owns the world-level model for how people learn:

- household knowledge;
- childhood instruction;
- apprenticeship;
- guild training;
- religious schooling;
- literacy and numeracy;
- scholarly study;
- administrative education;
- multilingual training;
- technical knowledge;
- oral transmission;
- professional certification.

It does **not** establish one universal school system.

# 1. Core rule

There is no single boundary between:

- childhood;
- work;
- training;
- schooling.

People learn through overlapping institutions.

A useful model is:

~~~text
HOUSEHOLD / COMMUNITY LEARNING
+
WORKPLACE LEARNING
+
APPRENTICESHIP
+
RELIGIOUS / CIVIC / PRIVATE SCHOOLING
+
SCHOLARLY OR PROFESSIONAL STUDY
=
EDUCATIONAL LIFE
~~~

A person's education is therefore a **path through institutions**, not simply years spent in school.

---

# 2. Education should begin with what people need to know

Do not begin by inventing school subjects.

Begin with:

> **What must a person learn in order to function in this household, occupation, institution, or polity?**

Examples include:

- counting;
- measuring;
- reading marks;
- household accounts;
- planting;
- navigation;
- animal handling;
- craft technique;
- route knowledge;
- languages;
- legal formulas;
- religious texts;
- local history;
- etiquette;
- land use;
- medicine;
- military drill.

Different lives require different knowledge.

---

# 3. Household learning is foundational

Many children first learn from:

- parents;
- older siblings;
- grandparents;
- kin;
- neighbors;
- household workers.

Household learning can include:

- speech;
- manners;
- religious practice;
- food preparation;
- childcare;
- repair;
- local geography;
- family history;
- work tasks;
- songs;
- counting;
- basic market behavior.

Formal schooling does not replace this.

---

# 4. Children learn by participating in work

See [Daily Life and Customs](Culture/Daily-Life-and-Customs.md).

A child may gradually move from:

~~~text
watching
→ carrying / fetching
→ simple supervised task
→ repeated responsibility
→ independent work
~~~

This is education even when nobody calls it school.

Examples:

- farm children learn seasonal work;
- dock children learn cargo marks and harbor routines;
- caravan children learn routes and animals;
- craft households teach tools and materials;
- merchant households teach accounts and languages.

Do not make every useful skill a classroom subject.

---

# 5. Formal instruction exists but is uneven

Some people receive structured teaching in:

- reading;
- writing;
- arithmetic;
- language;
- law;
- theology;
- history;
- administration;
- philosophy;
- technical fields.

Access varies by:

- class;
- location;
- occupation;
- faith;
- patronage;
- polity;
- family wealth;
- local institutions.

Exact attendance rates remain open.

---

# 6. Literacy is task-specific

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Education can produce different forms of literacy.

A person may be trained to:

- read religious texts;
- keep accounts;
- read contracts;
- copy records;
- recognize cargo marks;
- use maps;
- write letters.

Those skills do not automatically imply broad literary education.

---

# 7. Numeracy matters as much as literacy

Trade, craft, agriculture, navigation, and tax systems create demand for:

- counting;
- fractions or proportional reasoning;
- weights;
- measures;
- area;
- time;
- debt calculation.

A person may be highly numerate while only partly literate.

That should be ordinary.

---

# 8. Apprenticeship is one of the world's major educational systems

See [Guilds](Politics/Guilds.md).

Apprenticeship can combine:

- work;
- instruction;
- discipline;
- housing;
- wages or support;
- professional identity;
- certification.

Exact forms vary.

An apprenticeship may be:

- household-based;
- workshop-based;
- guild-regulated;
- informal;
- contracted.

There is no universal apprenticeship length or age.

---

# 9. Apprenticeship solves a real economic problem

A craft requires knowledge that cannot always be learned from a text.

Apprenticeships allow:

- masters to train future labor;
- young workers to gain skill;
- guilds to reproduce standards;
- families to place children into occupations;
- employers to secure long-term workers.

That creates both benefit and abuse.

---

# 10. Apprenticeship can be exploitative

A master may control:

- housing;
- work;
- certification;
- reputation;
- future employment.

Possible abuses include:

- excessive labor;
- withheld training;
- unsafe work;
- delayed certification;
- favoritism;
- debt.

Guild regulation may protect apprentices or protect masters.

Both can be true.

---

# 11. Apprenticeship does not always mean guild membership

Some professions may train through:

- family;
- informal mentorship;
- ship crews;
- farms;
- caravan households;
- military units;
- religious institutions.

A guild may later recognize or reject that training.

Exact credential rules remain local.

---

# 12. Certification is not universal

Some professions need formal recognition because failure is costly.

Examples might include:

- navigation;
- medicine;
- surveying;
- building;
- legal record work;
- high-value craft.

Other skills may be judged entirely by:

- reputation;
- demonstrated work;
- family tradition.

Do not create diplomas for every occupation.

---

# 13. Religious institutions are major educational providers

See [Religions](Politics/Religions.md).

Current canon already supports faiths maintaining:

- schools;
- archives;
- libraries;
- teaching traditions.

Religious education may include:

- literacy;
- scripture;
- philosophy;
- ethics;
- music;
- history;
- charity administration;
- recordkeeping.

Different faiths emphasize different fields.

There is no single religious education system.

---

# 14. Religious schooling can serve non-religious needs

A community may use a religious school because it is the available place to learn:

- reading;
- accounting;
- languages;
- recordkeeping.

Students may not all become religious specialists.

This can make religious institutions socially powerful without giving them political sovereignty.

Current religion design gives the Illuminated Circle interests in:

That supports scholars and teaching institutions.

It does **not** establish one continent-wide university controlled by the faith.

Local schools, libraries, patrons, and scholarly circles can vary.

# 16. The Eternal Forge overlaps with apprenticeship

The Eternal Forge's focus on:

- craft;
- disciplined transformation;
- ethical labor;
- apprenticeship;

makes it especially likely to interact with guild training.

But religious and guild authority should remain distinct.

A workshop can respect Forge teaching without being controlled by clergy.

# 17. Oral knowledge remains important

Some knowledge is best preserved through:

- stories;
- songs;
- recitation;
- demonstration;
- memory;
- repeated practice.

Examples include:

- route lore;
- genealogies;
- weather signs;
- craft techniques;
- community history;
- local ecological knowledge.

Oral knowledge is not simply an inferior version of written knowledge.

It can preserve things records ignore.

# 22. Elite education is broader and more political

Wealthy or politically important households may train children in:

- literacy;
- several languages;
- law;
- accounting;
- diplomacy;
- history;
- rhetoric;
- estate management.

This is not evidence of greater intelligence.

It is evidence of greater access.

---

# 23. Poor education is constrained by time as much as money

A child or adult may be unable to study because they are needed for:

- harvest;
- childcare;
- wage work;
- fishing;
- household production.

Seasonal labor can therefore shape schooling.

A school may exist and still be inaccessible.

# 24. Seasonal schooling is plausible

See [Calendars, Timekeeping, and Seasonal Coordination](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md).

In some communities, instruction may expand during:

- winter;
- off-season;

# 25. Adult education is ordinary

People may learn new skills because of:

- migration;
- new job;
- guild advancement;
- marriage;
- military service;
- religious conversion;
- new language environment.

Education is not confined to childhood.

---

# 26. Migration can create downward educational mismatch

A skilled person may move somewhere that does not recognize:

- their language;
- credential;
- guild status;
- training.

A trained healer, clerk, builder, or navigator can therefore be treated as unqualified after migration.

This is a legal and social problem, not evidence that the person lost knowledge.

# 27. Credentials are jurisdictional

See [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

A guild certificate accepted in one city may be:

- recognized;
- discounted;
- retested;
- rejected;

elsewhere.

Cross-border professions therefore need:

- reputation;
- mutual recognition;
- local examination;
- patronage;
- proof of work.

Exact rules remain open.

# 28. Educational records matter

See [Records, Literacy, and Authentication](Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

Training may be proved through:

- guild register;
- letter from a master;
- religious-school record;
- examination record;

# 29. Knowledge can be proprietary

Guilds, merchant houses, and families may restrict:

- techniques;
- routes;
- formulas;
- account methods;
- customer lists;
- maps.

Teaching therefore has politics.

A master may genuinely believe some knowledge should remain inside the profession.

---

# 30. Knowledge can also be treated as a public good

Other institutions may believe useful knowledge should spread.

Examples:

- religious charity teaching literacy;
- public debate traditions;
- agricultural knowledge exchange;
- medical instruction;
- route-safety information.

Conflict over who should know what can become political.

---

# 31. Books are not the only educational technology

Knowledge can be stored in:

- ledgers;
- manuals;
- diagrams;
- models;
- songs;
- marked tools;
- maps;
- examples;
- memorized formulas;
- demonstration pieces.

This matters in a world where writing materials and literacy remain uneven.

# 32. Copying is a learning activity

Students or trainees may learn by:

- copying letters;
- copying contracts;
- reproducing diagrams;
- reciting texts;
- duplicating accounts.

That also produces usable records.

Education and bureaucracy can reinforce each other.

# 39. The Convergence increases demand for educated intermediaries

The Convergence makes more work for:

- interpreters;
- scribes;
- clerks;
- surveyors;
- arbitrators;
- accountants;
- translators;
- record specialists.

Therefore post-Convergence society likely has more institutional demand for formal training than before.

That does not mean mass schooling suddenly appears.

# 40. Standardized education can strengthen identity

Governments, guilds, and faiths can teach:

- official histories;
- standard language;
- legal forms;
- moral values.

Over generations this can strengthen:

- polity identity;
- regional identity;
- religious identity;
- professional identity.

Education therefore participates in the same paradox as the Convergence:

~~~text
more cross-border interaction
+
stronger formal identities
~~~

# 41. Schools can create language shift

See [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

If schools teach a prestige language, children may gain it even when households use another.

That can produce:

- bilingualism;
- language shift;
- resentment;
- prestige accents;
- revival movements.

Exact language policy remains local.

---

# 42. Credentials can create class barriers

Once institutions rely on formal certification, people without access may be excluded even when they are skilled.

A long-standing practical skill can become harder to practice legally after:

- guild regulation;
- state licensing;
- formal schooling.

This is a natural Writing-the-Sequel consequence of institutional growth.

---

# 43. Knowledge institutions accumulate status

Over generations, a school, archive, workshop, or teacher lineage can acquire reputation.

That reputation can:

- attract students;
- attract patrons;
- raise fees;
- shape appointments;
- create rival institutions.

Educational geography should therefore emerge from history, not region labels.

---

# 44. There is no reason every major city needs an academy

Avoid the fantasy pattern:

> every capital has a grand academy.

An institution should exist because:

- teachers gathered there;
- patrons funded it;
- records accumulated there;
- students could travel there;
- a profession needed training there.

Sometimes a famous school should be in a secondary town, monastery, guild district, or border city.

---

# 45. Legacy "university guild" concepts remain provisional

Older notes contain ideas resembling:

- one large academic guild;
- formal university-like organizations;
- centralized scholarly orders.

Those are useful prompts but **not current canon**.

The current world already supports more distributed learning:

- faith schools;
- guild apprenticeships;
- scholarly families;
- private teachers;
- libraries;
- patronage;
- local schools.

A university-like institution can be added later if a specific historical need supports it.

---

# 46. Magic does not automatically create magical schools

See [Magic Baseline](Magic-Baseline.md).

No confirmed magical practice currently requires training infrastructure.

If magic is later promoted, ask:

- can it be taught?
- who teaches it?
- how dangerous is practice?
- what institutions regulate it?

Do not pre-build magical academies.

# 50. Design rule

When adding an educational institution, ask:

1. What knowledge does someone need?
2. Why can't it be learned entirely at home or work?
3. Who teaches it?
4. Who pays?
5. Who can attend?
6. What work must the student give up to attend?
7. Is the training written, oral, practical, or mixed?
8. What language is used?
9. Is there a credential?
10. Who recognizes that credential?
11. What institution benefits from controlling the training?
12. What knowledge is deliberately withheld?
13. What happens to people excluded from the system?

Do not begin with:

> What is the name of the academy?

Begin with:

> **What knowledge needs to be reproduced, and which institution has both the incentive and capacity to teach it?**
