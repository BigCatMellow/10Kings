# World Rules

These are the setting's equivalent of animation-model rules: constraints intended to prevent drift and keep new material recognizably part of the same world.

## 1. Regions are ecosystems, not gimmicks

**Do:** give regions environmental and historical emphases.

**Do:** remember that ordinary economic diversity exists everywhere.

**Don't:** reduce Western Uplands to mines, River Country to farms, North Country to fish, Forest Country to trees, High Country to caravans, or South Country to vineyards.

A region's famous specialty is what outsiders notice first, not all that exists there.

## 2. Geography must cause culture without dictating it

Climate, soils, rivers, coasts, elevation, forests, mineral deposits, and routes create pressures. Human history decides how people respond to them.

Two communities facing similar conditions may develop different solutions because of law, religion, language, past disasters, or political organization.

## 3. Borders are gradients

Modern political borders became clearer after The Convergence, but culture does not stop at a line on a map.

Border areas should show mixed food, building methods, accents, family ties, religious practice, law, tools, and loyalties.

## 4. History is messy

Pre-Convergence history must contain temporary victories, reversals, disputed claims, marriages, defections, raids, trade agreements, migrations, local exceptions, and contradictory memories.

Avoid: "Region A defeated Region B and owned this land until the Convergence."

Prefer: "Three valleys changed hands repeatedly; one town paid taxes to two rulers; one lineage still claims a field it has not controlled for 140 years."

## 5. Low fantasy means material limits matter

Travel takes time. Food spoils. Roads wash out. Animals tire. Messages arrive late. Iron rusts. Winter closes routes. Armies need food. Disease and injury matter. Building something large requires labor, money, timber, stone, tools, and political permission.

Magic cannot erase logistics.

## 6. Technology is craft-scale and pre-industrial

Use hand tools, animal power, water power, wind power, pulleys, screws, mills, kilns, presses, furnaces, boats, carts, roads, irrigation, fortification, paper/record systems where appropriate.

Avoid steam-age machinery, modern manufacturing logic, magical equivalents of electricity, or implausibly cheap precision.

## 7. Magic is scarce, uncertain, limited, or costly

Magic should feel like one more difficult property of the world, not a universal convenience.

When magic appears, define:
- who can use it;
- how reliable it is;
- what it costs;
- why it has not transformed every industry;
- how ordinary people understand or misunderstand it.

## 8. Institutions have constituencies

No kingdom, guild, religion, council family, or criminal organization acts as one mind.

Every institution should contain:
- beneficiaries;
- critics;
- careerists;
- true believers;
- reformers;
- people trapped by necessity;
- regional branches with different priorities.

## 9. The Council controls dependencies, not minds

The Council cannot simply order the world to obey.

It works through credit, contracts, monopolies, shortages, storage, shipping, construction, insurance-like arrangements, information, patronage, guild politics, and elite relationships.

Council plans can fail.

## 10. The Villain's schemes must survive ordinary scrutiny

His plans work because people have independent reasons to take each step.

A manipulated king still thinks he is defending a border. A guild still thinks it is protecting members. A religious leader still thinks the issue matters spiritually. A smuggler still wants profit.

Avoid schemes that require everyone else to become stupid.

## 11. Wurdren does not win by becoming the biggest power

His narrative function is strongest when his influence comes from relationships, credibility, persistence, compassion, local knowledge, and consequences of previous choices.

If he becomes another master strategist moving nations like pieces, the story loses its most important contrast.

## 12. Every major event should have second- and third-order effects

Ask:
1. What happens immediately?
2. Who pays for it?
3. Who unexpectedly benefits?
4. What becomes scarce?
5. What route changes?
6. What rumor spreads?
7. Which guild reacts?
8. Which faith interprets it?
9. Which criminal group exploits it?
10. What does the Council do?
11. How can the Villain use that response?
12. What does Wurdren encounter on the ground?

## 13. Culture is learned, contested, and changing

Younger border populations, migrants, merchants, soldiers, pilgrims, mixed families, and urban populations should often combine practices older generations consider incompatible.

"Traditional culture" itself should contain multiple traditions.

## 14. Draw globally, copy nowhere whole

Real cultures are inspiration pools, not templates.

Borrow solutions to similar human problems — high-altitude housing, irrigation, clan law, fishing preservation, courtyard living, mobile pastoralism, market institutions, oral history — then recombine them according to this world's own history.

## 15. Language affects habits of attention, not intelligence

Regional languages may make certain distinctions habitual: inclusive/exclusive "we," social status, evidence source, completed/ongoing action, animate/inanimate categories, certainty, direction, or kin relationships.

Do not claim grammar makes a people inherently rational, emotional, peaceful, collectivist, or superior.

## 16. Food must follow ecology, trade, class, season, and preservation

There is no single "Western Uplands cuisine" eaten identically by miners, nobles, farmers, children, and merchants.

Show regional families of food, then class and local variation.

## 17. Architecture is accumulated history

A city should contain old walls, reused foundations, additions, rebuilt burned districts, foreign quarters, guild construction, poor improvisation, elite fashion, and border influence.

Do not design an entire region in one architectural style.

## 18. Names need etymological logic

Settlement names may come from geography, founders, old peoples, industries, sacred events, rulers, directions, markets, forts, bridges, rivers, or older languages.

Names should often preserve history no longer visible.

## 19. Violence changes societies

Wars produce widows, missing heirs, debts, veterans, ruins, refugees, border minorities, abandoned roads, trauma, legends, land claims, taxes, and political institutions.

Do not use wars only to decorate timelines.

## 20. The world exists when the protagonists are absent

Guild elections continue. Religious debates continue. Crime changes. Children grow up. Crops fail. Roads are repaired. Families marry across borders. A town may solve its own problem before Wurdren arrives.

No faction should feel as though it waits offstage for the plot.
