# Prose test

The temporary rule lasted nine days before somebody printed it properly.

The inspector considered this a bad sign.

Temporary rules written by hand could still be denied.

Printed rules had families.

The new sheet arrived with:

- three route marks;
- two town seals;
- one corridor compact reference;
- enough amendments to prove nobody had gotten exactly what they wanted.

The inspector read it before opening the gate.

The guard captain read over their shoulder.

"That ours?"

"Partly."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we enforce it."

"Same thing."

"No."

The captain looked at the seals.

"Who changed the departure windows?"

The inspector pointed.

"Compact adjustment."

"Who asked for that?"

"Merchants."

"Which merchants?"

"Several."

"Helpful."

The inspector turned the page.

The main rules were familiar.

That was why they had survived.

### Guarded departures

Participating roadheads would publish:

- guarded departure windows;
- available escort capacity;
- current route warnings.

### Verification

Carriers joining guarded departures needed:

- recognized cargo / commercial papers;
- current route notice;
- or accepted transition evidence if already traveling when rules changed.

### Inspection

Roadheads could inspect:

- cargo marks;
- declared cargo category;
- carrier identity;
- passenger / guard count

where needed to keep the guarded group legible.

### Contribution

Participating carriers paid:

- corridor-specific protection / administration charges

under locally agreed schedules.

Not one High Roads tax.

The inspector appreciated that line because someone had clearly fought to include it.

### Emergency adjustment

Participating route authorities could:

- delay;
- redirect;
- split

a guarded departure when:

- route condition;
- verified attack;
- capacity

made the original movement unsafe.

The inspector read that section twice.

"Too broad," they said.

The guard captain nodded.

"Useful."

"Those are not opposites."

"They usually are."

---

By sunrise, the first argument had started.

A small carrier held up the new contribution schedule.

"I paid yesterday."

"Yesterday's departure."

"I paid for protection."

"You paid for yesterday's guarded departure."

"I am leaving today."

"Yes."

"Then protect me today."

"That is what today's charge is for."

The carrier stared.

"Do guards eat twice if I wait overnight?"

The guard captain said:

"Yes."

The carrier looked betrayed.

The inspector leaned over the counter.

"Your cargo is low-value repair cloth."

"So?"

"You can travel outside the guarded window."

The carrier looked toward the road.

"Safely?"

The inspector did not answer.

The carrier folded the fee notice.

"Exactly."

They paid.

Behind them, a larger merchant house presented:

- six wagons;
- its own hired guards;
- current route notice;
- recognized seals.

The clerk processing them moved faster.

The small carrier watched.

"Of course."

The inspector said:

"They have the documents."

"They have clerks."

"Also true."

"Same rule?"

"Yes."

"Same burden?"

The inspector looked at the six wagons.

"No."

That answer irritated both of them.

It was still true.

---

The new system worked best when nobody noticed it.

On the fourth day:

- a forged route advisory was caught because its mark did not match the current register;
- a wagon declared as ordinary household goods was found carrying restricted blasting material;
- two carriers were shifted out of one departure before a bridge closure created a dangerous backup;
- a guarded group changed route after a verified attack report.

Nothing spectacular happened.

That was the success.

Lenders began accepting the corridor notice as evidence of route status.

Some buyers preferred cargo booked through guarded windows.

A merchant house offered lower financing costs for recognized departures.

The inspector heard about that from three different people who wanted priority in the next window.

Success created queues.

Queues created complaints.

Complaints created more paper.

---

The route compact sent a second notice.

This one asked each participating roadhead to preserve daily counts for:

- wagons;
- pack strings;
- guards;
- passengers;
- broad cargo classes;
- delays;
- route diversions.

The inspector read it.

Then read it again.

The guard captain said:

"Good."

"Why?"

"Planning."

"For what?"

"Escorts."

"We already plan escorts."

"Badly."

The inspector looked at the columns.

"Why passengers?"

"Because people take space."

"Why cargo class?"

"Because some loads move slower."

"Why guards?"

"Because if three houses bring thirty guards between them, I do not need the same escort allocation as if they bring three."

That made sense.

The inspector disliked it.

Not because it was wrong.

Because the table answered more questions than the immediate one.

How many carts could move today?

How many armed people were already available?

Which cargo categories dominated the corridor?

How quickly could a roadhead assemble a protected group?

The guard captain saw none of that as ominous.

Why would they?

They needed tomorrow's escort plan.

The inspector opened a ledger.

"Fine."

---

Not every roadhead joined immediately.

One town refused the full passenger count.

Another accepted inspections but rejected the standard contribution schedule.

A pastoral-route authority insisted that:

- seasonal herd movement

could not be treated like commercial cargo.

The compact amended the rule.

Again.

That was High Roads governance.

The inspector found this comforting.

A rule nobody argued with was probably governing nobody.

---

A week later, an itinerant family arrived with:

- two carts;
- livestock;
- three children;
- no interest in joining a commercial guarded departure.

The inspector began explaining the new process.

The oldest adult stopped them.

"We are not cargo."

"I know."

"Then stop counting us like it."

The inspector looked at the daily ledger.

Passengers.

Animals.

Departure.

They understood the objection.

"The count is for road capacity."

"Then count the carts."

"The guards need to know how many people are in a departure."

"We are not taking the guarded departure."

"Then I do not need to count you."

The adult waited.

The inspector crossed out the line they had started.

"Local road?"

"Yes."

"No escorted window?"

"No."

"Then go."

The adult looked surprised.

"So the rule has sense."

"Occasionally."

The family passed.

The inspector added a note to the next compact review:

> **distinguish guarded-departure passenger count from ordinary local / seasonal movement.**

The rule became narrower.

Also better.

---

The first serious test came when an attack report arrived shortly before a guarded departure.

Not rumor.

Verified through:

- road station mark;
- two independent carrier reports;
- one injured guard.

The guard captain read it.

"Hold departure."

The inspector looked at the yard.

Twenty-three wagons.

Nine pack strings.

Several passengers.

Thirty-one private guards.

The number came to them without counting.

That bothered them.

"How long?"

"Until the east patrol checks the lower cut."

"That could be hours."

"Yes."

"Yard is full."

"Yes."

"Next window backs up."

"Yes."

"Merchants scream."

"Yes."

The inspector looked at the report.

"Hold."

The captain raised the signal.

The gate stayed closed.

People shouted immediately.

The inspector spent the next hour explaining the same thing in different words:

> verified attack;
> temporary hold;
> reassessment.

A large merchant threatened complaint.

A small carrier threatened to leave unguarded.

A passenger demanded refund.

The inspector gave none of them what they wanted.

Two hours later the patrol returned.

The attack had happened.

The road was clear now.

The guarded departure left late.

Everybody arrived.

The next day, complaints were filed.

So were thank-you notes.

More complaints.

The inspector preferred those.

They were easier to believe.

---

At the end of the month, the roadhead had:

- a guarded-departure board;
- an inspection register;
- daily movement counts;
- a current route-status ledger;
- a list of recognized escort providers;
- a procedure for verified emergency hold.

The inspector looked at the shelves.

None of this had existed in one place before.

Each piece solved a real problem.

Together they did something else.

The guard captain came in carrying a new board.

"Where do you want it?"

"What is it?"

"Reserve escorts."

The inspector looked at them.

"What does that mean?"

"People willing to take paid escort work on short notice."

"Why do we need a board?"

"So we know who exists."

The inspector looked at:

- movement counts;
- guard counts;
- departure schedules;
- reserve names.

"Put it by the door."

The captain did.

The inspector returned to the ledger.

That was how new systems arrived.

Not marching.

Needing a hook.

# V1 assessment

## Polycentric governance

**STRONG PASS.**

The rule belongs to:

- one participating corridor compact;
- several roadheads / towns;
- local adjustments.

It is not one High Roads national code.

## Safety payoff

**STRONG PASS.**

The system genuinely catches:

- forged notice;
- dangerous cargo mismatch;
- route-capacity problem;
- verified attack.

It is not security theater.

## Cost

**PASS.**

Small carriers experience:

- repeated charges;
- document burden;
- unequal administrative capacity.

Local / pastoral actors push back on overbroad counting.

## Correctability

**STRONG PASS.**

The itinerant-family interaction forces the rule to narrow.

That keeps the system from becoming instantly totalizing.

## Security conversion

**EXCEPTIONAL PASS.**

By the end the roadhead knows:

- movement capacity;
- cargo categories;
- guard strength;
- departure timing;
- reserve escort availability.

This is mobilization-relevant infrastructure built for commercial safety.

## Main risks

1. the new rule bundle may still be too complete / fast for nine days;
2. "restricted blasting material" may over-specify technology / regulation;
3. the reserve-escort board may move too quickly from commercial protection to proto-mobilization;
4. sequence may need clearer evidence that practices evolved from several temporary roadhead rules rather than appearing whole.

## Recommendation

Challenge pacing and scope.

Core should remain:

> temporary practices become corridor rules because they worked.
