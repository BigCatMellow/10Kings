## Revision rule — no protected names by default

Existing names should not be preserved simply because they are familiar or already embedded in notes.

The audit categories are therefore evaluative, not conservative.

A name may be:

- retained because it fits;
- reframed as a translation, exonym, nickname or later political name;
- substantially altered;
- fully replaced.

The deciding factor is **fit with culture, language history and world logic**, not continuity.

Only a very small number of story-central names may receive special handling, and even those should be reconsidered if they actively weaken the setting.

# Executive conclusion

The current world does **not** support a clean model in which:

```text
Ironcrest = one language
Northwind = one language
Greenvale = one language
Highridge = one language
Deepwood = one language
Sunplains = one language
```

Pre-Convergence politics were too fragmented.

The stronger historical model is:

```text
older local languages / dialect continua
        +
migration / conquest / trade / religion
        ↓
regional contact zones
        ↓
Convergence-era legal and commercial standardization
        ↓
modern prestige varieties + local dialects + mixed border speech
```

Modern regional identities can therefore be real and powerful **without being ancient linguistic monoliths**.

This also solves a major naming problem.

The six modern regional labels and several major geographic labels are unusually transparent English compounds.

One possible design interpretation is:

> **some macro-level names may be reader-facing or interregional translations / conventional renderings rather than literal native forms.**

That is a tool, not a preservation requirement.

If a macro label feels generic, culturally mismatched or too obviously theme-first once the relevant culture is deepened, it should be replaced rather than explained away.

Local endonyms, older substrate names, legal names and historical spellings can coexist where useful.

The point is to let the naming system emerge from the world rather than forcing the world to justify existing labels.

# 1. Historical language model

## Stage 1 — pre-regional language geography

Before the Convergence, language boundaries would have followed the same things political power followed:

- valleys;
- rivers;
- islands;
- harbor systems;
- caravan routes;
- forests;
- old settlement clusters;
- kin networks;
- migration;
- conquest;
- religious centers;
- trade.

A language map should therefore look **less tidy than the modern political map**.

One valley may preserve an old speech surrounded by a newer prestige variety.

A harbor chain may share maritime vocabulary across several rulers.

A major market can use three or four speech forms routinely.

A border town may have been bilingual centuries before the Convergence.

## Stage 2 — contact and specialized registers

Trade and institutions create languages inside languages.

Before any modern regional standard exists, the world plausibly contains:

- mining and craft registers;
- maritime law vocabulary;
- caravan and toll terminology;
- irrigation law;
- religious terminology;
- market pidgins;
- multilingual court practice;
- diplomatic interpreters;
- scribal conventions.

These specialized registers can cross ordinary language boundaries.

A smith in Greenvale and a smith in Ironcrest may share technical loanwords even when their household languages are unrelated.

## Stage 3 — Convergence standardization

The Convergence creates a new need:

**people who do not share a language must agree on exactly what a treaty means.**

That likely encourages:

- standardized spellings for important places;
- official exonyms;
- multilingual treaty copies;
- fixed terminology for weights, dates, borders and obligations;
- professional interpreters;
- a legal / diplomatic register;
- more regularized maps and route names.

Highridge is the strongest candidate for an important source of this interregional register because:

- it was already multilingual;
- it had arbitration traditions;
- its economy depended on cross-language negotiation;
- it is the leading candidate for the principal Convergence congress.

This does **not** mean everyone begins speaking “Highridge.”

A useful comparison is a professional koine or diplomatic register layered above ordinary speech.

## Stage 4 — post-Convergence regional standardization

The current [Convergence](../History/The-Convergence.md) owner says the settlement strengthened regional identities through law, schools, religious institutions, guild organization and elite historical narratives.

Language should participate in the same process.

After the Convergence, governments and institutions can promote:

- standard spellings;
- administrative vocabulary;
- court or school prestige dialects;
- regional histories written in favored forms;
- official place-name forms;
- legal definitions of personal names;
- standardized measures and records.

This means a practice now described as “traditional Ironcrest” can be genuinely old **or** a post-Convergence standardization of several older western traditions.

The same applies to language.

## Stage 5 — modern divergence and mixing

Standardization never finishes the job.

Modern pressures continue producing:

- urban dialects;
- guild registers;
- religious registers;
- class speech;
- youth slang;
- migrant varieties;
- border dialects;
- Port speech;
- Underpass contact varieties;
- revived archaic language;
- political renaming;
- deliberate rejection of official forms.

The world should therefore contain people who can disagree over the “correct” name of the place they are standing in.

# 2. Regional language ecology

These are **working historical shapes**, not finalized language families or conlangs.

## Ironcrest

Historical basis:

- mine-holding clans;
- fortified smith towns;
- valley lordships;
- merchant-backed warbands;
- craft alliances.

Likely language ecology:

- several western upland / valley varieties;
- old river and mountain substrate names;
- strong specialist craft vocabularies;
- town speech shaped by repeated labor migration;
- court / administrative standard emerging with political consolidation;
- heavy borrowing along Greenvale, Highridge and Deepwood routes.

Current responsibility / completion / inspection distinctions can remain a **possible feature of important Ironcrest varieties**, but not a universal trait inherited from “being industrial.”

### Naming implication

Ironcrest should contain names that predate mining dominance.

A river, village or mountain may have an opaque ancient name beside later names such as:

- translated “smith town”;
- a mine-family name;
- a fort name;
- a guild-renamed district;
- a royal administrative name.

---

## Northwind

Historical basis:

- coastal clans;
- harbor confederacies;
- island rulers;
- fishing-right alliances;
- raiding fleets.

Likely language ecology:

- coastal dialect chains;
- island varieties;
- inland languages or dialects distinct from major seaports;
- shared maritime vocabulary across political boundaries;
- clan / kin naming traditions;
- strong contact with Port and Ironcrest;
- prestige speech at major harbors that blends older clan varieties.

Inclusive / exclusive pronouns, evidentiality and coastal directional systems remain plausible features for **some important Northwind languages**, not mandatory regional psychology.

### Naming implication

Names can preserve:

- currents;
- reefs;
- seasonal anchorages;
- fishing banks;
- clan founders;
- islands;
- hazards;
- saints / shrines;
- obsolete maritime rights.

A place name may describe how sailors approach it rather than what it looks like from land.

---

## Greenvale

Historical basis:

- estate rulers;
- village leagues;
- river towns;
- grain confederacies;
- irrigation associations.

Likely language ecology:

- lowland river dialect continua;
- estate / market prestige forms;
- old village languages surviving around specific watersheds;
- substantial border bilingualism;
- agricultural terminology spreading through trade without erasing local speech.

Aspect and stewardship distinctions remain plausible as features of selected languages or registers.

### Naming implication

Names should often preserve:

- river channels;
- old estates;
- soils;
- mills;
- markets;
- family lands;
- flood events;
- irrigation works;
- vanished settlements.

“Greenvale” should not imply every local name is pastoral English.

---

## Highridge

Historical basis:

- toll lords;
- caravan families;
- herding confederacies;
- fortified pass towns;
- merchant coalitions.

Likely language ecology:

**Highridge should be the least linguistically uniform major region.**

It plausibly contains:

- old plateau languages;
- pass-town mixed dialects;
- caravan family speech;
- herding-community languages;
- migrant merchant languages;
- legal / arbitration registers;
- multilingual urban populations;
- route pidgins and stabilized mixed speech.

Its important linguistic contribution may be **standardization across languages**, not one dominant mother tongue.

### Naming implication

Highridge is especially likely to have:

- several names for one pass;
- translated route names;
- merchant shorthand;
- official treaty spellings;
- names derived from old toll families;
- names whose literal meaning is forgotten;
- “wrong” foreign pronunciations that become permanent.

---

## Deepwood

Historical basis:

- village alliances;
- forest clans;
- river settlements;
- warden networks;
- religious centers;
- hunting territories.

Likely language ecology:

- multiple forest and river language communities;
- waterways acting as language corridors;
- isolated pockets preserving older forms;
- specialist ecological vocabularies;
- ritual registers that cross local speech boundaries;
- substantial border borrowing.

Animacy / agency and ecological evidentiality remain useful possibilities for some language families.

They should not become “the Deepwood language is mystical.”

### Naming implication

Many names may refer to ecological states outsiders no longer understand.

A name translated by outsiders as “Old Grove” might originally distinguish a very specific succession stage, water condition or land-use status.

This makes translation loss especially important.

---

## Sunplains

Historical basis:

- city-states;
- irrigation leagues;
- merchant ports;
- noble estates;
- rural client territories.

Likely language ecology:

- multiple city languages / dialects;
- court prestige registers;
- rural speech;
- merchant lingua francas;
- irrigation and legal terminology shared across political borders;
- port varieties;
- older languages surviving beneath prestigious urban forms.

Status registers and rhetorical traditions are plausible in some cities, but should vary by polity and class.

### Naming implication

Sunplains should be one of the strongest areas for **political naming**:

- founder cities;
- dynasty names;
- civic virtues;
- canal / waterwork names;
- renamed conquered towns;
- old sacred names;
- port exonyms;
- deliberately archaic names used to claim legitimacy.

The same city can have different names in neighboring city-states.

---

## Port

Current canon already requires:

- native Port speech;
- heritage languages;
- trade pidgins / creoles;
- guild jargons;
- religious registers;
- neighborhood dialects.

The older “Trade Tongue” idea is useful only at the structural level.

Do not make Port speech a mechanical blend of one feature from each region.

A third-generation Port variety should have its **own grammar, slang, sound changes and identity**.

### Likely history

```text
multilingual harbor contact
→ simplified trade registers / interpreter culture
→ stable mixed neighborhoods
→ children acquire local mixed varieties natively
→ distinct Port speech emerges
→ Port speech exports commercial / cultural slang back outward
```

That final step matters.

Port should influence the rest of the world linguistically just as it influences culture.

---

## Underpass

The Underpass is not one ethnic or linguistic civilization.

Its communities come from:

- surface migration;
- miners;
- guides;
- religious communities;
- refugees;
- criminals;
- old enclave populations.

Likely result:

- enclave languages;
- route cants;
- occupational signal systems;
- mixed market speech;
- surface-language retention;
- local innovations caused by generations of isolation.

Do not create one “echo language.”

# 3. Naming strata

A place can accumulate several naming layers.

## Layer A — substrate name

Older than the current polity or regional identity.

Often:

- opaque;
- altered by sound change;
- misanalyzed;
- preserved only in a river / hill / district name.

## Layer B — local descriptive or lineage name

Named by people living there for:

- founder;
- clan;
- market;
- shrine;
- bridge;
- fort;
- spring;
- mill;
- quarry;
- waterwork.

## Layer C — conquest / administration name

A ruler or bureaucracy:

- renames it;
- respells it;
- translates it;
- appends a title;
- replaces a politically inconvenient name.

## Layer D — Convergence standard form

Treaties, maps and merchants need one form to use across languages.

That spelling can become internationally dominant even if locals use another.

## Layer E — commercial nickname

Merchants, guilds, sailors, soldiers or travelers shorten or alter the name.

Sometimes this becomes more common than the legal form.

## Layer F — political revival or reclamation

Later movements revive:

- the substrate name;
- a pre-conquest name;
- an old dynasty name;
- a religious name;
- a deliberately archaic spelling.

A naming dispute can therefore be a political argument.

# 4. Macro-name convention

## Working design rule

The following names should currently be treated as **stable reader-facing / interregional labels** whose exact local-language forms remain open:

- Ironcrest
- Northwind
- Greenvale
- Highridge / Highridge Plateau
- Deepwood
- Sunplains
- The Spine
- The Underpass
- Port

This keeps the wiki readable while avoiding the implication that every local culture names places using identical transparent English compounds.

### Important distinction

This does **not** mean these labels are fake.

They can be genuine widely used translations, exonyms or common diplomatic forms.

A Northwind speaker may use the local equivalent of “Northwind” in one context and a clan or homeland name in another.

An old Deepwood community may never describe itself as “Deepwood” except when speaking to outsiders.

Port citizens may simply say “Port” in the common interregional register while using older district or civic names locally.

### Open question

Whether the setting eventually needs a named “Common Speech” is unresolved.

Do not invent one merely to explain the reader-facing translation convention.

# 5. Existing-name audit

## Categories

- **KEEP / FRAME** — useful name; preserve, but explain its naming layer.
- **ETYMOLOGY NEEDED** — potentially good, but currently unsupported by naming history.
- **PLACEHOLDER / TRANSLATION** — descriptive design label should not silently become a native historical name.
- **LIKELY REPLACE** — weak fit under the improved naming rules unless a strong historical explanation appears.
- **PROTECTED CORE NAME** — central enough that the goal should be explanation, not casual replacement.

# 6. Personal naming systems — what must be decided

Do not build full name dictionaries yet.

For each major cultural / linguistic zone, eventually decide:

## Household / kin structure

- inherited family surname?
- patronymic / matronymic?
- clan name?
- house name?
- place name?
- profession?
- no permanent surname for many classes?

## Marriage

- does anyone change names?
- are children named through one line, both lines or local law?
- what happens in mixed marriages?
- can political marriages create compound house names?

## Class

- do elites use formal multi-part names?
- do workers use occupational bynames?
- do guild masters acquire office names?
- are some surnames legally protected?

## Religion

- baptism / initiation / pilgrimage names?
- devotional names?
- taboo names?
- names revived from saints / teachers / founders?

## Generation

- names that sound old-fashioned;
- fashionable imported names;
- revival names associated with politics;
- Port names no grandparent recognizes.

## Mobility

- how officials record foreign names;
- how names are shortened in Port;
- how Highridge contracts transliterate names;
- how a migrant family changes spelling over generations.

# 7. The Convergence as a naming event

The Convergence should probably leave visible marks in names even if it never intentionally reforms language.

Possible consequences:

## Standard treaty spellings

A treaty scribe chooses one form of a disputed town name.

Two centuries later, government maps still use it while locals use another.

## Translated boundary features

A ridge, river or stone gets a legal translated name because negotiators need a shared reference.

That translated name becomes internationally famous.

## Frozen mistakes

An interpreter mistranslates or simplifies a local name.

The mistake becomes legally binding.

## Duplicate names

Two treaty copies use different names for the same place.

Both later become evidence in a border dispute.

## Political renaming

A post-Convergence government renames a town to emphasize regional unity.

Older residents keep the previous name.

## Regional identity labels

The six modern macro-region names may become standardized precisely because governments, guilds, maps and schools need broad categories after the settlement.

This fits the established idea that the Convergence strengthened regional identity.

# 8. Naming and the competitive spheres

Do **not** name the Charter / Ascendant spheres yet.

The current history-first method gives a better route.

The eventual names should emerge from:

- the event that made the rivalry recognizable;
- a founding compact;
- an insult;
- a merchant term;
- a famous defection;
- a political slogan;
- a newspaper / broadside equivalent if one later exists;
- a treaty faction;
- a place associated with early coordination.

Several names may coexist.

For example, officials may use a dry diplomatic label while ordinary people use a mocking nickname.

The wiki's **Charter** and **Ascendant** labels remain analytical placeholders.

# 9. Smallest language-history tests

Before inventing language-family names, test the model on concrete cases.

## Test A — one Highridge pass

Give one major pass:

- an old local name;
- a caravan/trader name;
- a Convergence treaty spelling;
- one foreign translation.

Check whether all four can coexist without confusion.

## Test B — one Ironcrest town

Give it:

- a substrate river / valley name;
- a later mining or guild district name;
- a royal administrative name;
- a worker nickname.

Check whether the history is visible in ordinary usage.

## Test C — one Sunplains city

Give it:

- an old city endonym;
- a rival city's exonym;
- a dynastic or civic title;
- a Port merchant shortening.

Check whether politics changes which form a speaker chooses.

## Test D — one Port family

Track a surname or household name across three generations of migration.

Check:

- spelling;
- pronunciation;
- marriage;
- official records;
- neighborhood usage.

## Test E — Wurdren

Determine what his name signals to:

- someone his age;
- a younger Port clerk;
- someone from his home region;
- someone from a different language community.

If the answer is “nothing except fantasy protagonist,” the personal naming system is not ready.

# 10. Promotion recommendations

## Strong enough to promote structurally

The following are supported by current owners:

1. pre-Convergence language geography was fragmented;
2. modern regional languages should not be one-to-one with regions;
3. Highridge is likely unusually multilingual;
4. Port develops its own native mixed speech;
5. border communities develop stable mixed dialects;
6. post-Convergence institutions likely standardize some spellings and registers;
7. modern macro-region labels may be treated as common / translated labels while local forms remain open.

## Keep provisional

- exact number of language families;
- which families are related;
- phoneme inventories;
- native names of languages;
- exact Common / Trade Speech;
- exact script systems;
- exact local forms of macro-region names;
- etymologies of Summithold, Cindermarch, Solanterra, etc.;
- Wurdren's linguistic origin;
- final personal-name systems.

# 11. Immediate next work

After this audit, Phase 1 should continue in this order:

1. update the current Naming owner with the translation / naming-strata convention;
2. update Language and Thought with the historical multilingual model;
3. build **three naming-history vertical slices**:
   - Highridge pass;
   - Ironcrest town;
   - Sunplains city;
4. use those to derive reusable naming mechanics;
5. only then begin resolving existing names one by one;
6. build personal naming systems after place-name mechanics prove stable.

The goal is not to produce a conlang.

The goal is to make every important name feel as though **somebody inherited it rather than an author selected it from a fantasy-name list**.
