# Mythic Creature Historical Evidence Dossiers — V1

## Status

**Provisional author-side evidence design — not canon.**

This page develops the eight strongest legend families from:

- [Mythic Creature Legend Families — Synthesis V1](Mythic-Creature-Legend-Families-Synthesis-V1.md);
- [Mythic Creature Community Variants — V1](Mythic-Creature-Community-Variants-V1.md);
- [Mythic Creatures and Legendary Beasts — Framework V1](Mythic-Creatures-and-Legendary-Beasts-Framework-V1.md).

The evidence below is **fictional in-world evidence proposed for 10Kings**, not source-derived historical material.

Comparative research only supplies the mechanisms by which:

- records survive;
- stories mutate;
- specimens are misclassified;
- practical knowledge becomes folklore;
- institutions preserve contradictory accounts.

## Design rule

Every dossier must contain:

1. an earliest surviving account;
2. one physical / archival evidence object;
3. one famous disputed incident;
4. a worker / local version;
5. a child / popular version;
6. a scholarly explanation;
7. an unresolved remainder;
8. an author-side truth range.

The evidence should support more than one interpretation.

# 1. The Knocking Below

## Evidence character

**Occupational / archival / acoustic.**

Almost no serious tradition claims people routinely see a creature.

That is important.

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

One of the oldest surviving copied Stone Hills mine records contains a short entry about a crew abandoning a working after:

> repeated answering blows from beyond the face.

The original document is lost.

The surviving copy was made much later inside a guild / civic archive.

The record does **not** call the sound supernatural.

It mainly records:

- lost labor time;
- supervisor complaint;
- later collapse in the abandoned section.

### Interpretations

Believer:

> the warning was recorded before the collapse.

Skeptic:

> workers heard ordinary structural movement and correctly recognized danger.

Historian:

> later copyists may have preserved the entry precisely because the later collapse made it memorable.

All three can be true.

---

## Physical / archival evidence

### The sealed-heading reports

Several later mine / Underpass maintenance records independently note:

- tapping;
- knocks;
- repeated sound

from workings already closed or believed inaccessible.

Problems:

- old maps are incomplete;
- sound carries unpredictably underground;
- water / timber / rock movement can imitate striking.

The evidence is therefore real but weakly diagnostic.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Rescue Turn

Working incident:

A rescue party searching after a partial collapse reportedly stopped when an experienced worker heard a familiar knock pattern from the wrong direction.

The party changed route.

They eventually reached trapped workers from another side.

Later retellings say:

> the Knocking Below led them.

Contemporary / near-contemporary accounts disagree on whether:

- the knocking came from trapped workers;
- the trapped workers were too far away to produce it;
- the route change happened for another reason.

### Why this is useful

The incident is genuinely heroic even if nothing supernatural happened.

The legend does not need to steal agency from the rescuers.

---

## Worker version

Experienced miners:

> you do not need to believe in it to leave when the mine starts talking.

Younger workers:

- joke;
- imitate the rhythm;
- dare one another to stay.

Technical workers:

- use different sound classifications;
- may still know which folk rhythm older crews mean.

---

## Child / popular version

Simplified:

> something under the mountain knocks before it eats the tunnel.

This version is much more creature-like than worker testimony.

Port illustrations may give it:

- hands;
- claws;
- a miner's lamp;
- an invented face.

Miners often dislike these depictions.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Most scholars / engineers favor some combination of:

- rock stress;
- timber movement;
- trapped water;
- resonance;
- human warning codes remembered incorrectly.

A minority argue the sealed-heading corpus includes a residue not fully explained by known acoustics.

---

## Unresolved remainder

A small number of reports describe:

- the same short rhythm;
- from sealed or unmapped spaces;
- immediately before hazards not obviously connected to structural cracking.

The reports are too scattered to prove anything.

But they are consistent enough to remain annoying.

---

## Author truth range

**A / E / F / possible G**

Strongest working answer:

> mostly real mine acoustics + worker knowledge + accumulated disaster memory, with a deliberately unresolved deep-Underpass residue.

---

# 2. The Great Mouth / False Island

## Evidence character

**Maritime / zoological / copied records.**

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

An old North Coast captain's log describes:

- unusual calm water;
- dense fish;
- what appeared to be a low reef / dark shoal;
- sudden movement;
- loss of a small boat / gear.

The account never uses the modern popular creature name.

Later copyists identify it as an early Great Mouth sighting.

### Problem

The original language for:

- reef;
- back;
- shoal;
- mouth

is ambiguous enough that translation matters.

---

## Physical evidence

### The disputed rib / jaw fragment

One North Coast harbor preserves an oversized marine bone traditionally connected to a famous sighting.

Scholarly opinions:

- large whale;
- several bones reconstructed incorrectly;
- another known marine species;
- genuinely unusual specimen.

Port copies exaggerate it dramatically.

The actual object should look:

> disappointingly bone-like.

That helps.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Breathing Shoal

Several related family accounts describe a fishing party approaching a rich concentration of fish around what seemed to be a temporary shoal.

One person allegedly noticed:

- regular water movement;
- birds avoiding one section;
- a line that rose / fell "like breathing."

The boats pulled away.

The dark mass then moved.

### Later inflation

Popular versions add:

- island grass;
- landing party;
- enormous eye;
- entire boat swallowed.

Those details are absent from the older accounts.

---

## Local / worker version

Outer-island fishers:

> it was an animal, not a demon.

Some believe it is extinct.

Others say:

> if one still exists, the sea is large enough to hide it.

Major harbor crews are more skeptical.

---

## Child / popular version

Classic line:

> **do not step onto an island that breathes.**

Port festival versions sometimes make it absurdly enormous.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Strongest explanations:

- whale feeding behavior;
- multiple species conflated;
- floating kelp / debris + animals;
- lost / rare large marine species.

Naturalists disagree over whether "Great Mouth" refers to:

- one species;
- a behavior;
- several stories.

---

## Unresolved remainder

The oldest consistent accounts describe fish gathering in a pattern that some modern / current natural explanations do not fit cleanly.

The problem may be:

- bad translation;
- extinct behavior / species;
- storyteller compression.

Nothing requires magic.

---

## Author truth range

**A / B / C / D**

Working preference:

> **something biologically real almost certainly sits underneath the tradition.**

Whether it survives remains open.

---

# 3. The Marsh Lure

## Evidence character

**Environmental / oral / hydrological.**

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

An old Low Rivers flood / crossing record describes travelers delaying departure because:

> lights were walking over the old channel.

The phrase appears as ordinary local language, not an explanation.

Later scholars cannot tell whether it meant:

- literal lights;
- a known atmospheric condition;
- a local warning term.

This is useful because the earliest evidence may already be **idiomatic**.

---

## Physical / archival evidence

No convincing creature specimen exists.

Evidence instead includes:

- old channel maps;
- disappearance locations;
- flood marks;
- recurring sighting sites.

An interesting pattern:

many historical Marsh Lure places sit near:

- abandoned channels;
- seasonal overflow;
- marsh-edge crossings.

That supports natural explanation while making the folklore practically accurate.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Two Banks

During a high-water season, separate groups on opposite sides of a floodplain reportedly described:

- two pale lights;
- maintaining distance from one another;
- moving upstream against apparent wind.

One group stayed put.

Another attempted a crossing and lost a person / animal in a newly opened channel.

### Dispute

Skeptics argue:

- reflections;
- carried lanterns;
- mistaken current;
- later story-merging.

Believers point to the independent reports.

---

## Local / worker version

Floodplain residents:

> the lights do not need to be spirits to kill you.

Canal / irrigation workers:

- connect them with old channels;
- use the story as infrastructure memory.

CZ-3 hybrid communities:

- connect moving light with diverted / stolen water.

---

## Child / popular version

Something ahead of you:

- wants you to follow;
- moves when you move;
- stops when you stop.

The child version personifies what adults may treat as phenomenon.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Likely combination of:

- marsh / decomposition gases;
- reflections;
- bioluminescent organisms where ecologically plausible;
- lanterns;
- fog;
- changing water channels;
- expectation / fatigue.

No single explanation needs to cover every sighting.

---

## Unresolved remainder

The hardest cases involve:

- paired / coordinated lights;
- apparent movement against expected wind / current;
- simultaneous witnesses from separated locations.

Could still be observational error.

But not trivially.

---

## Author truth range

**A-like natural phenomenon / D / E / F / possible G**

Working preference:

> overwhelmingly natural / perceptual phenomenon with some deliberately difficult cases.

---

# 4. The Forest Owner

## Evidence character

**Jurisdictional / navigational / oral.**

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

An old Longwood dispute record concerns outsiders accused of taking a resource under a passage agreement that did not grant use rights.

One witness reportedly says:

> the forest returned them until they understood whose path they were on.

The legal record itself focuses on:

- permission;
- use;
- compensation.

Later storytellers treat the phrase as literal supernatural evidence.

### Why this is strong

The creature may emerge from **legal metaphor becoming folklore**.

Or the metaphor may already refer to an older story.

The direction of borrowing is unknown.

---

## Physical / archival evidence

Evidence includes:

- boundary markers;
- old path maps;
- repeated accounts of moved / missing markers;
- sites with unusually high disorientation reports.

Some marker movement is clearly human.

That does not resolve every case.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Survey Loop

A multi-community survey / inspection party attempted to mark a disputed route.

Accounts agree that:

- they traveled for hours;
- believed they were making progress;
- returned to their own previous camp / marker from an unexpected direction.

Disagreement:

- poor navigation;
- local guide sabotage;
- bad map;
- deliberate political theater;
- Forest Owner.

The embarrassing part:

> several members were experienced path workers.

---

## Local version

LW-2 interior users:

- may not imagine a body at all.

LW-1 edge community:

- uses the story in concession / rights arguments.

CZ-2 forest-pass community:

- merges some elements with route-animal traditions.

---

## Child / popular version

Outsider versions become much more literal:

- tall forest figure;
- antlers;
- bark skin;
- glowing eyes.

Longwood locals may consider these silly.

Children inside Longwood may still tell literal forms.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Strongest:

- path confusion;
- local enforcement;
- deliberate marker movement;
- acoustics;
- terrain repetition;
- legal metaphor personified over time.

---

## Unresolved remainder

Some independent accounts describe:

- impossible route loops;
- markers encountered in wrong order;
- multiple parties returning to the same restricted site.

The evidence is anecdotal.

But geographically clustered.

---

## Author truth range

**E / F / possible G**

Working preference:

> primarily cultural / jurisdictional memory with a genuinely unresolved navigational remainder.

---

# 5. The Weather Animal

## Evidence character

**Cryptid / ecological / physical.**

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

A High Roads toll / route record notes a closure after:

- shepherds / travelers reported an unfamiliar low-bodied animal at an unusual elevation;
- abrupt weather followed.

The record treats the animal as an ordinary but unidentified local warning.

It does not call it mythical.

---

## Physical evidence

### The old skin / bone

HR-C or another route center preserves a fragment attributed to the creature.

Possibilities:

- hide;
- jaw;
- paw / foot bones.

Problems:

- provenance incomplete;
- repairs / preservation altered it;
- scholars disagree whether parts belong to one animal.

This should be a classic **museum argument object**.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Empty Pass

A convoy reportedly delayed because several pastoral households said the animal had been seen unusually low.

A competing convoy continued.

A major weather / slide event then blocked the pass and caused losses.

### Political afterlife

The event is used by:

- herders to defend local knowledge;
- route authorities to justify precaution;
- merchants to complain about superstition being turned into closure policy.

This gives sightings economic stakes.

---

## Local version

M-2 pastoralists:

> it knows before we do.

HR-3 residents:

- interpret elevation of sighting.

HR-C scholars:

- classify reports.

Port:

- calls it a "mountain dragon" or other exaggerated category.

---

## Child / popular version

Usually larger, stranger, more dangerous than adult testimony.

May be said to:

- carry storms;
- eat wind;
- sleep under snow.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Most plausible:

- rare highland mammal;
- relict species;
- unusual behavior correlated with pressure / weather;
- several animals conflated.

---

## Unresolved remainder

The preserved anatomical fragment does not fit comfortably into one commonly proposed animal category.

That may be because:

- it is damaged;
- reconstructed badly;
- from a rare / extinct species.

No supernatural conclusion needed.

---

## Author truth range

**A / B / C / D**

Working preference:

> real rare / relict animal tradition, with folklore exaggeration.

---

# 6. The Charter Beast

## Evidence character

**Legal / iconographic / architectural.**

---

## Earliest surviving evidence

Unlike most families, the oldest evidence is probably **not a story**.

It is:

- creature imagery on old water / boundary / grant records.

Several early examples predate current city-state governments.

The images share:

- a few stable anchors;
- many local differences.

The creature may have been:

- divine symbol;
- legal emblem;
- animal;
- composite sign.

No one knows.

---

## Physical / archival evidence

Strong corpus:

- charter stones;
- seals;
- canal markers;
- legal copies;
- merchant guarantees.

This makes it the best-documented "creature" in the world while providing almost no evidence that it ever lived.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Moved Stone

During an old water-right dispute, a boundary / canal marker was allegedly moved.

When recovered / found:

- one side bore fresh scratches resembling part of the beast symbol.

Interpretations:

- fraud;
- warning by local users;
- natural damage;
- later embellishment.

The legal decision did **not** rely on supernatural evidence.

Later folklore does.

---

## Local versions

OC-U:

- lawful division / authority.

OC-D:

- witness against stolen water.

OC-C:

- remembers agreements / guarantees.

CZ-3:

- may merge with moving-light water folklore.

---

## Child / popular version

Classic:

> move the marker and it follows you home.

Body becomes much more animal-like than legal art.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Strongest:

- old legal / sacred emblem;
- composite of dangerous water / dryland animals;
- later personification.

---

## Unresolved remainder

Very early examples in separate city archives show unexpectedly similar anchors even where direct copying is difficult to document.

Possible explanation:

- older shared institution predating current states.

This may matter historically more than whether a beast existed.

---

## Author truth range

**E / F / symbolic; possible A/D animal contribution**

Working preference:

> not a literal species, but an old symbol carrying several layers of institutional memory.

---

# 7. The Road Hound

## Evidence character

**Distributed oral / route / witness tradition.**

This is the hardest family to localize because its strength lies in spread.

---

## Earliest surviving account — candidate

The oldest plausible written evidence is a travel / hospice / route journal describing:

- a dark dog;
- appearing alone;
- following a traveler for part of a dangerous route;
- disappearing before settlement.

The writer does not call it supernatural.

Later traditions claim the account as theirs.

---

## Physical evidence

No accepted body / bone / hide.

That absence matters.

Alleged evidence includes:

- track casts / sketches;
- old collar with no known owner;
- repeated route-side dog burials.

None clearly belong to the legend.

This is a **witness tradition**, not specimen tradition.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Same Dog

Two route accounts separated by many years describe a dog with the same unusual:

- damaged ear / tail;
- white mark;
- gait

at the same isolated crossing.

Later compilers treat this as proof of one impossible long-lived animal.

Problems:

- later writer may have known the earlier account;
- copying / oral influence;
- ordinary similar-looking dogs.

The case remains famous because provenance is uncertain.

---

## Local versions

M-1 repair network:

- route-memory / camp companion.

M-2:

- crossing / weather warning.

High Roads caravan:

- stay on route.

North Coast-derived Port families:

- linked to missing sailors / travelers.

Port children:

- brings lost people home.

---

## Child / popular version

Often friendlier than adult route traditions.

This inversion is useful.

Port child:

> follow the dark dog home.

M-1 young-worker story:

> never follow it if it walks behind you.

Contradictions are expected.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Strongest:

- working / guardian dogs;
- feral dogs;
- scavengers following routes;
- story contamination;
- traveler expectation.

Folklorists argue the stable anchors are cultural, not zoological.

---

## Unresolved remainder

Across widely separated communities, four elements remain unusually persistent:

- solitary;
- silent;
- route / threshold appearance;
- disappears before / at boundary.

This could simply be what makes a story recognizable.

Or not.

---

## Author truth range

**A / D / F / possible G**

Working preference:

> leave among the world's strongest unresolved legends.

Do **not** solve it.

---

# 8. The Estuary Captive

## Evidence character

**Official / legal / biological / ethical.**

This is the most documentable single legendary incident.

---

## Earliest surviving account

The event itself is preserved through a **cluster of Port records**, not one narrative.

Possible surviving entries:

- fishermen's report / catch declaration;
- damaged-net compensation;
- custody order;
- food / water expense;
- examination notes;
- later legal memorandum.

No one document gives the whole story.

That is stronger than a perfect chronicle.

---

## Physical / archival evidence

There is no surviving body.

Possible surviving object:

- damaged section of unusually reinforced net traditionally associated with the capture;
- restraint / holding-room fixture;
- copied sketch.

The object itself proves only:

> something was caught / held.

Not what.

---

## Famous disputed incident — The Return

Accounts agree that the captive eventually:

- escaped;
- was released;
- or was allowed into the water under supervision.

Then disappeared.

The dispute is over what happened immediately before.

Some records / family accounts say:

- it remained underwater far longer than a human could comfortably manage;
- resurfaced farther away;
- returned once before leaving.

Others omit this entirely.

This is the strongest biological mystery.

---

## Harbor-family version

The moral center:

> **Port hurt something because it did not know what category to put it in.**

Creature identity is secondary.

This version survives through P-1 / D-1 harbor-family memory.

---

## Legal version

Later jurists care about:

- personhood;
- custody;
- property;
- foreigner status;
- jurisdiction.

The case becomes an old embarrassment because law had no safe category.

---

## Naturalist version

Competing hypotheses:

- human;
- marine mammal;
- unusually adapted diver / swimmer;
- hoax;
- several incidents merged;
- unknown species.

Descriptions conflict enough to support all of them badly.

---

## Child / Three Moons version

The captive becomes:

- puppet;
- mask;
- comic figure;
- swimming trickster.

A child can know this cheerful figure before learning the historical story involved detention / cruelty.

That cultural disjunction is valuable.

---

## Scholarly explanation

Most conservative:

> badly recorded human / marine-animal incident later merged into one story.

This explains:

- contradictory anatomy;
- communication failure;
- custody confusion.

It does not explain every record neatly.

---

## Unresolved remainder

The most stubborn detail should be **one narrow physiological claim**, not a list of magical powers.

Working candidate:

> several independent early sources imply an underwater duration / return behavior that does not fit the ordinary human explanation.

The timing is imprecise.

No definitive proof.

---

## Author truth range

**A / C / D / human case / possible G**

Working preference:

> deliberately leave genuinely unresolved.

The ethical truth of the story should matter more than zoological truth.

---

# 9. Evidence distribution rule

Do not give every legend the same evidence type.

| Family | Main evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Knocking Below | work / accident records |
| Great Mouth | captain logs + bone |
| Marsh Lure | flood geography + witness reports |
| Forest Owner | legal / path disputes |
| Weather Animal | specimen + route records |
| Charter Beast | legal iconography |
| Road Hound | distributed witnesses |
| Estuary Captive | official custody records |

This unevenness makes the world more believable.

---

# 10. Belief should track evidence differently

## Most scholars likely accept a real core

- Great Mouth;
- Weather Animal.

## Scholars accept phenomenon / cultural core but not creature

- Marsh Lure;
- Knocking Below;
- Charter Beast;
- Forest Owner.

## Scholars remain unusually divided

- Road Hound;
- Estuary Captive.

That does not mean scholars are the final authority.

Locals may have better evidence for some questions and worse for others.

---

# 11. Famous evidence should acquire institutional homes

Future site work should decide where important objects / records live.

Possible homes:

- Stone Hills worker / guild archive;
- NC-3 harbor record house;
- HR-C natural-history / route collection;
- OC-U / OC-D water archive;
- Port legal archive / museum;
- family-held object;
- religious collection.

Avoid putting every mysterious object in Port.

Port can copy / catalogue things without owning them.

---

# 12. Story-use rule

Do not turn these dossiers into exposition scenes.

Evidence should enter story through ordinary uses:

- worker refuses a shift;
- child buys a festival mask;
- clerk cites an old case;
- traveler argues over a road story;
- canal worker points to a scratched stone;
- naturalist complains that the famous illustration is wrong.

The world should **already know these stories**.

The reader learns by encountering their afterlife.

---

# 13. Next step

The next useful myth pass is now:

> **local naming + transmission history**, but only for the first 3–4 legends most likely to appear near the main story.

Priority:

1. Estuary Captive — Port / Three Moons relevance;
2. Road Hound — Wurdren / mobile-route relevance;
3. Forest Owner — Longwood counter-domino relevance;
4. Knocking Below — Stone Hills current-pressure relevance.

For each:

- derive 2–4 local names from existing language / naming topology;
- identify which name Port scholars standardized;
- identify which local community rejects that name;
- write one short in-world fragment from:
  - record;
  - child rhyme;
  - worker saying;
  - scholarly note.

Do not globally name all eight yet.
