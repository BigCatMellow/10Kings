# Old Cities Functional Governance Test

## Status

**Provisional structural test.**

This page tests how Old Cities political plurality functions without collapsing the region into either isolated city-states or a single southern union.

It does **not** establish:

- a fixed roster of city-states;
- exact city constitutions;
- one regional league;
- one shared court;
- one common tax system;
- named merchant dynasties;
- exact client territories;
- a permanent Old Cities union.

The working question is:

> **Which functions belong to individual cities and their hinterlands, which cross city boundaries, and which require temporary or durable coordination among rivals?**

---

# 1. Existing constraints

The Old Cities already contains or historically contains:

- city-states;
- estate regions;
- port towns;
- irrigation leagues;
- noble houses;
- merchant dynasties;
- rural client territories;
- competing civic identities;
- shared or cross-border water systems;
- temporary alliances;
- repeated betrayal and realignment.

The current world already establishes that a durable regional union would materially change the balance of power.

Therefore the default assumption should remain:

> **coordination does not imply unification.**

---

# 2. The basic political geometry

Old Cities authority is likely to be layered across several distinct spaces.

## Civic core

The city itself may control:

- walls and gates;
- markets;
- courts;
- taxation;
- public works;
- guards;
- civic offices;
- diplomatic representation.

## Hinterland / client territory

The city's practical reach may extend into:

- farms;
- orchards;
- estates;
- villages;
- roads;
- ports;
- waterworks.

That reach need not be uniform.

A city can be strong at the gate and weak in a distant estate district.

## Estate and merchant power

Large landholders and merchant houses can control:

- credit;
- storage;
- ships;
- workshops;
- labor;
- patronage;
- rural clients.

They can be politically dominant without being identical to the city government.

## Functional infrastructure

Water systems, ports, roads, and trade corridors can cross political boundaries.

These create institutions that do not fit inside one civic hierarchy.

## Inter-city coordination

Rival cities may create:

- bilateral agreements;
- water compacts;
- defense leagues;
- trade arrangements;
- arbitration forums;
- temporary blocs.

Those can be powerful without becoming a sovereign regional government.

---

# 3. Minimum functions by scale

## Neighborhood / village

Needs ordinary capacity for:

- local water distribution;
- market life;
- maintenance;
- local disputes;
- mutual aid.

## Estate / rural district

Needs some authority over:

- tenancy or labor;
- land use;
- irrigation branches;
- local storage;
- roads;
- rents or obligations.

Exact legal status varies by city and district.

## City-state / polity

Needs meaningful capacity for:

- taxation;
- public order;
- major infrastructure;
- serious law;
- military force;
- diplomacy;
- market regulation;
- urban water;
- some control of its hinterland.

## Cross-border water / infrastructure system

Needs:

- maintenance;
- flow information;
- access;
- scheduling;
- emergency repair;
- dispute mechanisms.

The existing irrigation test already establishes this layer.

## Temporary or durable inter-city body

May be required for:

- common defense;
- piracy or route security;
- emergency water coordination;
- joint infrastructure;
- limited trade rules;
- collective bargaining with an outside power.

Its authority should remain tied to the function that created it unless later history expands it.

---

# 4. Test 1 — drought across a shared water system

## Condition

Rainfall drops.

An upstream city or district controls a headwork needed downstream.

## Existing result

The [Old Cities Shared Irrigation Test](Old-Cities-Shared-Irrigation-Test.md) already demonstrates that:

- the political map and water map can diverge;
- users on both sides need maintenance and information;
- interdependence can stabilize cooperation;
- the same dependency can become coercive leverage.

## Governance implication

A city cannot govern shared water solely through its ordinary territorial law.

The system requires:

- cross-border access;
- technical communication;
- records;
- dispute procedure;
- some enforceable expectations.

## Result

**PASS for city sovereignty plus functional cross-border authority.**

Water does not require political union.

It does require institutions that survive rivalry.

---

# 5. Test 2 — canal sabotage accusation

## Condition

A gate fails or a channel is damaged.

Downstream users accuse the upstream city of deliberate sabotage.

The upstream city claims:

- neglect;
- flood damage;
- private action;
- lack of labor;
- false accusation.

## What the system needs

The dispute cannot be resolved credibly by propaganda alone.

Useful evidence may include:

- maintenance records;
- worker testimony;
- flow records;
- physical inspection;
- prior warnings;
- messenger logs.

## Result

**PASS for technical institutions with political importance.**

Canal workers, record keepers, and water associations can matter diplomatically because they possess evidence rulers need.

This gives non-elite institutions leverage without making them sovereign.

---

# 6. Test 3 — rural client territory resists city demands

## Condition

A city raises:

- taxes;
- grain levies;
- troop obligations;
- labor requirements;
- water fees.

A rural district or estate coalition refuses.

## Political question

Does the city rule the countryside directly?

Possibilities can vary:

- direct officials;
- local notables;
- estate intermediaries;
- chartered villages;
- tributary arrangements.

The system should not assume one answer everywhere.

## Result

**PASS for uneven hinterland control.**

A city-state can be a real state without exercising identical authority across every village and estate.

This creates internal politics between civic elites and rural power holders.

---

# 7. Test 4 — merchant dynasty becomes too powerful

## Condition

A merchant house controls:

- major credit;
- warehouses;
- shipping;
- political patronage;
- public loans.

It becomes indispensable to the city.

## Risk

Commercial power can begin to distort:

- elections or appointments where present;
- court outcomes;
- tax policy;
- foreign policy;
- infrastructure spending.

## Public response

Possible checks include:

- rival merchant houses;
- civic offices;
- guilds;
- popular assemblies where locally supported;
- noble families;
- religious institutions;
- debt restructuring;
- outside lenders.

## Result

**PASS for private power inside civic politics, not outside it.**

Merchant dynasties can dominate periods of city history without automatically becoming hereditary sovereigns.

The exact balance can differ sharply city to city.

---

# 8. Test 5 — port or road blockade

## Condition

One city attempts to pressure a rival by blocking:

- a road;
- harbor access;
- canal passage;
- a market route.

## Immediate effects

The target city may reroute trade.

But secondary actors also lose:

- neutral merchants;
- rural producers;
- neighboring polities;
- shared water users;
- creditors.

## Wider politics

A local blockade can therefore trigger:

- arbitration;
- retaliation;
- pressure from third cities;
- merchant opposition;
- Convergence claims if protected passage is implicated.

## Result

**PASS for external constraints on city sovereignty.**

A city can control infrastructure inside its jurisdiction while still being constrained by agreements and dependencies outside it.

---

# 9. Test 6 — refugee or labor movement between rivals

## Condition

War, drought, or economic collapse drives people from one Old Cities polity into another.

## Receiving-city problem

The destination gains labor but faces pressure on:

- housing;
- water;
- wages;
- charity;
- policing;
- citizenship or residence rules.

## Political complication

Merchant and estate interests may want labor.

Existing workers may resist wage pressure.

Religious or kin networks may provide aid.

The source city may demand return of debtors, soldiers, or political opponents.

## Result

**PASS for city-specific citizenship layered with trans-city social networks.**

Regional culture does not erase political membership.

Migration creates durable mixed communities and legal conflicts without requiring unified Old Cities citizenship.

---

# 10. Test 7 — temporary defense league

## Condition

An outside threat or powerful neighboring bloc causes several cities to coordinate defense.

## Necessary functions

A league may need:

- troop contributions;
- money;
- command rules;
- supply;
- intelligence;
- arbitration;
- rules for leaving.

## The danger

The largest contributor may try to turn emergency coordination into permanent dominance.

Smaller cities may prefer the threat to a new hegemon.

## Result

**PASS for issue-specific inter-city institutions with built-in suspicion of consolidation.**

The Old Cities can create strong leagues.

The existence of a league does not mean a regional state has formed.

This also explains why city-state coordination in current events can look threatening to outsiders and rivals.

---

# 11. Test 8 — Convergence dispute between two Old Cities polities

## Condition

Two rival cities dispute:

- a border;
- protected passage;
- a water agreement;
- a port claim;
- seizure of merchants.

## Representation

Each city may speak for itself.

A league may speak only if the issue falls within its delegated authority.

Water associations, merchant houses, or estates may provide evidence without possessing diplomatic standing.

## Result

**PASS for plural Convergence representation.**

The Convergence should treat actual political actors as parties.

"The Old Cities" is not one signatory merely because outsiders use one regional name.

---

# 12. Functional map that survives all eight tests

| Level | Functions strongly supported | Functions that should not automatically be assigned here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neighborhood / village | local maintenance, ordinary water distribution, local disputes | diplomacy, city-wide taxation |
| Estate / rural district | land, labor, branch irrigation, local storage, obligations | independent foreign policy by default |
| City-state / polity | taxation, public order, major works, military force, diplomacy, market regulation | unilateral control of every shared water or trade system |
| Cross-border infrastructure body | shared water, maintenance, technical records, access, emergency coordination | general government |
| Inter-city league / bloc | delegated defense, trade, infrastructure or bargaining functions | permanent sovereignty unless separately established |
| Convergence | cross-border obligations, protected passage, recognized claims, arbitration | ordinary Old Cities civic law |

This is a functional distribution, not a regional constitution.

---

# 13. Strong structural conclusion

The Old Cities currently works best as:

> **a competitive city-state system with uneven hinterlands, powerful private interests, and functional compacts that cross political borders without erasing civic sovereignty.**

The tests strongly support:

1. real city-state sovereignty and strong civic identities;
2. uneven control over estates, villages, and rural client territories;
3. cross-border water and infrastructure institutions that survive political rivalry;
4. merchant and noble power operating inside civic systems rather than replacing them by default;
5. temporary or durable leagues whose authority is limited to delegated functions;
6. strong reasons for cooperation without a default path toward unification;
7. city-specific citizenship or political membership despite a shared regional cultural sphere;
8. direct Convergence participation by actual cities or other recognized polities.

A permanent Old Cities union remains a major political change, not the default endpoint of coordination.

---

# 14. Political consequences

## Rivalry produces institutions as well as wars

Repeated competition can create:

- diplomatic customs;
- neutral meeting places;
- shared measurements or translations;
- water agreements;
- hostage or guarantee practices;
- arbitration habits.

Rivalry therefore makes the region more institutionally dense, not merely violent.

## Civic politics can be externally sophisticated and internally unequal

A city may be skilled at diplomacy while its rural clients have little voice.

A merchant family may finance public monuments while exploiting debtors.

A water compact may preserve peace while distributing shortages unfairly.

This fits the existing theme of public culture and private power.

## Cooperation can frighten outsiders

Because permanent union would be strategically consequential, even ordinary coordination can be misread as consolidation.

That makes current city-state coordination politically volatile without requiring anyone to be secretly planning conquest.

# 15. Writing the Sequel pressures

Possible later tests:

- a temporary defense league survives as a commercial bloc;
- an old water tribunal becomes a prestigious diplomatic forum;
- a merchant bailout creates permanent civic oversight of public debt;
- a rural tax rebellion wins chartered privileges;
- a refugee district becomes a politically distinct urban constituency;
- a canal-maintenance agreement is later misremembered as part of the Convergence;
- a dominant city repeatedly fails to convert league leadership into sovereignty.

These are **development prompts**, not current canon.

---

# 16. Breath scene seeds

Keep these non-canon unless later promoted:

- two rival cities agree on canal maintenance and argue over whose seal appears first on the notice;
- a rural village pays taxes to a city whose citizens barely know where it is;
- a merchant family funds a public fountain and is accused of buying gratitude with public debt;
- a league meeting spends longer arguing over troop accounting than strategy;
- refugees from one city recreate a neighborhood festival in its rival;
- everyone says a temporary alliance is temporary, including the clerks who have kept its records for forty years.

---

# 17. Still open

Do **not** settle without further testing:

- exact city-state roster;
- exact city constitutions;
- borders and hinterlands;
- citizenship rules;
- merchant and noble political privileges;
- rural representation;
- exact water institutions;
- exact port relationships;
- exact league memberships;
- standing versus temporary military coordination;
- exact Convergence signatories or delegates;
- whether any current bloc is actually moving toward durable union.

Those questions should now be answered against a tested plural city-state system rather than by assuming either isolation or inevitable federation.
