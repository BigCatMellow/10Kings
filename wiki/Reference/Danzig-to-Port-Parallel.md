## What the legacy notes say

The source explicitly labels the section:

> **Port: Internal Dynamics**  
> *Based on the Free City of Danzig parallel*

The following ideas come from that legacy source and should therefore be treated as imported design material unless and until the current Port owner adopts them.

### Population and identity

The notes describe Port as having:

- a mixed population drawn from many regions;
- refugees, stateless people, unwanted migrants, and people seeking a new start;
- locally born people whose identity is primarily **"from Port"** rather than inherited from another region;
- sharp class divisions, including an especially vulnerable orphan population.

The older note gives a specific claim that more than 80% identify as being from Port first. That number is **legacy material, not current locked canon**.

### Economic trap

The notes describe Port as having very little agricultural territory and therefore being highly dependent on imported necessities.

That creates:

- higher prices;
- chronic working-class insecurity;
- dependence on unstable trade volume;
- unemployment;
- resentment that outsiders profit from the harbor;
- incentives for speculative and illegal economic activity.

### Industries

The legacy material gives Port several important industries:

- shipbuilding and ship repair;
- timber processing;
- food processing;
- brewing and confectionery;
- harbor-support services.

It also imagines a decline in shipyard employment following demilitarization and lean economic periods.

### Smuggling

The notes treat smuggling as a structural consequence of Port's position rather than simply a criminal subculture.

Its location, trade restrictions, poverty, and access to multiple markets make contraband networks economically attractive.

The core idea is:

**smuggling can be a survival economy.**

### Sovereignty problem

The legacy notes describe citizens who govern many internal matters while larger powers constrain the city's external affairs.

The resulting identity is:

- neither comfortably part of another kingdom;
- nor fully sovereign;
- dependent on treaties and outside protection;
- vulnerable to decisions made elsewhere.

This is one of the clearest places where the Danzig inspiration appears.

### Internal factions

The imported note proposes:

- **Harbor Masters' Alliance** — old dock families seeking stability;
- **Workers' Collectives** — dockworkers and seasonal laborers seeking better conditions;
- **Merchant Factors** — prosperous intermediaries between Port and outside trade;
- **The Displaced** — recent arrivals with little political voice;
- **Festival Council** — a weak or constrained nominal government.

These remain legacy proposals unless adopted by the current Port page.

### Political mood

The source does **not** imagine Port as a population simply waiting for revolution.

Instead it emphasizes:

- fear of losing already-fragile livelihoods;
- resignation;
- survival pragmatism;
- political caution;
- dependence on whatever keeps ships arriving.

That distinction is important to the original concept.

## Historical Danzig parallels

The real Free City of Danzig provides useful structural precedents for several of those ideas:

| Historical Danzig feature | Possible Port design use |
| --- | --- |
| Local government under an international guarantee | Port can govern itself while neutrality depends on outside recognition |
| Polish control of foreign relations | Another institution or coalition could constrain Port's external diplomacy |
| Shared/customs arrangements | Trade law can bind Port economically to neighbors without making it their territory |
| Guaranteed outside access to docks and waterways | Other powers may possess treaty rights inside Port |
| Joint port administration | Port infrastructure can be governed through negotiated institutions rather than simple sovereignty |
| League High Commissioner and dispute process | A neutral mediator or treaty mechanism could exist when neighboring powers clash over Port |
| Strategic importance to surrounding states | Port's neutrality can survive because no major power wants a rival to control it |

These are **design parallels**, not declarations that Two Sons must reproduce the historical arrangement.

## Combined design role with the ECW analogue

Port's design now uses **Danzig and ECW for different layers of the same city**.

The Danzig material primarily informs:

- sovereignty;
- neutrality;
- outside guarantees;
- treaty access;
- customs and harbor politics;
- economic dependence;
- local identity under external pressure.

The [ECW / competitive-kingdom analogue](Competitive-Kingdom-Dynamics.md) primarily informs:

- Port's outsider cultural identity;
- experimentation;
- intense local loyalty;
- talent development;
- cultural borrowing by larger rivals;
- the gap between Port's formal size and its wider influence.

The two references should not be collapsed into one analogy.

A useful shorthand is:

**Danzig explains why Port is geopolitically strange. ECW explains why Port is culturally strange.**

Together they create a city that larger states cannot ignore politically and cannot stop learning from culturally.

## Questions this reference raises for Port

These are intentionally questions rather than new canon:

- Who guarantees Port's neutrality?
- Who conducts or limits its foreign relations?
- Who collects customs duties?
- Does Port keep all harbor revenue?
- Do neighboring kingdoms possess treaty rights inside the harbor?
- Is there a joint harbor authority?
- Who arbitrates disputes involving Port?
- What rights do Port citizens have outside the city?
- How does someone become a Port citizen?
- Which institutions actually control food imports?
- What happens if a major kingdom tries to bypass Port?
- What happens if one outside power gains too much influence there?

Those answers should be settled in the current owner pages rather than silently inherited from Danzig.
