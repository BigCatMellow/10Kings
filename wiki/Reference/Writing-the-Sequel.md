# Writing the Sequel

## Status

**Worldbuilding method — not setting canon.**

Source inspiration is currently stored inside [DragonSword — Middle Worldbuilding](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/DragonSword/blob/main/worldbuilding/MIDDLE-WORLDBUILDING.md), but **Writing the Sequel is a separate concept**. It is grouped there only because of the source file's formatting.

## Core idea

Treat a worldbuilding premise as something people have already lived with.

Do not stop at:

> What is true about this world?

Keep going:

> What happened because it was true?

A useful chain is:

```text
premise
→ consequence
→ adaptation
→ normalization
→ culture
→ inherited present
```

The interesting material is often not the original premise. It is what came afterward.

## Why this matters for 10Kings

10Kings is being built as a world, not merely as a backdrop for one immediate story.

So **Writing the Sequel is not a shortcut around detail**.

It is a way to generate the details that should exist because the world has had time to react to itself.

If a condition has existed for decades or centuries, people should have:

- adapted to it;
- built institutions around it;
- exploited it;
- regulated it;
- ritualized it;
- misunderstood it;
- inherited simplified stories about it;
- developed workarounds;
- turned emergency measures into ordinary rules;
- forgotten why some customs began.

## Basic example

**Premise:**

> A mountain route is dangerous every winter.

Do not stop there.

Ask what happened afterward:

```text
winter route danger
→ repeated isolation
→ settlements store emergency reserves
→ merchants price seasonal risk
→ local authorities regulate reserve access
→ inns and monasteries develop traveler obligations
→ winter couriers become specialist work
→ families plan marriages, debts, and travel around closure dates
→ sayings, festivals, food preservation, architecture, and social expectations form
```

By the present day, people may no longer think of these things as adaptations.

They are simply "how things are done."

## The sequel of history

This is especially useful for major historical events.

Do not stop at:

> A war happened.

Ask:

- what roads were rerouted afterward?
- which border became formal?
- which families gained land?
- which profession expanded?
- which tax was introduced temporarily and never repealed?
- which monument became politically loaded?
- which food became associated with hardship?
- which migration changed a dialect?
- which legal exception still exists?
- which official history differs from family memory?

A historical event becomes convincing when its **aftermath survives into ordinary life**.

## The sequel of institutions

Do not stop at:

> A guild controls a profession.

Ask what decades of that control produced.

Possibilities:

- apprenticeship dynasties;
- black-market work;
- family strategies for getting children admitted;
- neighborhoods built around guild halls;
- rival unofficial certifications;
- standardized tools;
- occupational surnames;
- resentment from excluded workers;
- guild charities;
- political blocs;
- old rules nobody remembers the original reason for.

## The sequel of ecology

Do not stop at:

> This region is dry.

Ask what generations of dryness caused.

That can produce:

- water law;
- inheritance rules;
- settlement placement;
- irrigation politics;
- architectural forms;
- preservation techniques;
- social status around wells or cisterns;
- drought rituals;
- migration patterns;
- insults and metaphors;
- political disputes over expansion;
- foods designed around fuel and water limits.

## The sequel of trade

Do not stop at:

> Two regions trade heavily.

Ask what long-term trade creates.

It may produce:

- mixed families;
- loanwords;
- diaspora neighborhoods;
- shared dishes;
- seasonal migration;
- specialized brokers;
- treaty law;
- warehouse districts;
- cross-border criminal networks;
- religious borrowing;
- stereotypes;
- political constituencies that oppose war because their livelihood depends on the relationship.

## The sequel of crisis

Temporary responses are especially important.

A crisis may create:

```text
emergency
→ emergency rule
→ practical success
→ vested interest
→ permanent institution
→ tradition
```

Later generations may defend the institution without knowing that it began as an emergency measure.

That is often more useful than inventing a custom with no history.

## The sequel across generations

Use generational distance.

The same event can become:

- **living memory** — "I remember it."
- **inherited memory** — "My parents lived through it."
- **official history** — "We learned about it."
- **mythic shorthand** — "Everyone knows what that means."
- **misremembered tradition** — people retain the custom but not the cause.

This is where history turns into culture.

## Difference from Middle Worldbuilding

[Middle Worldbuilding](Middle-Worldbuilding.md) asks:

> How does the world actually function because it is this way?

**Writing the Sequel** asks:

> What accumulated after people lived with that reality for a long time?

They overlap, but they are not the same method.

Middle Worldbuilding is primarily about **systemic consequences and functioning**.

Writing the Sequel is primarily about **time, adaptation, inheritance, and accumulated aftermath**.

Used together:

```text
premise
→ MIDDLE WORLDBUILDING:
  how does this change systems and daily function?
→ WRITING THE SEQUEL:
  what accumulates after generations live with those systems?
→ concrete present-day world
```

## Relationship to Worldbuilding Breath

[Worldbuilding Breath](Worldbuilding-Breath.md) is a third, separate concern.

- **Middle Worldbuilding** builds functional systems.
- **Writing the Sequel** builds accumulated history and normalization.
- **Worldbuilding Breath** controls how incompletely and naturally those things are revealed in prose.

## Working questions

When using Writing the Sequel, ask:

1. How long has this condition existed?
2. What did the first generation do about it?
3. What solution became standard?
4. Who gained power or wealth from that solution?
5. What secondary problems did the solution create?
6. What later institution formed around it?
7. What do people now consider normal that originally was not?
8. What part of the original reason has been forgotten?
9. What does the younger generation misunderstand about it?
10. What physical, legal, culinary, linguistic, religious, or social residue remains today?

The point is not merely to imagine history.

It is to make the **present world look like it has already had a past**.
