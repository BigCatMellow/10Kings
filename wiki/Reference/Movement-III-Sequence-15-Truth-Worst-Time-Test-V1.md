## Working ally role

**Provisional recurring character function — no final name yet.**

The ally is a Port-based guild / professional coordinator who believes:

- permanent cross-guild communication in Port is useful;
- reciprocal credentials / clerical services / arbitration are good;
- Port needs more political agency;
- regional branches should retain substantial authority;
- Port coordination should not quietly become a chain of command.

They are therefore a **federal coordinator**, not a full Port centralizer.

They have worked with the Villain on legitimate reform.

They do **not** know:

- that he is cultivating war;
- the whole pressure board;
- the full conspiracy architecture.

## Working true record

A real protected contract / priority annex includes:

- the currently active protected order;
- a **first-call extension clause** allowing the buyer to claim additional inspected capacity if specified security conditions are triggered;
- a longer option horizon than the Stone Hills labor compromise currently assumes;
- substantial cancellation / priority consequences.

The owners' current statements can still be technically true:

> the currently committed share is what they reported.

The record changes the political meaning:

> **"temporary current share" may sit inside a much larger contingent future claim.**

This is not a forgery.

# Prose test

The guild coordinator had spent six years arguing that Port needed more permanent offices.

They had spent almost as long arguing that permanent offices should not be allowed to become government.

Most people found this distinction tiring.

The Villain did not.

That was one reason they still worked with him.

The other was that he got things done.

Not speeches.

Things.

A disputed credential recognized across three branches.

A copied standard corrected before it ruined a contract.

A mutual-aid payment moved through Port when the home office took too long.

A room found for a branch that could not afford a permanent hall.

The coordinator trusted work more than slogans.

That had been safe so far.

---

He asked them to meet in an ordinary office.

No guards.

No secret cellar.

Two clerks worked in the next room.

The door did not fully close.

That was normal.

The coordinator sat.

"You said Stone Hills."

He handed over a copied contract abstract.

They recognized the format.

"Where did you get this?"

"Does it matter before you read it?"

"Yes."

"Then read it suspiciously."

They did.

The first page was unsurprising.

Protected industrial work.

Inspection requirement.

Guaranteed purchase.

Confidential customer designation.

The second page changed it.

They read the clause twice.

Then a third time.

"First call."

"Yes."

"If the contingency is declared."

"Yes."

"This is not the current allocation."

"No."

"Then why are you showing me?"

He waited.

The coordinator disliked when he did that.

"Because the Stone Hills shops are negotiating on current allocation."

"Yes."

"They asked for the committed period."

"Yes."

"This clause is an option."

"Yes."

"An option is not a commitment."

"No."

The coordinator put the page down.

"So the owners were not lying."

"I did not say they were."

"Then say what you are saying."

He looked at the contract.

"They are asking workers to accept confidentiality because the current protected share can be independently checked."

"Reasonable."

"Maybe."

"Do not do that."

"Do what?"

"Use 'maybe' when you have already decided the answer."

He smiled slightly.

That irritated them more.

The coordinator tapped the clause.

"If the contingency never triggers, this means nothing."

"If it triggers?"

"The buyer gets first claim on additional inspected capacity under the stated terms."

"For how long?"

They read the dates.

Longer than the temporary review period in the Stone Hills proposal.

That mattered.

They hated that it mattered.

"Does the local branch have this?"

"I don't know."

"Does the reviewer?"

"Not yet. The review has not started."

"Does the owner intend to show it?"

"I don't know."

The coordinator looked up.

"You know exactly when their next meeting is."

"Yes."

There it was.

Timing.

They leaned back.

"You want this released."

"I want you to decide whether a branch should agree to bounded secrecy without knowing the category of future claim attached to the contract."

"That is a very careful sentence."

"I have been around clerks."

They did not smile.

"You could send it yourself."

"Yes."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because you believe branches should decide what coordination requires."

The answer landed exactly where he intended.

The coordinator knew that too.

"You are using my argument."

"It remains your argument."

"Convenient."

"Often."

They read the clause again.

---

The document was real.

That was the problem.

If it had been false, the choice would have been easier.

The guild coordinator could verify:

- format;
- marks;
- cross-reference;
- inspection schedule.

They could not independently prove every handwritten notation.

They did not need to.

The first-call clause was printed into the copied annex.

A Port office had handled the abstract because:

- inspection recognition;
- guarantee terms;
- cross-branch acceptance

ran through shared clerical channels.

That did not make the document public.

It did mean the document was not imaginary.

The coordinator said:

"If I send this, negotiations may collapse."

"Yes."

"You say that easily."

"No."

"Sounded easy."

He looked toward the half-open door.

A clerk passed outside carrying three ledgers.

"Do you believe they should accept the settlement without seeing it?"

The coordinator hated the question because it was the wrong question.

Or rather:

> too narrow a correct question.

"What else happens if they see it?"

"Ask them."

"That's your answer to everything when you want somebody else to own the consequence."

He looked back at them.

For the first time, no smile.

Good.

The coordinator continued.

"You built half the system that made this document reach Port."

"More than half would be flattering."

"You centralize information and then act surprised when it gives you leverage."

"Do you want the Port offices dismantled?"

"No."

"Then the leverage exists."

"That is not an answer."

"No."

They sat in silence.

The coordinator folded the abstract.

"I am not publishing the customer name."

"I did not ask you to."

"I am not sending the whole contract."

"Fine."

"I am sending the clause and enough verification that the branch knows it is real."

He nodded.

"Do not nod."

He stopped.

The coordinator stood.

"If this blows up the settlement—"

"It might."

"I know."

They looked at him.

"Do you?"

"Yes."

That answer was worse than evasion.

They left.

---

The Stone Hills branch received the copy before evening.

Not the public.

Not the whole town.

A branch contact.

Then:

- one shop representative;
- another;
- the worker representative from the hall.

By the time the next meeting began, enough people had seen it that secrecy was already impossible inside the room.

The worker representative put the copied clause on the table.

The production supervisor read it.

Their face did not change.

That was almost worse.

"You knew."

"Yes."

The room changed.

The supervisor held up one hand.

"The clause is not active."

The small-shop worker said:

"You knew."

"It is an option."

"You knew."

"Yes."

The worker representative said:

"Would the review group have seen it?"

"Underlying reviewers, yes."

"When?"

"When the review began."

"Before shops accepted the package?"

A pause.

"No."

There it was.

The guild contract clerk stepped in.

"The current committed share remains exactly what was represented."

The worker representative looked at them.

"And the future first-call?"

"Not current commitment."

"How much can it take?"

The clerk did not answer.

The small-shop worker laughed.

Not kindly.

The priority-line worker from the previous meeting took the paper.

"If it triggers, do we get more work?"

The small-shop worker turned.

"That's your question?"

"It's mine."

The room split immediately.

Not into:

- workers;
- owners.

Into different fears.

One group saw:

> hidden long-term strategic capture.

Another saw:

> job security.

Another saw:

> proof the temporary review was designed around too narrow a definition.

Another saw:

> a contingent clause being treated as an active conspiracy.

The supervisor said:

"This is exactly why customer contracts are confidential."

The worker representative answered:

"No. This is exactly why secrecy about names became secrecy about scope."

The supervisor hit the table.

"We agreed to show scope."

"Current scope."

"Because that is what exists."

"This exists."

"As an option."

"It exists."

Both statements were true.

That made the argument worse.

---

The temporary package did not collapse in one dramatic vote.

Nothing that useful happened.

Two shops said they would continue under the review terms.

One demanded the first-call clause be included in the review before accepting anything.

The neighboring small shop refused to sign onto the package until:

- future contingent capacity;
- renewal options

were also disclosed to the accepted reviewers.

A priority-line group objected to any action that might cause the buyer to cancel.

The safety inspector said none of this changed the hours problem.

Nobody listened enough.

The next day's work continued.

But less smoothly.

One ordinary line slowed when workers refused voluntary overtime.

A protected line continued.

Rumor outran the actual clause before noon.

By afternoon people were saying:

- the whole region had been promised away;
- foreign buyers owned the workshops;
- the contracts were permanent;
- the contracts were military;
- the contracts were all Council-controlled.

None of those statements followed from the page.

That did not stop them traveling.

The worker representative spent half the day saying:

> "That's not what it says."

This was not the position they had expected to occupy.

---

In Port, the guild coordinator received the first message back.

> **Real. Confirmed. Meeting delayed. Branch divided.**

They read it twice.

The Villain was not in the office.

They were glad.

Another note followed later.

> **Workers demanding contingent capacity disclosure. Owners requesting clearer protected-contract rules from territorial / guild authorities.**

That was not what the coordinator had intended.

They had intended:

> informed consent.

What they had helped create was:

> demand for formal rules.

Maybe that was better.

Maybe it was worse.

They did not know.

The coordinator opened their own copy of the clause.

The marks remained the same.

The truth had not changed.

Only its timing had.

That thought made them uncomfortable enough to close the file.

---

The Villain received a shorter report.

He read:

- package delayed;
- branch split;
- contingent priority now public inside the dispute;
- authorities being asked to define protected-contract categories more clearly.

He did not need more.

The operation had not produced:

- strike;
- riot;
- collapse.

Good.

Those would have been noisy.

It had produced something more useful:

> **a real dispute now demanded a formal answer to who could claim priority and under what conditions.**

He filed the report.

No celebration.

No second push.

The next move belonged to Stone Hills.

# V1 assessment

## Bounded Villain intervention

**STRONG PASS.**

He:

- does not forge;
- does not invent grievance;
- does not directly tell workers what to think.

He times access to a true record.

## Guild federalist ally

**STRONG PASS.**

The ally:

- has their own ideology;
- challenges the Villain;
- chooses the release;
- does not know the war objective;
- is not merely an operative.

This character is likely strong enough for a recurring packet.

## Truth content

**STRONG PASS.**

The clause does not prove current owner statements false.

It reveals:

> the "temporary current share" sits inside a future contingent priority option.

That genuinely changes the trust question.

## Worker agency

**STRONG PASS.**

Workers interpret the same truth differently.

Rumor exaggerates it.

The worker representative actively corrects false versions even while opposing the secrecy.

## Result

**PASS.**

The near-compromise becomes harder.

It does not collapse cartoonishly.

More importantly, Stone Hills actors now demand:

> **formal priority rules.**

That sets up Sequence 16.

## Main risks

1. the Villain / ally conversation is somewhat rhetorically polished;
2. the exact provenance of the copied annex may still need a more concrete access chain;
3. the ally's release could be construed as an unauthorized confidentiality breach, which may need consequences;
4. Sequence 16 in the spine is High Roads "Security rules," while Stone Hills formal priority rules are also now due. Need preserve braid order rather than force one immediately after another.

## Recommendation

Challenge:

- ally independence;
- document access / release consequences;
- whether Sequence 16 should remain High Roads next while Stone Hills formalization develops in parallel.

Do not erase the core intervention.
