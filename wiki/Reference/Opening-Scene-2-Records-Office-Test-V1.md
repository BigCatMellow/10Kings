# Opening Scene 2 — Records Office Voice Test V1

## Status

**Author-side prose experiment — not canon.**

Uses [Opening Scene 1 — Voice and Pacing Test V2](Opening-Scene-1-Voice-Test-V2.md) as the current voice baseline.

Purpose:

- test the Records Worker as a recurring character without a final name;
- test bureaucratic friction without turning it into exposition;
- show Wurdren's ordinary competence;
- demonstrate the first genuine benefit of cross-guild coordination;
- stop before the Council-linked guarantee intervention.

# Prose test

The shared records office was not where Wurdren remembered anything being.

That was becoming a theme.

The grain agent led them through the warehouse belt, across a lane where carts were being unloaded directly into temporary sheds, and into a stone building with three open doors and twice as many people trying to use them.

A board beside the entrance listed services.

Wurdren read the first four lines and stopped.

"Which one are we?"

The grain agent looked.

"Warehouse transfer."

"That sounds promising."

"It says recognized warehouse transfer."

"Less promising."

Inside, the room was long, bright, and loud in the way only a place devoted to paperwork could be. Not loud with shouting. Loud with questions repeated at increasing volume by people convinced the problem was that nobody had heard them the first time.

Clerks worked behind a waist-high counter. Shelves behind them held bound books, rolled documents, trays of seals, and wooden markers cut with symbols Wurdren did not recognize. A conversion table had been painted directly onto one wall. Someone had pasted a newer strip of figures over part of it.

The grain agent joined a queue.

Wurdren looked toward the open doorway.

Across the lane, under another awning, several guild notices had been pinned to a broad board.

He could not read them from here.

One had a red corner.

He found himself wondering what red meant now.

When he had last spent any time in Port, a red corner had meant dangerous work or urgent work, depending on which guild posted it. Sometimes both.

"You're leaning," the grain agent said.

"I'm standing."

"Toward the door."

"I can stand directionally."

"You can also leave if you want."

Wurdren looked at her.

She shrugged.

"You got us here."

It was not accusation.

That made it worse.

"I know."

The line moved.

He stayed.

When their turn came, the clerk behind the counter did not look up immediately. They finished writing three lines, sanded the page, shook it clean, closed the book, and only then held out a hand.

"Paper."

The grain agent gave over the warehouse contract.

The clerk read.

"Original."

She handed over another sheet.

"Reservation."

Another.

"Buyer's terms."

Another.

The clerk looked up.

"Warehouse refusal."

The grain agent paused.

"They refused us."

"I believe you."

"They told us to come here."

"I believe that too."

"Then why do you need—"

"Because you are asking me to move a contracted delivery from one recognized place to another. I need to know the first place released the obligation rather than you deciding you prefer the second."

"We don't prefer the second."

"Then this should be easy."

The grain agent looked at Wurdren.

He had learned, over many years, that there were moments when defending a companion was honorable and moments when it merely doubled the number of fools in the room.

This appeared to be the second kind.

"What do they have to give us?" he asked.

The clerk looked at him for the first time.

"A written refusal. Their mark. Date. Reason if they'll state one."

"Full capacity?"

"That will do."

"Then you can move us?"

"Then I can tell you whether the other warehouse is recognized under the reciprocal schedule."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

"What happens if it is?"

"Then the warehouse part of your problem is solved."

"And the rest?"

The clerk tapped the buyer's paper.

"The person standing behind this promise agreed to one custody chain. You are proposing another. I can tell them the new chain is recognized. I cannot make them trust it."

The grain agent rubbed her forehead.

"So we have two problems."

"You had several. I have reduced them."

Wurdren almost smiled.

The clerk saw it.

"Do not encourage me."

He did smile then.

Behind them, somebody in the queue sighed loudly enough to be heard in another polity.

Wurdren pushed away from the counter.

"I'll get the refusal."

The grain agent tightened her grip on the folio.

"I'm coming."

"No. If this line takes as long the second time, one of us should keep our place in the world."

The clerk had already reached for the next person's documents.

"Take the left door when you come back," they said.

The grain agent blinked.

"Why?"

"You've been entered."

"For what?"

"Warehouse transfer."

"We haven't transferred anything."

"That is why you are coming back."

Wurdren left before the conversation acquired another layer.

Outside, the red-cornered notice across the lane caught his eye again.

He could read part of it now.

Escort.

North—

A cart rolled between him and the board.

By the time it passed, he was already walking back toward the quay.

The warehouse runner was exactly where they had left him and looked disappointed to see Wurdren return.

"What now?"

"I need you to tell me in writing what you already told me in person."

The runner stared.

"No room."

"That part."

"I told you."

"I was impressed by the clarity."

"Then why—"

"The records office wants your mark on it."

The runner looked toward the line of boats.

Then toward Wurdren.

Then back toward the boats.

"I do not have time for this."

Wurdren nodded.

"Neither does the woman paying berth fees because you don't have room."

"That isn't my fault."

"No."

The runner's expression changed slightly.

Wurdren had found that people were often prepared for an argument and somewhat inconvenienced by agreement.

"It's not yours," Wurdren said. "It's also not hers. Put that on the paper if you like."

The runner took the offered sheet.

"I am not writing that."

"Then write 'full.'"

He did.

He pressed the warehouse mark beside it and handed it back.

Wurdren looked at the mark.

"Date."

The runner closed his eyes.

Wurdren waited.

The date was added.

"Anything else?"

"Your eternal gratitude."

"Go."

Wurdren went.

When he returned to the records office, the grain agent was no longer at the main counter. A clerk at the door pointed him left without asking a question.

The Records Worker sat at a narrower desk with the original papers spread in front of them.

Wurdren put the refusal beside the reservation.

They read it.

"Good."

"That's generous."

"It has a date."

"You set a low bar."

"I work with the public."

They opened a bound schedule and ran one finger down a column.

"South transfer is recognized for grain weights under the reciprocal agreement."

The grain agent leaned forward.

"So we're done."

"For storage recognition."

Her face fell.

The clerk continued.

"The warehouse can receive the grain and issue a receipt your buyer can verify. Your original guarantor still has to accept the custody change."

"Can they refuse?"

"Of course."

"Why would they?"

"Because their promise is the reason other people are willing to act as though your promise is worth money."

The clerk turned the buyer's sheet around and pointed to the relevant line.

"If I could change that for them, their guarantee would not be worth much."

The grain agent stared at the line.

Wurdren looked at the shelves behind the clerk.

Every book on them represented people trusting marks made by people they had never met.

He had spent most of his life thinking of paperwork as the thing that happened after real work.

The thought had never survived much contact with clerks.

"Who do we need?" he asked.

The clerk named a commercial house with an office nearer the inner transfer yards.

The grain agent made a small unhappy sound.

"You know them?" Wurdren asked.

"I know their fees."

The clerk began gathering the papers into a new order.

A second clerk leaned through the doorway.

"You're still here."

The Records Worker did not look up.

"So are you."

"Your sister sent word."

That got a glance.

"What word?"

"That she is eating without you."

"Tell her I support her decision."

The other clerk disappeared.

The Records Worker handed the papers back.

"Take this copy to South transfer. Take that one to the guarantor. Do not let either keep your only copy."

The grain agent tucked them away.

"Thank you."

The worker had already opened the next book.

"Thank me when someone accepts the grain."

Outside, the hiring board was still across the lane.

The red-cornered notice was visible again.

Wurdren read enough this time to understand that somebody wanted armed escort work north of Port.

Better pay than grain.

Probably more interesting.

The grain agent was beside him, sorting the newly ordered papers.

"Which way?" she asked.

Wurdren looked once more at the board.

Then toward the inner transfer yards.

"Guarantor," he said.

They went that way.

# Scene 2 test notes

## What works structurally

- Records Worker has competence and an independent life.
- terminology emerges through conflict rather than lecture.
- Wurdren's skill is patience / reading people / practical persistence.
- cross-guild reciprocal recognition solves a real problem.
- the unresolved guarantee naturally hands into Scene 3.
- the hiring-board temptation keeps the complete-person arc active without stopping the plot.

## Potential correction

The clerk currently has a fairly dry / sharp voice close to Wurdren's own humor.

Before final prose, differentiate them more strongly:

- Wurdren's humor can be social / deflective;
- Records Worker's humor should be exact / procedural / exhausted.

## Information-budget check

The scene introduces:

- reciprocal warehouse recognition;
- guarantor logic;
- shared records office.

It does **not** explain:

- Council;
- Villain;
- Port treaty government;
- broader financial system.

**PASS.**
