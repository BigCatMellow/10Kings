## Design rule

These are **legend families**, not a confirmed bestiary.

Each family has:

- a shared core;
- multiple cultural oicotypes;
- several plausible historical / natural roots;
- an unresolved remainder.

The author does not need to decide the final supernatural truth immediately.

# 1. The Knocking Below

## Core tradition

Miners / tunnel workers in several Stone Hills and Underpass communities tell of **something below the working face that knocks before danger**.

Shared anchors:

- heard rather than seen;
- repeated knocking / tapping;
- occurs before some collapses / bad-air events;
- experienced workers disagree about whether responding to it is superstition or prudence.

## Possible historical / natural core

Could combine:

- rock stress;
- timber supports shifting;
- dripping / water pressure;
- mine acoustics;
- worker warning codes;
- dead-worker memory.

Possibly no creature ever existed.

Or:

- one rare subterranean animal may have contributed to the sound tradition.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Deep mine crews

Treat the knocks as a **warning**.

Some crews stop work after a particular rhythm.

Younger workers may mock the custom until an older worker refuses to continue.

### Large industrial civic mines

Management / engineers tend to explain the sounds mechanically.

Workers may accuse owners of calling the legend foolish because:

> "the mine warns before the owner does."

This gives the story labor-political meaning.

### Great Weaver-influenced communities

May interpret the sound as:

- the mountain / deep earth expressing strain;
- not necessarily a creature with a body.

### Port scholars

May classify several unrelated underground traditions under one standardized "Knocker" category that miners find simplistic.

## Supernatural remainder

Some testimony describes:

- knocks traveling against the geology;
- the same rhythm heard in sealed workings;
- warnings occurring before dangers no known sound should precede.

No proof.

## Present status

**Living occupational folklore.**

# 2. The Ore-Spoiler

## Core tradition

Some mine districts tell of a being / presence that:

- makes rich-looking ore worthless;
- turns metal "sick";
- poisons a furnace;
- leaves workers ill.

Shared anchors:

- deceptive appearance;
- bitter / strange smell or smoke;
- association with greed / rushing extraction.

## Possible natural core

Strong possibilities:

- toxic mineral contamination;
- arsenic / sulfur-like hazards;
- poor ventilation;
- mistaken ore;
- processing failures.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Small craft / mine communities

The being is described as:

- mischievous;
- spiteful toward careless workers;
- attracted by boasting.

### Industrial reform circles

Interpret the legend as worker memory of:

- poisoned air;
- dangerous ores;
- owners forcing work despite known hazard.

### Old religious communities

May treat it as:

- a lesser earth spirit;
- punishment for taking more than agreed.

### Technical schools

Use the old name jokingly for a class of contaminated / misleading ore even while denying the creature.

## Supernatural remainder

Some old assay books / family accounts claim:

- ore composition changed between extraction and processing;
- tools / lamps behaved strangely;
- sickness occurred where later tests found no obvious toxin.

## Present status

**Half folklore, half technical slang.**

# 3. The Great Mouth / False Island Family

## Core tradition

North Coast sailors tell of an enormous marine being whose still body can be mistaken for:

- reef;
- shoal;
- floating debris;
- dark water.

Then:

- fish gather near / inside it;
- the "island" moves;
- the sea closes.

Shared anchors:

- enormous mouth;
- deceptive stillness;
- fish concentration;
- rare sightings.

## Possible natural core

Could derive from:

- unusually large whales;
- rare feeding behavior;
- several marine species combined;
- old populations / migration patterns no longer common.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Outer-island fishers

Treat it as a **real animal**, extremely rare.

They think inland stories exaggerate it.

### Major North Coast harbors

Treat it more as:

- legendary occupational history;
- warning against approaching unfamiliar feeding activity.

### Great Weaver coastal communities

May interpret it as:

- a great participant in the sea's balance;
- neither evil nor benevolent.

### Port naturalists

Have attempted to reconstruct it from:

- old logbooks;
- whale bones;
- copied illustrations.

Their "official" reconstruction may look nothing like fisher descriptions.

### Children inland

Know a simplified version:

> do not step onto an island that breathes.

## Supernatural remainder

Several older accounts describe behavior beyond known animals.

Could be:

- exaggeration;
- lost species;
- mistranslation.

## Present status

**Probably extinct / misidentified / still occasionally claimed.**

# 4. The Archive Beast

## Core tradition

Port maintains records / alleged specimens connected to a famous "sea monster" whose form has changed drastically across centuries of copying.

Shared anchors:

- one old specimen / carcass / partial body;
- one early drawing;
- later copies become more elaborate;
- respected authorities disagree.

## Possible historical core

Could be:

- deformed shark / ray / fish;
- decomposed marine mammal;
- composite fraud;
- genuine unknown species.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Port scholars

Debate:

- taxonomy;
- specimen provenance;
- whether several records refer to one creature.

### Harbor workers

Treat the official reconstruction as a joke.

They may have their own name for the actual kind of animal they think produced it.

### North Coast sailors

Some say:

> Port has the bones and still got the creature wrong.

### Festival culture

The creature has become:

- mask;
- puppet;
- carved sign;
- cheap souvenir.

This annoys people who regard the original event seriously.

## Supernatural remainder

One small piece of the preserved evidence does not fit the common natural explanation.

Possible:

- tooth;
- vertebra;
- scale;
- tissue description.

Not enough to settle anything.

## Present status

**Institutional folklore / natural-history controversy.**

# 5. The Marsh Lure

## Core tradition

Low Rivers / wetland communities tell of lights or eyes that appear:

- over still water;
- along flood paths;
- near dangerous crossings.

People who follow them:

- become lost;
- step into deep water;
- disappear;
- emerge far from the expected route.

Shared anchors:

- light;
- distance misjudgment;
- quiet water;
- movement just ahead of the observer.

## Possible natural core

Could combine:

- marsh gas / combustion phenomena;
- reflection;
- bioluminescence;
- lantern misperception;
- flood channels;
- fatigue / fog.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Floodplain communities

Treat the lights as a practical warning:

> if the lights are "walking," the ground is not where you think it is.

### Irrigation villages

May say the creature / lights follow:

- old channels;
- abandoned ditches;
- seasonal overflow.

### Children

Hear a more personified version:

- something wants you to follow.

### Old Cities canal migrants

May reinterpret the same phenomenon through their own canal-guardian beliefs.

### Port

Urban storytellers may turn it into a romantic ghost legend, stripping away the water-safety function.

## Supernatural remainder

Witnesses sometimes report:

- lights moving against wind / current;
- multiple lights maintaining distance;
- voices.

## Present status

**Common belief / phenomenon, creature embodiment varies.**

# 6. The Forest Owner

## Core tradition

Several Longwood communities tell of a presence that:

- does not necessarily attack;
- causes people to become lost;
- changes familiar paths;
- punishes taking the wrong thing in the wrong place / season.

Shared anchors:

- disorientation;
- boundary violation;
- altered path / return;
- strong association with use-rights.

## Possible historical core

Could derive from:

- real boundary enforcement;
- local guides misleading trespassers;
- woodland acoustics / navigation;
- seasonal restrictions;
- predator encounters;
- old rights disputes.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Interior use-right communities

May treat the being as:

- personification of layered rights;
- "owner" only in the sense that no one person owns the forest.

### Western-edge market communities

Treat it as:

- old cautionary tale;
- something locals tell outsiders.

### Great Weaver branches

May give the story stronger spiritual meaning.

### Route-builders / merchants

May use a simplified phrase:

> the forest turns you around.

Some do not believe in a creature at all.

### Outside Old Cities / Port retellings

May depict a literal:

- wild man;
- tree-being;
- monster.

Longwood tellers may consider those versions ridiculous.

## Supernatural remainder

Some old pathfinding accounts describe:

- impossible loops;
- markers moved overnight;
- multiple parties independently returning to the same place.

## Present status

**Strongly local; belief varies sharply.**

# 7. The Weather Animal

## Core tradition

High Roads communities report a rare mountain animal / being whose appearance is associated with:

- sudden wind;
- heavy weather;
- landslide;
- avalanche;
- dangerous temperature shift.

Shared anchors:

- low / heavy body OR indistinct shape;
- rarely seen clearly;
- emerges before weather change;
- disputed tracks / remains.

## Possible natural core

Could combine:

- rare real mountain species;
- unusual animal behavior before pressure changes;
- misidentified mustelid / cat / reptile;
- old relict fauna.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Pastoral households

Treat it as a **weather sign**.

If one is seen, animals are moved.

### High-route towns

Treat sightings as:

- route omen;
- evidence that a pass should close.

### Central switching city

Naturalists / traders argue over:

- specimens;
- sketches;
- whether three named creatures are actually one species.

### Mobile herding circuit

May regard the being as protective because:

> it appears before people need to leave.

### Port readers

Know an exaggerated "mountain dragon" version.

## Supernatural remainder

There may be:

- one preserved skin;
- bone;
- old tax record / bounty;

whose classification remains uncertain.

## Present status

**Cryptid-like / possibly extinct / possibly surviving.**

# 8. The Wind Guardian Family

## Core tradition

Old Cities traditions describe a fierce creature / presence associated with:

- dry wind;
- dust;
- illness;
- bad seasonal air.

Yet its image is also used **protectively**.

Shared anchors:

- frightening face / aspect;
- wind;
- threshold / doorway;
- turning danger away.

## Possible historical core

Could combine:

- dust storms;
- seasonal disease;
- heat / cold wind;
- protective architecture;
- inherited sacred imagery.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Upstream city

Creature is primarily:

- wind / sickness warning;
- associated with seasonal exposure.

### Downstream city

Its image is placed near:

- water houses;
- canal gates;
- household entrances.

The logic:

> frightening thing faces outward.

### Coastal Old City

Interprets it more as:

- storm / harbor guardian.

### Rural irrigation communities

May retain small protective carvings without much theology.

### Reformers / rationalists

Call the carving:

- ancestral custom;
- "weather face";
- architectural superstition.

## Supernatural remainder

Some old records attribute:

- epidemics stopping at marked districts;
- winds changing after ritual acts.

Impossible to separate from chance / later storytelling.

## Present status

**Iconographically widespread, literal belief variable.**

# 9. The Charter Beast

## Core tradition

Old Cities legal / canal traditions share a composite beast associated with:

- land grants;
- water rights;
- canal boundaries;
- curses on violators;
- old stones / seals.

Shared anchors:

- appears on records / boundary monuments;
- tied to disputes over water / land;
- said to "remember" the true boundary.

## Possible historical core

Could be:

- purely legal-symbolic;
- derived from several dangerous animals;
- based partly on real canal predator / scavenger;
- later personified through stories.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Upstream lawyers

Treat it as:

- archaic legal emblem meaning the grant is protected.

### Downstream canal communities

Tell stories that it:

- walks erased boundaries;
- appears when water is diverted illegally.

### Rural children

Hear:

> move a boundary marker and it follows you home.

### Merchant coast

Uses a stylized version on:

- seals;
- arbitration houses;
- guarantee documents.

### Great Weaver / other religious interpretation

May reinterpret the beast as:

- witness rather than enforcer;
- the land / water remembering the agreement.

## Supernatural remainder

Some charters from different eras / cities contain suspiciously similar creature imagery even where direct copying is hard to establish.

Could be:

- shared tradition;
- old transregional institution;
- coincidence.

## Present status

**Legal symbol + folklore creature.**

# 10. The Road Hound

## Core tradition

Across several travel cultures, people report a solitary dark dog-like animal / apparition appearing near:

- crossroads;
- passes;
- camps;
- people traveling alone.

Shared anchors:

- solitary;
- unusually quiet;
- follows but rarely approaches;
- appears during transition / danger.

## Possible historical core

Could combine:

- feral / working dogs;
- livestock guardian dogs;
- route companions;
- scavengers;
- traveler stress;
- old burial / road customs.

## Cultural oicotypes

### High Roads caravans

Protective:

> if it follows ahead, stay on the route.

### Mobile repair / seasonal-work families

Ambivalent companion:

- appears before safe camp;
- sometimes before death.

### North Coast

Associated with:

- drowned travelers / sailors who never returned.

### Port

Children's story:

> the black dog brings lost visitors home.

### Some religious branches

Interpret as:

- guide;
- omen;
- restless soul;
- none of the above.

## Supernatural remainder

Reported dogs sometimes:

- leave no tracks;
- disappear at a boundary;
- are recognized by unrelated travelers years apart.

## Present status

**One of the widest shared legend families.**

This is a prime example of:

> **same creature, culturally different take.**

# 11. The Night Road

## Core tradition

Traveling groups in several regions tell of a moving nocturnal phenomenon:

- riders;
- animals;
- lights;
- bells;
- distant voices;
- something like a caravan / procession traveling an established route.

Shared anchors:

- night;
- known path;
- heard before seen;
- travelers are told not to obstruct it.

## Possible historical core

Could combine:

- real night caravans;
- military movements;
- funeral processions;
- smugglers;
- wind / echo;
- older migrations.

## Cultural oicotypes

### High Roads

Old caravan lost in a winter disaster.

### Stone Hills

Dead miners returning along labor routes.

### North Coast roadheads

Drowned sailors walking inland.

### Mobile circuit

Not dead at all:

- "the road remembering everyone who has used it."

### Port entertainment

Reduced to ghost story / festival performance.

## Supernatural remainder

Some old route logs report:

- bells / traffic where no caravan arrived;
- tracks ending abruptly;
- witnesses from different communities giving structurally similar accounts.

## Present status

**Shared route phenomenon rather than confirmed creature.**

# 12. The Estuary Captive

## Core tradition

Port has an old story that fishermen / harbor workers once captured **something living** in the estuary.

Shared anchors:

- caught in net / trap;
- humanoid or humanlike enough to unsettle observers;
- unable / unwilling to speak intelligibly;
- held by authorities;
- eventually returned / escaped to water;
- official record survives in incomplete form.

## Possible historical core

Could be:

- injured / isolated human;
- foreign diver;
- person with unusual condition;
- marine mammal;
- hoax;
- unknown species;
- mixed memory of several cases.

## Cultural oicotypes

### Old harbor families

Often emphasize:

> Port treated a frightened stranger badly.

Creature identity is secondary.

### Port official history

Treats it as:

- disputed custody case;
- example of archaic law.

### Religious storytellers

May say:

- water spirit;
- transformed human;
- omen.

### Naturalists

Argue from:

- measurements;
- copied sketches;
- net records.

### North Coast fishers

Some insist Port officials simply failed to recognize a known animal.

Others insist:

> no known animal does what the records say it did.

### Three Moons popular culture

The figure may have become:

- mask;
- puppet;
- children's tale.

## Supernatural remainder

The surviving official description includes at least one detail that does not fit the strongest natural explanation.

## Present status

**Famous Port legend with contested ethical / zoological meaning.**

# 13. Cross-family transmission map

These families should overlap.

## Underpass → Stone Hills → Port

Migrant miners carry:

- Knocking Below;
- Ore-Spoiler.

Port publishes / standardizes names miners dislike.

## North Coast → Port → wider continent

Port turns:

- Great Mouth;
- Archive Beast;
- Estuary Captive

into:

- natural-history controversy;
- broadsheet stories;
- festival imagery.

Those versions travel back outward.

## Low Rivers ↔ Old Cities

Marsh Lure and canal traditions cross through:

- irrigation workers;
- merchants;
- seasonal labor.

An Old Cities listener may reinterpret the light as:

- a canal guardian rather than lure.

## Longwood ↔ High Roads

Forest Owner traditions and route phenomena overlap where:

- passes meet woods;
- rights disputes meet travel routes.

## Mobile circuits

Road Hound / Night Road absorb motifs from everywhere.

This makes mobile peoples major carriers of creature traditions.

# 14. Shared-creature example — how one legend changes

Take the **Road Hound**.

## Shared core

- dark;
- dog-like;
- solitary;
- appears to travelers;
- unusual behavior.

## High Roads version

> weather / route guardian.

## Mobile-worker version

> camp finder / sometimes death omen.

## North Coast version

> drowned person's escort.

## Port children's version

> rescues lost festival visitors.

## Rationalist explanation

> generations of stories about large working / feral dogs.

## Scholar's problem

The local versions contradict each other.

But:

- timing;
- road association;
- silence;
- solitary appearance

remain unexpectedly stable.

That is the exact texture desired.

# 15. Which families are strongest enough to promote later?

## Tier 1 — strongest

- Knocking Below;
- Great Mouth;
- Marsh Lure;
- Forest Owner;
- Weather Animal;
- Charter Beast;
- Road Hound;
- Estuary Captive.

These have:

- strong source mechanisms;
- direct world fit;
- cultural variation;
- ambiguity.

## Tier 2 — useful but should wait

- Ore-Spoiler;
- Archive Beast;
- Wind Guardian;
- Night Road.

These may become:

- part of another family;
- more symbolic than creature-like;
- regionally narrow.

Do not force twelve separate entities if eight stronger traditions can carry the world better.

# 17. Next step

Before final names / canon promotion:

1. attach each Tier 1 family to **specific communities** from the cultural analogue project;
2. decide how the legend moved between those communities;
3. give each culture's version:
   - local name;
   - distinctive feature;
   - moral / practical meaning;
4. identify 1–2 pieces of evidence each community cites;
5. decide which family has the strongest genuinely supernatural remainder.

Only then should individual stories / sightings be written.

That preserves:

> **culture first, legend second, bestiary last.**
