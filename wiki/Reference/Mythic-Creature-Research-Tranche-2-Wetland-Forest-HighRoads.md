# 1. Low Rivers / wetland research pool

## 1A. Kelpie — water danger given agency

### Source tradition / geography

Scottish water-spirit folklore.

### Source

National Trust for Scotland:

- https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/a-place-for-natural-heritage-folklore

### Source-derived features

The National Trust describes kelpies as:

- shape-shifting water spirits;
- commonly appearing as horses on land;
- luring children onto their backs;
- dragging them into water.

The Trust explicitly interprets the tale as likely functioning, at least in part, as a warning keeping children away from dangerous water.

### Core mechanism

~~~text
real drowning hazard
→ memorable creature
→ child-facing warning
→ practical avoidance behavior survives
~~~

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

For the Low Rivers, a legendary animal could encode:

- deceptively calm channels;
- undercut banks;
- sudden deep water;
- seasonal currents;
- dangerous flood pools.

The creature does not need to resemble a horse.

The useful mechanism is:

> **danger becomes something that invites you closer.**

That is stronger than a generic "river monster attacks people."

### Do not borrow

- kelpie name;
- horse form;
- Scottish cultural identity;
- exact child-abduction tale.

# 2. Will-o'-the-wisp — real phenomenon, uncertain explanation

### Source tradition / geography

Widespread European marsh / bog light folklore.

### Source

Smithsonian:

- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/what-actually-sparks-will-o-the-wisps-a-new-study-traces-the-science-behind-the-mysterious-wandering-lights-180987421/

### Source-derived features

Will-o'-the-wisp traditions describe strange lights associated with:

- marshes;
- swamps;
- bogs.

Folklore has interpreted them as:

- spirits;
- ghosts;
- tricksters.

Modern scientific explanations remain debated.

A 2025 experimental study demonstrated "microlightning" in methane-containing bubbles, offering one possible mechanism, while other researchers caution that this does not by itself explain historical sightings.

The article also notes another proposed historical mechanism:

- marsh gas interacting with travelers' lanterns.

### Why this is useful

This is a near-perfect **uncertainty-preserving phenomenon**.

Something physical may genuinely occur.

Its mechanism can remain unclear even to modern investigation.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A Low Rivers "creature" tradition may begin not with a body but with:

- lights;
- eyes;
- reflections;
- flame-like movement.

Later stories can give the lights:

- intent;
- voices;
- a body;
- different moral meanings.

The apparent decline of sightings after ecological / technological change is especially useful.

A legend could become "less real" as:

- wetlands are drained;
- lantern practice changes;
- waterways are engineered.

That does not prove older witnesses were lying.

### Do not borrow

- will-o'-the-wisp name;
- standard ghost-light appearance as a finished design.

# 3. Welsh corpse-candle traditions — repeated testimony and route memory

### Source tradition / geography

Welsh folk belief.

### Sources

Museum Wales oral-history collection:

- https://museum.wales/collections/folktales/?story=15
- https://museum.wales/collections/folktales/?story=8

### Source-derived features

Museum Wales preserves recorded oral accounts concerning **corpse candles / corpse lights**:

- small lights interpreted as omens of death;
- lights moving along routes;
- witnesses linking later deaths / funerals to earlier lights;
- beliefs transmitted through families and neighbors.

The museum source is particularly useful because it preserves:

- named tellers;
- interview context;
- how belief and family trust shape testimony.

### Mechanism

The important feature is not necessarily the death omen itself.

It is:

> **an ambiguous sensory event becomes meaningful because a community repeatedly maps it onto routes, households, and later events.**

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A river / marsh creature tradition could use:

- repeated lights;
- tracks / routes;
- later disappearance / death;
- community memory.

This creates creature lore that depends on **pattern recognition**, not physical capture.

Possible transformation:

> people say the "creature" travels a specific flood path because lights / deaths repeatedly line up with it.

### Do not borrow

- corpse-candle name;
- Welsh death-omen cosmology;
- exact funeral symbolism.

# 4. Púca and the seasonal berry boundary

### Source tradition / geography

Irish seasonal folklore.

### Source

National Museum of Ireland:

- https://cms.museum.ie/en-IE/News/2023/Ghost-turnips%2C-pucas%2C-fortune-telling-and-evil-abd

### Source-derived feature

The museum records a Halloween / seasonal story told to children that the **Púca** went about spitting on wild fruits, discouraging people from eating berries / apples after that seasonal point.

### Why this matters

This is an excellent example of:

~~~text
seasonal practical rule
→ supernatural contamination story
→ memorable behavior boundary
~~~

The source does not establish that the custom originated *because* late fruit was unsafe.

That causal explanation should remain a **10Kings research inference**, not a sourced fact.

### Useful 10Kings mechanism

Longwood / Low Rivers communities may encode:

- last safe harvest date;
- fungal risk;
- frost damage;
- animal contamination;
- overripe fruit;
- ecological recovery periods

through a creature that "claims" food after a threshold.

The key is:

> **the creature marks when human rights of use end.**

That dovetails extremely well with Longwood's layered resource-right culture.

### Do not borrow

- Púca name / identity;
- Samhain;
- Irish religious / seasonal customs.

# 5. Low Rivers / wetland synthesis

Strong recurring mechanisms now include:

- attractive danger;
- real but poorly understood natural phenomena;
- routes carrying supernatural meaning;
- seasonal boundaries encoded as creature behavior.

This suggests Low Rivers legends should probably be less about:

> giant thing in swamp.

and more about:

> **water that looks safe, lights that seem to lead somewhere, and creatures whose behavior tells people when / where not to cross, harvest, swim, or camp.**

# 6. Longwood / forest-boundary research pool

## 6A. Leshy / forest-master tradition

### Source tradition / geography

East Slavic forest-spirit traditions.

### Research sources

Modern scholarly discussion:

- Tomasz Z. Majkowski, Magdalena Kozyra, Aleksandra Prokopek, "The Invention of Leshy" (2025):
  - https://doi.org/10.1177/15554120251397689

Supplementary museum / reference leads:

- Afanasyev local museum:
  - https://xn----7sbgidbbbrildbg7a5cxa5b6d.xn--p1ai/exponat/leshiy
- Rijksmuseum library record for comparative "wild man" scholarship:
  - https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/publication/The-wild-man-medieval-myths-and-symbolism--5b9243473966831f3487f687b85a9446

### Source-derived / research-supported features

The scholarly search material supports that:

- "Leshy" as now imagined is not a single timeless pan-Slavic monster;
- traditions vary regionally and historically;
- the figure is associated with forest ownership / guardianship and changing interpretation;
- modern fantasy has substantially reshaped the image.

The local museum source describes a forest "owner" / spirit who:

- is not simply evil;
- punishes violations of forest rules;
- belongs to a culturally mixed local tradition.

### Core mechanism

> **the forest is treated as a social domain with an owner / rules, not empty land.**

This is extremely relevant to Longwood.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A Longwood legendary creature may personify:

- local use-right boundaries;
- hunting etiquette;
- cutting limits;
- navigation rules;
- obligations to people who already use the land.

Its danger may be:

- disorientation;
- misdirection;
- lost time;

rather than direct predation.

### Important caution

Do not flatten living Slavic traditions into:

> antlered fantasy forest god.

Modern fantasy imagery is itself part of the distortion history.

### Do not borrow

- Leshy name;
- Slavic religious identity;
- modern antler / bark monster iconography;
- exact rituals.

# 7. European Woodwose / Wild Man — wilderness as social contrast

### Sources

Rijksmuseum library / Metropolitan Museum exhibition record:

- https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/publication/The-wild-man-medieval-myths-and-symbolism--5b9243473966831f3487f687b85a9446

Association for Suffolk Museums:

- https://suffolkmuseums.org/projects-archive/the-last-woodwose/

### Source-derived features

The Wild Man / Woodwose appears widely in:

- medieval European art;
- literature;
- church carving;
- later local legend.

The Suffolk museum project emphasizes that the figure remains embedded in local visual / legendary history and can be reinterpreted through modern ecological concerns.

### Useful mechanism

The figure can operate as:

- boundary between settlement and wilderness;
- projection of what "civilized" people imagine outside society;
- symbol whose meaning changes across time.

This suggests a 10Kings legendary creature can reveal as much about:

> **outsiders' ideas of Longwood**

as about Longwood communities themselves.

That is particularly useful.

One polity's "wild forest man" may be a grotesque misunderstanding of:

- isolated woodcutters;
- itinerant charcoal workers;
- remote religious practitioners;
- actual rare fauna.

### Do not borrow

- hairy wild-man design;
- medieval Christian "civilized vs wild" moral frame.

# 8. Longwood synthesis

Two strong but different functions emerge.

## L-A — the Forest Owner

Legend encodes:

- use rights;
- boundaries;
- getting lost;
- respect for local rules.

## L-B — the Outsider's Wild Thing

Legend encodes:

- settled outsiders misunderstanding remote workers / communities;
- fear of people outside formal jurisdiction;
- later folkloric transformation.

These should **not necessarily become the same creature**.

The contrast could itself become world texture:

> Longwood locals tell one story; outsiders think it is the story of a "forest monster" and get it wrong.

# 9. High Roads / mountain research pool

## 9A. Stollenwurm — a cryptid-like Alpine animal tradition

### Source tradition / geography

Swiss Alpine / Jura legend.

### Sources

Swiss fairy-tale / legend archive (Mutabor Märchenstiftung) preserving earlier published collections:

- https://www.maerchenstiftung.ch/maerchendatenbank/6958/der-stollenwurm
- https://www.schweizermaerchenpreis.ch/maerchendatenbank/5662/der-stollenwurm-bei-woelfliswil
- https://www.schweizermaerchenpreis.ch/maerchendatenbank/7439/der-stollenwurm-von-woelfiswil

Swiss Alpine Club historical discussion:

- https://www.sac-cas.ch/de/die-alpen/von-drachen-und-stollenwuermern-5392/

### Source-derived features

Historical collections describe a reputed Alpine animal:

- short / thick;
- serpent-like;
- short legs;
- descriptions varying substantially;
- reports linked in some traditions to heat / weather change;
- testimony attributed to ordinary rural / pastoral witnesses.

Some collectors speculated that witnesses might have misidentified:

- otter;
- marten;
- polecat;
- weasel;
- other real animals.

One preserved story even involves an alleged carcass / skeleton whose later evidentiary trail becomes unclear.

### Why this is valuable

This is closer to a **cryptid tradition** than a purely moral monster.

It contains:

- testimony;
- inconsistent morphology;
- weather association;
- livestock harm;
- missing physical evidence;
- naturalist attempts at explanation.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

High Roads can support legends of:

- rare mountain fauna;
- animals emerging before weather changes;
- witnesses whose descriptions disagree;
- disputed old specimens.

This is excellent for the desired:

> **may once have existed / may still exist / truth somewhere in the middle**

tone.

### Do not borrow

- Stollenwurm / Tatzelwurm name;
- exact cat-headed short-legged serpent design;
- Swiss locality.

# 10. Wildmannli — weather knowledge and pastoral labor

### Source tradition / geography

Liechtenstein / Alpine legend.

### Source

Official Liechtenstein tourism heritage page:

- https://en.tourismus.li/reiseland/unser-land/feiertage-brauchtum/top-sagen-liechtensteins/die-wildmannli.html

### Source-derived features

The Wildmannli legend describes mountain beings who:

- lived in caves;
- cared for cattle;
- could be helpful to pastoral households;
- did not come during dangerous **Föhn** wind conditions.

A story turns failure of expected supernatural help into a lesson about weather / pastoral dependence.

### Core mechanism

This is extremely useful:

> **a supernatural helper's "rules" encode when conditions are unsafe enough that no competent herder would behave normally.**

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A High Roads legendary creature can be believed to:

- warn of weather;
- refuse to appear before storms;
- move animals before dangerous conditions;
- be blamed / praised for herd survival.

This can preserve:

- wind knowledge;
- avalanche / storm signs;
- seasonal pasture experience.

### Do not borrow

- Wildmannli identity / appearance;
- Liechtenstein local tale;
- magical coal-to-money motif.

# 11. Swiss Alpine legend structure — real place gives credibility

### Source

National Museum Zurich:

- https://www.landesmuseum.ch/en/exhibitions/temporary/2022/traditional-tales-from-the-alps/traditional-tales-from-the-alps
- https://www.landesmuseum.ch/en/about-us/media/traditional-tales-from-the-alps-25268

### Source-derived insight

The museum emphasizes a key distinction:

> legends are tied to **real places**, which gives them a veneer of credibility.

It also notes:

- legends can feature real / alleged historical figures;
- the same basic story can exist in regional variants;
- stories can carry instructional functions.

### 10Kings application

This should become a hard design rule for High Roads legendary creatures.

Do not say:

> they live in the mountains.

Say:

> **people report them at this pass, this rock field, this abandoned shelter, this particular descent.**

Then ask why that place generates the story.

This makes the creature part of:

- navigation;
- weather;
- jurisdiction;
- local memory.

# 12. High Roads synthesis

Three mechanisms stand out:

## H-A — uncertain surviving animal

Cryptid-like testimony + inconsistent morphology + lost specimen.

## H-B — weather creature

Creature behavior encodes:

- storm;
- wind;
- avalanche;
- unsafe grazing.

## H-C — place-bound legend

Credibility comes from exact location and repeated local testimony.

A strong High Roads creature may combine all three **without copying any one Alpine being**.

# 13. Meta-research lesson — some "old folklore" is modern invention

One useful research discovery is the **barbegazi** problem.

A modern French-language folklore investigation:

- https://crieauloup.fr/legendes/barbegazi/

reports that the commonly circulated "barbegazi" Alpine creature appears to have no traceable source before a 1985 illustrated encyclopedia and is likely a modern invented creature later repeated as traditional folklore.

This source is not being used as a creature comparator.

It is being preserved as a **research warning**:

> **"obscure folklore" websites often circulate modern fantasy inventions as ancient tradition.**

Therefore every future comparator should preferably have at least one of:

- museum;
- archive;
- historical text;
- scholarly publication;
- reputable heritage institution.

Do not assume repetition on modern bestiary sites establishes tradition.
