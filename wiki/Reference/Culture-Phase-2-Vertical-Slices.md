# Culture Phase 2 — Ordinary-Life Vertical Slices

## Status

**Reference / design tests — not canon.**

These slices test the new owners:

- [Daily Life and Customs](../Culture/Daily-Life-and-Customs.md)
- [Clothing and Material Culture](../Culture/Clothing-and-Material-Culture.md)

Each slice separates:

- **Established / working anchors** — supported by current owner pages;
- **Provisional test construction** — invented only to test whether the cultural system produces believable ordinary life.

The goal is not to canonize seven model households.

The goal is to see where the framework breaks.

---

# Test 1 — Western Uplands mixed workshop household

## Established anchors

Current Western Uplands supports:

- mixed mining / craft / farming economies;
- workshops attached to homes;
- public wells and shared yards;
- apprenticeship;
- labor conflict;
- dangerous work;
- practical literacy;
- imported River Country staples;
- guild power;
- worker / owner tension.

## Provisional household

A modest town household contains:

- one experienced metalworker;
- spouse / partner working partly in household commerce;
- two children;
- an apprentice sleeping in the workshop loft;
- an older relative who no longer performs heavy work.

The household does not live only from smithing.

It also:

- keeps a garden;
- rents a small goat enclosure;
- repairs farm tools for nearby villages;
- buys grain through a merchant tied to River Country routes.

## Daily rhythm

Morning:
- apprentice and senior worker open the workshop;
- household food is simple and portable;
- older relative supervises errands / child care;
- partner handles accounts, purchasing or market exchange depending on household arrangement.

Midday:
- food eaten around work;
- customers arrive;
- guild information and political rumor circulate through the workshop.

Evening:
- repairs;
- mending;
- accounting;
- shared meal;
- neighbors borrowing tools or exchanging services.

## Clothing test

Work clothing is more important than “Western Uplands fashion.”

Likely:
- heavy apron;
- replaceable outer sleeves;
- sturdy footwear;
- head covering near sparks;
- patched everyday layers.

The apprentice may wear an institutional marker smaller than a uniform:
- pin;
- cord;
- stamped leather tag;
- guild color on a belt.

Exact marker remains unresolved.

## Social questions exposed

- Does the apprentice count as family?
- Who pays if the apprentice is injured?
- Who owns the tools?
- Can the older relative's craft knowledge give authority despite reduced labor?
- Does guild membership include funeral / injury support?
- Is a patched garment evidence of poverty, experience, or both?

## Current-pressure test

A Blackbellow recruiter offering higher wages does not merely tempt “the worker.”

It threatens:
- household income;
- guild loyalty;
- apprentice future;
- supplier relationships;
- local reputation.

**Result: PASS.**

The Western Uplands world becomes richer when work, household and guild are inseparable but not identical.

# Test 2 — North Country secondary harbor household

## Established anchors

Current North Country supports:

- coastal towns;
- fishing / shipping;
- seasonal storms;
- clan and harbor obligations;
- communal work yards;
- preservation;
- mixed inland food production;
- fishing-right politics;
- declining / shifting fish stocks.

## Provisional household

A harbor household includes:

- one person who spends part of the year at sea;
- one preservation / market worker;
- several children;
- a widowed cousin whose own household was lost after a storm;
- seasonal boarder from an inland pastoral district.

This is not treated as an unusual “extended family.”

It is one plausible response to seasonal labor and maritime risk.

## Daily rhythm

When boats are out:
- shore household handles drying / salting / selling;
- news of weather dominates public attention;
- children's tasks shift toward carrying, sorting and household errands.

When storms close the harbor:
- more people remain indoors;
- repair work increases;
- food preservation and shared meals become more important;
- crowding becomes socially visible.

## Clothing test

Different household members need different clothes.

Sailor:
- close-fitting work layers;
- weather-resistant outer garment;
- secure footwear;
- gloves.

Market / preservation worker:
- washable apron / outer layer;
- warm indoor/outdoor clothing;
- head covering against weather / smoke where useful.

Inland boarder:
- clothing optimized for herding / overland work rather than ship decks.

This creates visual difference inside one household.

## Social questions exposed

- What does a harbor owe a family after someone is lost at sea?
- Which kin are expected to absorb widows / orphans?
- Does a rescue obligation override clan rivalry?
- What hospitality is owed to visiting crew members?
- How long can a seasonal boarder remain before becoming part of the household?

## Current-pressure test

Fish-stock decline affects:
- income;
- marriage prospects;
- inheritance of boats / rights;
- clothing replacement;
- winter food;
- willingness to join convoy agreements.

**Result: PASS.**

North Country's strongest cultural depth comes from managing absence, risk and access—not “stoicism.”

# Test 3 — River Country river-market household

## Established anchors

Current River Country supports:

- market towns;
- river trade;
- estate and cooperative systems;
- mills;
- storage;
- seasonal labor;
- broad food base;
- land / seed / storage politics;
- current abundance crisis.

## Provisional household

A river-market household combines:

- small tenant or cooperative farm interest;
- mill / market work;
- two generations under one roof;
- one young adult considering work elsewhere.

Household wealth is partly stored in:

- tools;
- stored food;
- animals;
- credit;
- access rights;

not simply coin.

## Daily rhythm

Peak season:
- nearly everyone contributes to harvest / storage;
- hired or exchanged labor moves between households;
- meals shift around work.

Off-season:
- repairs;
- textile work;
- market activity;
- legal / tenancy disputes;
- courtship and visiting become easier because labor pressure drops.

## Clothing test

Clothing should show labor and washability rather than “farmer costume.”

Possible working traits:
- sleeves / lower garments adapted to mud and work;
- sun / rain headwear;
- repair and re-dyeing;
- second-best garments for market or worship;
- estate / town fashions differing sharply from farm clothing.

## Social questions exposed

- Who controls stored surplus?
- Which goods belong to the household versus the cooperative?
- Does a married child remain part of the household?
- How do tenancy and inheritance interact?
- Who hosts seasonal workers?
- Is bringing food to a sick neighbor charity, obligation, or both?

## Current-pressure test

The abundance crisis makes customs political.

A practice once designed for scarcity—communal storage, grain pooling, inherited obligations—may become controversial when prices collapse.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

River Country becomes most distinctive through **seasonality, storage and property relationships**, not generic pastoral warmth.

# Test 4 — High Country caravan-yard neighborhood

## Established anchors

Current High Country supports:

- multilingual towns;
- caravan yards;
- inns;
- public scales;
- stables;
- repair shops;
- trade registers;
- route authorities;
- seasonal travel;
- strong imported-goods presence.

## Provisional neighborhood

A dense caravan district contains:

- permanent local households;
- lodging houses;
- translators;
- animal handlers;
- repair workers;
- itinerant merchants;
- pilgrims;
- temporary labor.

The neighborhood constantly contains people who do not know the local etiquette.

## Daily rhythm

Morning:
- departures;
- weighing;
- feed purchases;
- permit checks.

Midday:
- markets;
- repairs;
- bargaining;
- translation work.

Night:
- lodging;
- dispute settlement;
- public eating;
- gossip from several routes.

## Clothing test

Clothing becomes strongly occupational and portable.

Caravan worker:
- layered travel clothes;
- scarf / wind protection;
- repair kit;
- pouches.

Permanent local clerk:
- cleaner office clothing;
- seal / writing equipment;
- footwear suited to stone streets.

Wealthy merchant:
- imported textile or trim;
- practical travel outer layer covering expensive inner clothing.

## Social questions exposed

- What does an innkeeper owe a sponsored traveler?
- Who is responsible for damage caused by a guest?
- Which language is used in a dispute?
- Does a caravan employer provide clothing / equipment?
- How do local residents view long-term foreigners?

## Current-pressure test

Caravan attacks make hospitality and sponsorship more restrictive.

A stranger who once needed only money may now need:
- references;
- guild papers;
- a known sponsor.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

High Country culture is most visible through **guesthood, translation and temporary belonging**.

# Test 5 — Forest Country river-town household

## Established anchors

Current Forest Country supports:

- river settlements;
- forest products;
- managed woodland;
- agriculture in clearings;
- water and cutting rights;
- logging conflict;
- urban as well as rural communities;
- specialist ecological knowledge.

## Provisional household

A river-town household includes:

- one river / timber worker;
- one market / processing worker;
- children;
- an unrelated young person fostered through a kin / work relationship;
- regular contact with a warden network.

The household buys significant food rather than living entirely by foraging.

## Daily rhythm

Work changes with:
- river height;
- timber movement;
- fishing;
- road condition;
- cutting rules.

Public life centers around:
- dock / landing;
- market;
- workshops;
- water access;
- local permission systems.

## Clothing test

Wet conditions matter more than symbolic leaves.

Likely:
- fast-drying / replaceable outer layers;
- leg / foot protection;
- rain cover;
- work gloves;
- insect protection where necessary;
- tool belts / baskets.

A city merchant may wear imported fabric completely unlike a forest worker.

## Social questions exposed

- Who has authority to grant access to a forest area?
- Can visiting kin gather resources?
- What counts as theft versus customary use?
- Who helps after a logging injury?
- How does a town resident differ from a remote settlement resident in etiquette?

## Current-pressure test

Logging expansion turns ordinary permission customs into political flashpoints.

A seemingly minor question—“who said you could cut here?”—can involve:
- household rights;
- town law;
- warden authority;
- guild contract;
- regional politics.

**Result: PASS.**

Forest Country becomes much stronger when culture is about **rights, use and local ecological knowledge**, not mystical presentation.

# Test 6 — South Country irrigation-city neighborhood

## Established anchors

Current South Country supports:

- multiple city-states;
- irrigation;
- dense urban life;
- merchant families;
- civic competition;
- public / private speech differences;
- water law;
- warm / dry climate;
- city-specific food and identity.

## Provisional neighborhood

A middling urban district contains:

- courtyard households;
- small merchants;
- irrigation clerks;
- craft workers;
- renters;
- servants / laborers;
- visiting rural relatives.

The same street contains different legal statuses and wealth levels.

## Daily rhythm

Morning:
- water delivery / allocation matters;
- markets open early;
- heat changes work timing.

Midday:
- some work slows or shifts indoors / shade.

Evening:
- public social life increases;
- shops, courtyards and neighborhood gathering become more active.

Exact schedules remain local, not regional canon.

## Clothing test

Heat adaptation produces:
- breathable layers;
- head / neck protection;
- shade.

But city fashion determines:
- cut;
- dye;
- ornament;
- status.

An irrigation laborer and a wealthy patron may both wear loose light cloth while looking nothing alike.

## Social questions exposed

- Who controls household water?
- How is a guest offered water without creating unsustainable obligation?
- Which parts of a courtyard are private?
- How visible is household wealth from the street?
- Are public disputes shameful, expected, or class-dependent?
- Which city fashions are mocked by rivals?

## Current-pressure test

Drought rumors can change etiquette before actual scarcity:

- less generous water offering;
- complaints about fountains;
- scrutiny of gardens;
- resentment toward wealthy households.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

South Country is culturally strongest when **water law, dense civic life and city rivalry** produce behavior—not “elegance.”

# Test 7 — Port dock-neighborhood household

## Established anchors

Current Port supports:

- mixed families;
- native Port identity;
- immigrant neighborhoods;
- dock labor;
- warehouses;
- interpreters;
- legal pluralism;
- migration;
- refugees;
- crowding;
- street food;
- guilds;
- rapid cultural experimentation.

## Provisional household

A crowded dock district dwelling contains:

- Port-born couple from different family backgrounds;
- children;
- one grandparent who migrated decades ago;
- unrelated boarder working in a warehouse;
- rotating short-term relative / sailor guest.

No one in the home has exactly the same linguistic biography.

## Daily rhythm

Household members work different shifts.

Meals are not always eaten together.

Childcare is assembled from:
- relatives;
- neighbors;
- older children;
- paid help where affordable.

Privacy is limited.

Neighborhood life therefore matters enormously.

## Clothing test

Port is where the material-culture system becomes visibly dynamic:

- imported secondhand coat;
- locally copied fashionable cut;
- guild-issued dock gear;
- altered migrant garment;
- reused sailcloth bag;
- foreign buttons on locally made clothing.

Port fashion should generate new combinations that **become normal Port forms**, not remain visible collages.

## Social questions exposed

- When does a boarder become “family”?
- Which language do parents use with children?
- Which neighborhood institution watches children?
- How are foreign names recorded?
- What does a Port-born person consider foreign?
- Who gets sleeping privacy?
- What clothing signals that someone is newly arrived?

## Current-pressure test

Refugee inflow increases:
- rents;
- crowding;
- secondhand clothing demand;
- informal childcare;
- resentment;
- new food / fashion / language influence.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

Port may be the best place to test whether the whole culture system behaves dynamically.

# Cross-slice findings

## 1. Household form should be generated locally

The framework does not need one canonical family structure per region.

The stronger recurring variables are:

- labor;
- housing;
- migration;
- risk;
- property;
- care.

## 2. Workwear beats regional costume

The clothing system works best when:

```text
occupation + climate + class
```

predict more than region alone.

Region still affects available material and trade access.

## 3. Institutions enter the home

Guilds, faiths, cooperatives, clans, courts and neighborhood networks matter because they influence:

- injury support;
- apprenticeship;
- funerals;
- childcare;
- lodging;
- debt;
- marriage;
- reputation.

This gives [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md) a human-scale expression.

## 4. Privacy should vary dramatically

Housing type and household form affect:

- romance;
- secrets;
- conflict;
- prayer;
- gossip;
- sleep.

This is a useful scene-level distinction the older notes largely lacked.

## 5. Current crises should alter ordinary customs

The crisis becomes believable when it changes things like:

- who gets invited;
- whether a guest is affordable;
- whether clothes are replaced;
- whether children leave for work;
- whether storage is shared;
- whether boarders are accepted;
- whether strangers require sponsorship.

## 6. Material culture should circulate

A trade route can visibly move:

- cloth;
- buttons;
- shoe forms;
- bags;
- dyes;
- tools;
- repair methods;
- jewelry;
- secondhand clothing.

This creates cultural mixing without resorting to costume mashups.

---

# What failed from the legacy model

The vertical slices do **not** need:

- one emotional temperament per region;
- fixed regional color palettes;
- symbolic motifs on everyone's clothes;
- profession-themed proverbs everywhere;
- one festival explaining an entire culture;
- one costume silhouette per region.

Those approaches would make the world easier to identify at a glance but materially less believable.

# Next tests

Before Phase 3 food deepening, Phase 2 should still answer several specific questions:

1. What textile supply chains are actually plausible between regions?
2. Which fibers are local versus imported?
3. How common are guild welfare / burial / injury benefits?
4. What legal forms of household and marriage exist in major polities?
5. How does apprenticeship affect residence and childhood?
6. What does secondhand clothing trade look like in Port and High Country?
7. Which customs differ more by **class or profession** than by region?

These should be resolved only as far as needed for food and ordinary-life worldbuilding.
