# Opening Scene 3 — Guarantee and Hidden Triage Test V1

## Status

**Author-side prose experiment — not canon.**

Continues:

- [Opening Scene 1 — Voice and Pacing Test V2](Opening-Scene-1-Voice-Test-V2.md);
- [Opening Scene 2 — Records Office Test V2](Opening-Scene-2-Records-Office-Test-V2.md).

Purpose:

- resolve the opening grain problem;
- show Port's newer coordination system genuinely work;
- show a powerful commercial network solve the remaining guarantee problem quickly;
- expose the first visible cost of limited risk / storage capacity;
- keep the Council link entirely author-side;
- let Wurdren complete a meaningful act he still undervalues.

## Author-side commercial mechanism

The guarantor's Port office has recently received authority to use **limited supplemental backing for staple cargo** when:

- the original delivery was valid;
- the named warehouse failed for capacity rather than misconduct;
- a substitute warehouse is reciprocally recognized;
- the cargo can still reach settlement promptly.

This authority exists because larger commercial correspondents are trying to keep ordinary food movement functioning during Three Moons strain.

Author-side only:

> one of the correspondent relationships making this fast response possible is Council-linked.

Nobody in the scene knows that.

The backing is finite.

Elsewhere the same office is:

- shortening extensions;
- demanding stronger collateral;
- declining marginal non-staple exposure.

This is the first visible form of:

> **help + hidden triage.**

# Prose test

The guarantor's office was quieter than the records office and more expensive in every visible way.

The floor had been swept recently. The benches matched. The writing tables were wide enough that two people could spread papers without touching elbows. Even the queue had been arranged into separate lines.

Wurdren distrusted it immediately.

The grain agent did not appear to notice the furniture.

She had begun counting time instead.

"South Transfer closes intake at dusk."

Wurdren looked toward the windows.

"We have daylight."

"We have papers, another walk, unloading, weighing, receipt, and settlement."

"Less daylight."

"Considerably."

A clerk near the entrance took one look at the transfer sheet and sent them to the shorter of the two lines.

The grain agent read the small board above it.

"Amendments."

"That sounds manageable."

"It sounds billable."

They waited.

The people ahead of them did not look ruined. That reassured Wurdren less than it should have.

One was a ship supplier arguing quietly over a delayed account. Another was a woman with two sealed letters and the expression of someone who had already heard no from three different people and intended to continue until language failed.

At the far wall, runners moved in and out through a door marked for correspondence.

Every few minutes one returned with a folded slip, a mark, or another question.

The grain agent watched them.

"That's what we're waiting on?"

"Probably."

"You say that as if you understand this."

"I understand waiting."

"Useful profession."

"I have practiced widely."

Her mouth moved, almost a smile, then stopped when the next desk opened.

The guarantor's clerk held out a hand.

"Transfer."

The grain agent gave them the copied certification.

"Original buyer terms."

She produced those too.

"Warehouse release."

Another sheet.

The clerk read all three without comment.

Then:

"Cargo remains grain?"

"Yes."

"Same quantity?"

"Yes."

"Same seller?"

"Yes."

"Same buyer?"

"Yes."

"Any delay before arrival?"

"No."

The grain agent hesitated.

"Not before arrival."

The clerk looked up.

"Current delay."

"Berth and transfer only."

"Damage?"

"No."

"Dispute over weight?"

"No."

"Dispute over title?"

"No."

"Good."

The grain agent leaned forward.

"Good enough?"

"Good enough to ask."

They copied several lines onto a narrow sheet, sanded it, and sent it through the correspondence door.

"How long?" the agent asked.

"Depends who answers."

"Can you narrow that?"

"No."

She pressed her lips together.

The clerk moved the papers to one side.

"You can sit."

"If I sit, will it be faster?"

"No."

"Then I'll stand."

Wurdren chose the bench.

His knee appreciated the commercial system more than the rest of him did.

Across from them, the ship supplier was still at the neighboring desk.

"I've carried the account six years," the man was saying.

The clerk there spoke quietly enough that Wurdren caught only pieces.

"...not the old extension..."

"...festival exposure..."

"...covered storage already committed..."

The supplier's voice rose.

"Committed where?"

The clerk said something Wurdren did not hear.

The supplier looked across the room as if the answer might be sitting on one of the benches.

Wurdren looked away.

The grain agent had heard it too.

"Is that us?"

"I don't know."

"Covered storage."

"That could mean forty things."

"You don't know that."

"No."

That was true.

A runner came through the correspondence door carrying three folded slips.

None came to their desk.

The grain agent started counting something on her fingers.

"Stop doing that," Wurdren said.

"What?"

"Making time visible."

"It is visible."

"Not normally this unpleasantly."

She ignored him.

At the neighboring desk, the ship supplier gathered his papers.

"What am I supposed to do with seven days?"

The clerk answered.

"Pay the revised rate or reduce the covered amount."

"On goods already here?"

"Yes."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the answer available today."

The man stood there another moment.

Then he took the papers and left.

Nobody watched him go except Wurdren.

The guarantor's clerk noticed.

"Different account," they said.

Wurdren looked back.

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

"I was considering it."

"Consider something else."

The grain agent glanced between them.

"Does his problem have anything to do with mine?"

The clerk did not answer immediately.

"Not directly."

"Indirectly?"

"The office has limited exposure."

There it was.

The grain agent's expression changed.

"If you back this transfer, somebody else gets less?"

"Not as a rule."

"Today."

The clerk folded their hands.

"Today we are using more of the office's backing on staple cargo than we were last week."

"Because of the festival?"

"Because warehouses are full, routes are late, and staple deliveries that would normally clear are getting caught between arrangements."

"Who decided that?"

"People above my desk."

Wurdren liked that answer more than he expected.

The grain agent did not.

"Can they decide ours?"

"They already have."

A runner had appeared beside the desk.

The clerk took the folded slip, opened it, and read.

Then read it again.

"Supplemental backing approved through settlement."

The grain agent said nothing.

The clerk turned the slip toward her.

The mark at the bottom meant nothing to Wurdren.

It meant something to her.

She sat down.

Not gracefully.

Just suddenly, as if someone had removed a board she had been leaning against all day.

"We're covered?"

"For the warehouse change, through settlement today."

"Today."

"Yes."

"And tomorrow?"

"You do not want tomorrow."

"No."

The clerk added the new endorsement to the transfer copy.

"This goes to South Transfer. They issue recognized receipt. Buyer can settle against that receipt under the amended guarantee."

The grain agent took the papers.

Her hand shook once.

She stopped it by closing the folio.

"What does this cost?"

The clerk named a fee.

She stared.

Then laughed once.

It was not amusement.

"Of course."

"Less than another day at berth and an uncovered sale."

"I know."

The clerk's tone did not change.

That somehow made the answer easier to take.

Wurdren stood.

His knee objected.

He ignored it.

"South Transfer," he said.

The grain agent nodded.

They made it halfway to the door before someone called after them.

The guarantor's clerk held up one finger.

"Settlement copy comes back here."

The agent turned.

"Why?"

"Because the supplemental backing ends when the buyer pays. If I don't close it, the office still carries the exposure."

She nodded.

That explanation she understood immediately.

Outside, Port seemed louder than before.

Carts passed in both directions. A man with a handbell was trying to clear a path for a load of timber and accomplishing only noise. Somewhere beyond the warehouses, festival music had started and stopped after three notes.

The grain agent walked faster.

Wurdren kept up.

"How much have you lost?" he asked.

She looked at him.

"Today?"

"Since the warehouse."

"Berth, runner fees, copies, amendment, transfer, guarantee."

"Approximately?"

She gave him a look.

He remembered.

"Right."

She did the figures anyway.

"Less than if they had said no."

That was not the same as saying little.

South Transfer accepted the papers without argument.

That was the impressive part.

The clerk there checked the reciprocal mark, checked the guarantor endorsement, and called for scales.

No one sent them back across Port.

No one asked Wurdren to find another witness.

No one argued over whether the substitute warehouse counted as a warehouse.

The grain moved.

Sacks came off the boat and onto the scale platform in a steady line.

Marks were checked.

Weights were entered.

A receipt was written.

The grain agent watched all of it.

When the final sack disappeared through the storage doors, she stood still for a moment.

"That's it?" Wurdren asked.

"No."

He waited.

She took the receipt to a settlement desk at the far side of the yard.

That took another quarter hour.

Then she came back with a smaller packet of papers and less tension in her face.

"Now that's it."

"Paid?"

"Enough."

Wurdren held out his hand.

She stared at it.

"My fee."

"I thought you were having a moment."

"I was."

She paid him.

He counted it.

She noticed.

"You made me count sacks twice."

"Consistency is a virtue."

"You don't know how many sacks there were."

"I knew there would still be the same number at the end."

She shook her head.

Then she looked back at the warehouse.

"My brother gets paid."

Wurdren had not known she had a brother involved.

"Good."

"The boat crew gets paid."

"Also good."

"I can take the next contract instead of explaining why I lost this one."

"Better."

She tucked the remaining papers into the folio.

"For what it's worth, you could have left at the quay."

"I know."

"You could have left at the records office."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

The honest answer was not flattering.

Because he had not wanted his first job back on the road to end with him walking away while the person beside him lost money.

Because the problem had been his long enough that abandoning it felt like cheating.

Because none of that was the kind of reason he had imagined when he decided he needed to make something of the years he had left.

"Professional pride," he said.

She looked unconvinced.

"Very grand."

"I am trying to bring more grandeur into my work."

That finally got the smile.

They parted outside the yard.

The grain agent went back toward the river.

Wurdren turned toward the hiring boards.

For the first time since entering Port, he had no cargo, no papers, and no one waiting for him to fix something.

The day felt suddenly open.

Behind him, grain that had nearly become somebody's loss sat in a warehouse under a recognized seal.

A buyer would accept it.

A boat crew would be paid.

A brother upriver, apparently, would be paid too.

Wurdren considered the whole affair.

Respectable.

Useful.

Hardly the sort of thing a man came back to the road to be remembered for.

He headed toward the red-cornered notices.

# V1 assessment

## What works

### The system genuinely helps

The grain problem ends.

Not partially.

Not symbolically.

The seller / agent avoids a serious loss.

### The coordination payoff is mundane

The impressive achievement is:

> nobody has to rebuild trust from zero after the warehouse change.

That demonstrates the value of:

- reciprocal records;
- standardized recognition;
- commercial correspondence.

### Hidden triage is visible without villainy

The office openly has:

- limited exposure;
- shifting priorities.

Staple cargo receives more protection.

Another commercial account receives worse terms.

Nobody twirls a mustache.

### Council remains hidden

No Council name.

No secret order.

No suspicious whispered meeting.

Only:

- a policy;
- a correspondent mark;
- fast backing.

### Voice differentiation holds

Grain Agent:
- time / cost / settlement.

Guarantor clerk:
- risk / exposure / promise.

Wurdren:
- social deflection.

## Main risk

The neighboring ship-supplier denial is almost too neatly adjacent.

This is acceptable for a prose test because Scene 3 needs the causal cost visible.

For final manuscript, consider making the loser:

- less obviously parallel;
- visible through a later line at the records office;
- or someone whose revised terms are noticed after Wurdren's success.

The important thing is not simultaneity.

It is:

> **limited institutional capacity is being allocated.**

## Wurdren arc

Strong pass.

He has done something:

- useful;
- relational;
- nonheroic.

He immediately files it below the standard of significance he came looking for.

## Recommendation

**Scene 3 passes structurally.**

Before treating V1 as the baseline:

1. challenge the visibility / coincidence of the losing transaction;
2. decide whether the Records Worker needs a short handoff;
3. check whether the guarantor explanation reveals too much system logic too early.

If those survive, proceed to Scene 4:

> **the person who stopped looking.**
