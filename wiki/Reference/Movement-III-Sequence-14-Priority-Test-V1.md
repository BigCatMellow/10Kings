## POV

They are not yet a named / promoted recurring character.

Working role:

> **shop-floor representative / mutual-aid and labor negotiator in a major industrial district.**

They care about:

- safety;
- hours;
- ordinary-order displacement;
- workers losing hours at non-priority shops;
- secrecy around allocation.

They are not automatically anti-strategic-production.

# Prose test

The Port pins were finished before the bridge hardware.

Everyone knew because the bridge clerk came in person.

They stood in the workshop office with the old delivery slip in one hand and the new date in the other.

"This was ahead of Port."

The production clerk said:

"Yes."

"So why is Port leaving first?"

"Inspection slot."

"My order needs inspection."

"Different slot."

"Metal is metal."

The worker representative, standing by the door, said:

"No."

Both clerks looked at them.

They shrugged.

"Sorry."

The bridge clerk pointed at the yard.

"My crews are waiting."

The production clerk answered:

"So were theirs."

"Port has other suppliers."

"So does the bridge."

"Not for this work."

"Neither did Port."

The bridge clerk looked at the worker representative.

"You people on strike?"

"Not today."

"Helpful."

The production supervisor appeared from the inner office.

They had heard enough.

"The bridge order moves tomorrow."

"It was tomorrow yesterday."

"Then it has remained consistent."

Nobody laughed.

The supervisor tried again.

"We opened one inspection space for the Port pins because the lift was partially out of service."

"And what moved?"

The worker representative asked it even though they knew.

The supervisor looked at them.

"Mine-haulage repair."

The bridge clerk stared.

"So they moved mine work for Port and bridge work for mine."

"Not exactly."

"That is exactly enough."

The bridge clerk left.

The production clerk looked at the closed door.

"That went well."

The worker representative pointed toward the delay board.

"Four."

The supervisor said:

"I know."

"Five if the bridge moves again."

"I know."

"Good. Saves time."

The supervisor leaned both hands on the desk.

"We have a meeting tonight."

"I know."

"Then can we avoid having it twice?"

The representative looked through the wall toward the shop floor.

Protected work was running.

Ordinary work was waiting.

Next door, a smaller shop had sent workers home early because two expected repair orders had been postponed.

Here, people were staying late.

That was the entire argument in two buildings.

"No," they said.

"We cannot."

---

The meeting hall had been built by workers before anyone currently alive had been born.

It had hosted:

- funerals;
- technical lectures;
- injury collections;
- apprenticeship examinations;
- weddings;
- wage disputes;
- political speeches nobody admitted attending afterward.

Tonight every bench was full.

Not every person was a worker.

That annoyed some people.

The town had sent:

- a market official;
- one safety inspector.

The guild branch had sent:

- two craft officers;
- one contract clerk.

Three workshop owners attended.

One mine representative.

Several foremen.

A creditor-house observer sat near the back and wrote nothing.

The worker representative disliked them on principle.

A woman from the neighboring small shop stood first.

"We lost another day."

One of the larger workshop owners said:

"Because your buyer delayed the order."

"Because their buyer cannot get the material when protected work takes first draw."

"That is not the same."

"It looks the same from home."

Several people agreed.

A skilled worker from the priority line stood.

"I have had full wages for six weeks."

The room changed.

They continued.

"My brother at the fitting shop has had three short weeks."

The small-shop woman nodded.

"So when you say stop the protected contracts, which one of us are you helping?"

Nobody had said stop them.

That did not make the question less useful.

The worker representative stood.

"We are not asking to stop them."

Someone from the back said:

"Some are."

"Then they can say it themselves."

No one did.

The representative continued.

"We are asking:

- how much capacity is protected;
- how long;
- what ordinary work is being moved;
- what happens to safety when people stay late to meet protected dates;
- what happens when one shop has overtime while the next shop has none."

One owner said:

"You know we cannot publish customer contracts."

"Why not?"

"Because confidentiality is part of the contract."

"Convenient."

"Also normal."

A guild contract clerk said:

"It is normal."

The room disliked them for confirming it.

The representative looked at the clerk.

"Can the guild see them?"

"Some."

"Can the town?"

"Depends on the contract."

"Can workers?"

"No."

"Then when owners say the work must move, who can check?"

The safety inspector raised a hand.

"I can check safety."

"Not allocation."

"No."

The market official said:

"I can see ordinary-order complaints."

"Not the protected schedule."

"No."

The guild craft officer said:

"We can verify that a protected contract exists and that the work category is recognized."

"Can you say whether it needs half the shop?"

"No."

"Can you say whether it lasts a month or a year?"

"No."

"Can you say who gets the material?"

"No."

The worker representative looked at the room.

"Then everybody is supposed to trust the words 'priority contract.'"

One of the large-shop workers said:

"I trust my pay."

That drew laughter.

Not hostile.

The representative pointed at them.

"Good. Keep trusting it. I want to know whether you still have a shoulder after another month."

The worker rolled that shoulder.

"Pay me enough and I will decide."

The safety inspector said:

"No."

More laughter.

The room loosened.

That mattered.

---

The owners had a real answer.

That mattered too.

A workshop owner stood and put three sheets on the table.

Not contracts.

Production summaries.

"Ordinary orders are down."

The small-shop woman said:

"Because you move them."

"Some. Not all."

The owner continued.

"External buyers are delaying ordinary purchases because:

- routes are uncertain;
- Port is congested;
- they expect prices to change;
- they are waiting to see what the labor dispute does.

The protected contracts are not merely taking capacity.

They are also the orders still arriving on time."

A foreman added:

"If we lose them, some of the overtime disappears."

The small-shop woman said:

"Good."

The skilled priority-line worker said:

"My wages disappear with it."

"Not all of them."

"Enough."

A mine representative stood.

"We need the protected output."

The room turned.

The worker representative asked:

"For what?"

The mine representative smiled without humor.

"Not all of it is yours to know either."

That was the wrong answer.

The owner beside them muttered something.

Too late.

Voices rose.

The worker representative hit the table with an open hand.

Once.

The sound carried.

"That," they said, "is the problem."

The mine representative started to answer.

The representative cut them off.

"You want people to accept longer hours, moved repair work, short weeks at other shops, and secrecy because the work is too important to explain."

"We can explain the category."

"Then explain it."

The mine representative looked at the guild clerk.

The guild clerk answered instead.

"Protected work includes several categories:

- major mine / transport repair;
- large infrastructure;
- high-grade retained orders;
- security-related contracts where disclosure is restricted."

The last category changed the room.

Not because nobody suspected.

Because someone official had said it.

The skilled priority-line worker asked:

"Security for who?"

The clerk said:

"I cannot answer that."

A man near the back said:

"There it is."

The meeting nearly broke.

It did not.

That was important.

---

The compromise began with the safety inspector.

"Give me hours."

The owners looked at them.

"Total hours by shop and line. Not customer."

The worker representative said:

"And accidents."

"Injuries and stoppages."

One owner nodded slowly.

"That we can do."

The guild craft officer said:

"We can verify protected share by broad category."

The representative looked at them.

"Numbers?"

"Percent of scheduled capacity."

"By shop?"

"Yes."

"Duration?"

The contract clerk hesitated.

"Current committed period."

"Renewals?"

"Not customer detail."

"Existence of renewal options?"

The clerk thought.

"Possibly."

That was movement.

The market official said:

"I can track ordinary repair delay by class."

"Public?"

"Aggregate."

The small-shop woman said:

"I need actual orders."

"You know your actual orders."

"I need to know whether everybody else's are moving too."

"Aggregate tells you that."

She did not look convinced.

But she did not say no.

The worker representative began listing terms on the board.

Not demands.

Possible terms.

1. **safety / hour reporting** visible to workers and town inspector;
2. **protected-capacity share** verified by guild / town-recognized reviewer;
3. **ordinary repair floor** so designated public / safety-critical civilian repairs cannot all be displaced;
4. **overtime premium and refusal limits** beyond an agreed threshold;
5. **regular review date** rather than indefinite "temporary" priority;
6. **no public customer names** where confidentiality genuinely applies.

The room attacked each one.

That was progress too.

---

The hardest question was the ordinary repair floor.

Owners said:

"Who decides critical?"

The bridge clerk had returned.

They said:

"Bridge."

Someone shouted:

"Obviously."

A mill repairer shouted:

"Mill."

Someone else:

"Mine."

A town official:

"Water."

The worker representative looked at the board.

"If everything is critical, nothing is."

The mine representative said:

"Correct."

That was the first useful thing they had said.

The guild officer proposed:

- a rotating emergency-review group;
- limited authority;
- only for civilian repair orders displaced beyond a set delay.

The room hated the word group.

It hated the alternative more.

By the time lamps were being lit, they had something close to an agreement.

Not an agreement.

Close.

The owners would:

- provide aggregate protected-capacity figures;
- accept external safety / hour review;
- preserve a bounded civilian repair channel;
- negotiate overtime limits.

Workers would:

- not stop protected work during the review period;
- bring safety complaints through the agreed inspection process before a general stoppage where immediate danger did not exist;
- vote on the package the next evening.

The customer identities would remain confidential.

That was the part everyone disliked differently.

The worker representative stood beside the board after most people left.

The production supervisor came over.

"You can sell that?"

"To who?"

"Your people."

"They are not mine."

"You know what I mean."

"Yes."

"Can you?"

The representative looked at the terms.

"Maybe."

The supervisor laughed once.

"After four hours, maybe."

"You wanted certainty?"

"I wanted production."

"You have production."

"For now."

The representative looked toward the door.

Workers were leaving in small groups.

Some angry.

Some relieved.

Some doing arithmetic.

"That's all anybody has," they said.

---

The priority line ran the next morning.

So did the ordinary line.

The bridge hardware entered inspection.

The delayed mine-haulage repair stayed delayed.

The small neighboring shop brought back part of one shift after an ordinary buyer confirmed a new date.

Nothing was solved.

Enough had changed that a vote made sense.

The worker representative spent the afternoon collecting:

- safety-hour numbers;
- ordinary-delay examples;
- questions about the review group.

A younger worker from the priority line asked:

"If we pass it, do we find out who the security buyers are?"

"No."

"Then what's the point?"

"We find out how much of the shop they are taking."

"Same thing?"

"No."

"Feels like it."

The representative understood.

That was why the vote was not certain.

"Read the numbers," they said.

"Then decide."

The worker took the sheet.

"You're voting yes?"

The representative looked at the hall.

"Probably."

"Probably."

"It is an old political tradition."

The worker left.

The representative stayed.

The vote was tomorrow.

That was close enough to feel dangerous.

# V1 assessment

## Internal worker variation

**STRONG PASS.**

Workers include:

- priority-line workers benefiting from full wages;
- neighboring-shop workers losing hours;
- safety-focused workers;
- transparency-focused workers;
- workers suspicious of stopping valuable contracts.

No monolithic labor side.

## Owner / institution variation

**PASS.**

Owners have a real argument:

- protected work is also keeping production / wages alive while ordinary demand is uncertain.

Guild / town / safety actors have partial but incomplete authority.

## Allocation grievance

**STRONG PASS.**

The political question becomes:

> who gets to verify that "priority" is actually necessary, and how much ordinary life may it displace?

That is more sophisticated than:

> secret weapons bad.

## Near-settlement

**STRONG PASS.**

A plausible compromise now exists around:

- aggregate transparency;
- safety oversight;
- ordinary repair floor;
- overtime rules;
- review date;
- limited confidentiality.

Sequence 15 can damage something real.

## Security conversion

**PASS.**

The category:

> security-related contract

is now publicly acknowledged without identifying buyer / purpose.

That makes strategic allocation politically legible.

## Main risks

1. six-part proposed settlement may be too administratively tidy for one hall meeting;
2. the guild / town reviewer structure may accidentally canonize new offices;
3. the meeting has many anonymous speakers, which could blur voice;
4. the phrase "vote tomorrow" implies a worker decision procedure not yet owned by canon.

## Recommendation

Run a bounded challenge focused on institutional over-specificity.

The settlement needs enough structure to be endangered by Sequence 15 without accidentally establishing Stone Hills labor law.
