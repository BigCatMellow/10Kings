# Culture Phase 3 — Food Vertical Slices

## Status

**Reference / design tests — not canon.**

These slices test [Food](../Culture/Food.md) against the ordinary-life communities already used in [Culture Phase 2 — Ordinary-Life Vertical Slices](Culture-Phase-2-Vertical-Slices.md).

Concrete foods below are **provisional examples**. Their purpose is to test ecology, fuel, labor, preservation, class, trade, and current crises.

They are not regional signature dishes.

---

# 1. Stone Hills — workshop household

## Established anchors

- hardy grains, roots, legumes, dairy, livestock and lower-valley orchard goods;
- substantial Low Rivers food imports in industrial districts;
- metalwork / mining labor;
- attached workshops and mixed household economies;
- labor unrest.

## Provisional kitchen

A modest workshop household has:

- one main iron or ceramic pot;
- griddle / baking surface;
- knife;
- storage crock;
- access to a shared or household oven depending on neighborhood;
- inexpensive fuel from the local supply system.

The household does **not** cook elaborate roast meat every night simply because it lives near metalworkers.

## Ordinary workday food

Possible plain foods:

- grain porridge with leftover vegetables;
- bread with cheese or bean paste;
- thick bean / root pot;
- cold bread, onions, pickles and cheese carried to work;
- small amounts of preserved meat used to flavor a larger dish.

Names should remain literal until local naming history exists.

## Work and fuel

Legacy notes proposed cooking beside forge heat.

That is plausible for **some workshops**:

- a pot kept warm near a safe heat source;
- water heated during work;
- quick griddle food near industrial districts.

It should not become a romantic regional custom.

## Class contrast

A guild master can afford:

- finer flour;
- more meat;
- imported fruit;
- wine;
- specialty spices.

A casual laborer may depend heavily on:

- grain;
- legumes;
- onions / roots;
- cheese;
- employer or guild meals.

## Crisis test

During labor unrest:

- households stretch meat farther;
- protective gear and food compete for cash;
- shopkeepers extend credit;
- guild kitchens or strike kitchens become politically important;
- recruiters may include meals / lodging in employment offers.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

Food makes labor politics visible without requiring a speech about economics.

# 2. North Coast — harbor household

## Established anchors

- fish / shellfish;
- roots and hardy grains;
- pastoral dairy in some districts;
- berries;
- preserved meat;
- drying, salting, smoking, fermentation and cold storage;
- imported grain;
- shifting fish stocks.

## Provisional kitchen

A harbor household may depend on:

- hearth pot;
- smokehouse access;
- barrels / crocks;
- drying racks;
- cool storage;
- shared processing space.

Food production and preservation overlap with paid work.

## Ordinary food

Possible examples:

- grain porridge with bits of preserved fish;
- fish / root broth;
- bread or cakes with cultured dairy;
- rehydrated salt fish with vegetables;
- dried fish carried aboard;
- berries preserved or dried when available.

A major port household might instead eat imported bread, Old Cities oil, Low Rivers cheese, or Port-bought condiments.

## Meal timing

Tides and departures can matter more than conventional meal names.

A fisher may eat:

- something hot before departure;
- cold preserved food aboard;
- main cooked meal after return.

## Crisis test

As fish stocks decline:

- poorer households use more grain / roots;
- good preserved fish becomes more expensive;
- households sell premium catch rather than eat it;
- imported grain becomes politically sensitive;
- children notice that familiar food appears less often.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

The crisis changes the table before it becomes a formal political argument.

# 3. Low Rivers — mill / market household

## Established anchors

- broad grain, pulse, vegetable, dairy, egg, livestock and orchard base;
- mills;
- storage;
- river transport;
- estate, cooperative and market-town systems;
- current abundance / price crisis.

## Provisional food system

A river-market town may contain:

- millers;
- bakers;
- dairy sellers;
- vegetable stalls;
- meat sellers;
- brewers;
- communal or commercial ovens;
- storage houses.

Households buy different amounts of prepared food depending on work and fuel access.

## Ordinary food

Possible examples:

- coarse or fine bread depending on class;
- bean or pea soup;
- cheese and onions;
- dumpling / noodle / dough forms where local practice supports them;
- eggs;
- seasonal fruit;
- cooked greens;
- leftovers folded into filled bread;
- fresh milk near production zones, cultured / preserved dairy farther away.

## Important correction

Abundance does not mean everyone eats lavishly.

A tenant family can live in one of the world's richest agricultural regions and still:

- owe grain;
- sell the best produce;
- eat cheaper staples;
- carry debt.

## Current abundance crisis

A paradoxical scene can contain:

- bread cheaper than last year;
- farmer unable to pay debt;
- grain stacked in storage;
- livestock sold to cover loans;
- household reducing luxuries despite full markets.

That is much stronger than treating “harvest abundance” as automatic prosperity.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

# 4. High Roads — caravan-yard district

## Established anchors

- local hardy grains, legumes, dairy, upland livestock and roots;
- major imported-food circulation;
- caravan yards;
- inns;
- markets;
- fuel constraints in exposed areas;
- attacks disrupting routes.

## Three food systems in one district

### Local household food

Likely built around:

- reliable grains;
- dairy;
- legumes;
- roots;
- preserved meat.

### Traveler food

Needs to be:

- portable;
- quick;
- predictable;
- available near routes.

Possible forms:

- flat breads;
- filled breads;
- dumpling-like parcels;
- roasted / fried small foods;
- dried fruit / nuts;
- broth;
- skewered food where fuel and cost allow.

### Merchant / elite food

Can display access to:

- foreign fruit;
- wine;
- fish;
- fine flour;
- spices;
- sweets;
- specialty cheeses.

## Fuel logic

Efficient cooking matters.

Portable braziers, shared ovens or cooking several foods from one heat source are plausible where fuel is expensive.

Do not import a named real-world oven merely for aesthetic effect.

## Crisis test

Caravan attacks cause:

- imported foods disappearing unevenly;
- inns substituting local ingredients;
- salt / spices / dried fruit changing price;
- provisioning becoming more conservative;
- new routes introducing unfamiliar foods elsewhere.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

High Roads food is most distinctive through **the coexistence of local household food and traveler-market food**.

# 5. Longwood — river-town household

## Established anchors

- cultivation in clearings;
- nuts, mushrooms, greens, fish, game, domestic animals, berries and honey;
- imported grain / salt;
- river towns;
- forest-product economy;
- extraction conflict.

## Key correction

Foraging is a supplement, not a magical replacement for agriculture.

A town household may buy:

- grain;
- salt;
- dairy;
- meat;

while also using seasonal:

- mushrooms;
- greens;
- berries;
- nuts;
- river fish.

## Provisional cooking

Possible practical methods:

- pot cooking;
- smoking;
- drying;
- fermentation;
- leaf wrapping where the local vegetation supports it;
- ash / pit cooking where useful.

No method is spiritually “gentle” by default.

## Class / location contrast

Remote settlement:
- more direct access to forest and river foods;
- less imported grain;
- stronger preservation dependence.

River town:
- more market food;
- imported salt;
- grain;
- workers buying prepared meals.

Wealthy merchant:
- imported wine, spices and urban goods.

## Conflict test

Logging or road expansion can alter food by:

- muddying water;
- changing fish access;
- reducing gathering areas;
- opening cheap grain imports;
- bringing wage labor that leaves less time for household food collection.

Both gain and loss can happen simultaneously.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

# 6. Old Cities — irrigation-city neighborhood

## Established anchors

- grains;
- pulses;
- vegetables;
- fruit;
- nuts;
- oils;
- dairy;
- sheep / goat / poultry;
- coastal fish;
- dried fruit;
- irrigated agriculture;
- city-state variation;
- drought anxiety.

## Provisional city kitchen

A dense household may use:

- earthenware pots;
- griddle / oven access;
- brazier;
- water jars;
- market-prepared bread;
- preserved / dried ingredients.

Small urban households may purchase more cooked food than rural households.

## Ordinary foods

Possible forms:

- bread with oil or cheese;
- pulse stews;
- cooked vegetables;
- grain dishes;
- yogurt / cultured dairy where locally supported;
- nuts / dried fruit;
- grilled or fried market food;
- fish in coastal cities.

Do not assume the entire Old Cities uses the same herb, oil, bread or spice blend.

## Heat and work

Some cities may shift work / eating around the hottest period.

That remains a **local urban practice**, not universal regional canon.

## Water politics

Food can make drought visible through:

- rising cost of irrigated vegetables;
- pressure on water-intensive processing;
- poorer households buying drier / cheaper staples;
- resentment toward elite gardens or wasteful kitchens;
- argument over which crops receive water.

## Civic competition

Two neighboring cities can both eat grain + pulses + vegetables while still having strongly different cuisines because of:

- cooking fats;
- local water;
- bread type;
- market law;
- religious rules;
- port access;
- prestige foods.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

# 7. Port — dock boarding house

## Established anchors

- heavy food imports;
- docks;
- warehouses;
- immigrants;
- mixed families;
- street food;
- ship provisioning;
- crowding;
- shift work;
- refugees and displaced workers.

## Provisional household

A boarding-house kitchen serves:

- permanent family;
- two boarders;
- irregular sailor / relative guests;
- workers on different shifts.

The household cannot count on everyone eating together.

## Material constraints

Likely:

- small kitchen;
- limited storage;
- purchased fuel;
- nearby food stalls;
- cheap preserved goods;
- access to imported ingredients that may be individually affordable in small amounts.

## Ordinary Port food

Port-born food can emerge from practical urban conditions:

- one-pot meals stretched for changing headcount;
- filled breads;
- fried / griddled foods;
- cheap soups;
- preserved fish / meat;
- rice / grain / noodle forms where imports support them;
- condiments brought by migrant communities;
- leftovers repurposed for sale.

Exact staples remain open.

## What makes it Port food

Not that it combines six regional ingredients.

It becomes Port food because:

- Port-born cooks make it;
- Port workers buy it;
- the ingredients are available through Port markets;
- the method suits Port kitchens / streets;
- the name and social context become local.

A food can have foreign ancestry and still be fully Port-born.

## Crisis test

Refugee inflow / credit problems can:

- increase cheap food stalls;
- create new boarding-house foods;
- drive substitution;
- close established vendors;
- spread unfamiliar techniques;
- intensify arguments over “real Port food.”

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

# Cross-slice findings

## 1. Region is not a menu

The strongest distinctions come from:

- work;
- season;
- class;
- urban / rural setting;
- storage;
- market access;
- migration;
- religion.

Macro-region mainly sets the larger ecology and trade environment.

## 2. Preservation is infrastructure

Smokehouses, barrels, cellars, granaries, drying racks, ovens and warehouses can be politically important.

Preservation failure can create scarcity after a successful harvest or catch.

## 3. Prepared food should be common where households lack time or fuel

Best candidates:

- Port;
- High Roads caravan districts;
- large industrial towns;
- major river markets;
- busy harbors.

Street food is not a modern anomaly.

## 4. Food crises are often substitution crises

People rarely jump directly from “normal food” to starvation.

They first:

- buy cheaper cuts;
- reduce meat;
- substitute grain;
- sell premium food;
- preserve differently;
- eat fewer fresh foods;
- rely on public / guild / faith kitchens;
- take credit;
- change fuel use.

Those substitutions are excellent story texture.

## 5. Culinary borrowing needs a route

A borrowed food should be traceable through:

- migration;
- marriage;
- market;
- caravan;
- ship;
- pilgrimage;
- military service;
- refugee movement.

## 6. Food names must stay ordinary

The food system does not need:

- Frost Stew;
- Forge Bread;
- Forest Whisper Broth;
- Sunfire Pilaf.

It needs names equivalent to:

- bean pot;
- mill bread;
- north-market cakes;
- Miller's stew;
- dock soup;
- three-grain bread;
- old-market pie;

once the relevant local history actually supports them.
