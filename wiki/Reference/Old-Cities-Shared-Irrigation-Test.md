This test asks whether the mechanism found in the North Shoulder roadheads also appears in a non-transport system.

---

# 1. Plain condition

Two politically distinct Old Cities polities depend partly on the same managed water system.

The exact physical form remains open, but a strong test case is:

- a river or spring-fed headworks in / near the upstream polity;
- a main canal or managed channel crossing a political boundary;
- branch canals and storage serving both sides;
- downstream orchards, fields, neighborhoods, and workshops;
- maintenance structures that require repeated access.

The two polities are rivals.

They are not one water state.

---

# 2. Why the system crosses the border

The current Old Cities owner already supports:

- irrigation leagues;
- water law;
- canal gates;
- springs;
- river diversions;
- borders following waterworks;
- historical conflict over water.

The cross-border system can predate the current political boundary.

That is preferable to designing the boundary first and then adding a canal for symbolism.

A plausible sequence is:

older water system  
→ settlement expands around it  
→ political control changes  
→ border cuts across inherited infrastructure  
→ both successor polities still need the system

This is Writing the Sequel logic applied to political geography.

---

# 3. Stakeholders

The relevant constituency is not only two rulers.

## Upstream farmers / orchard holders

Need:
- predictable releases;
- canal maintenance;
- protection from downstream claims that consume all surplus.

## Downstream farmers / orchard holders

Need:
- reliable flow;
- warning before gates change;
- protection from upstream diversion.

## Town households

Need:
- drinking / washing water where connected;
- predictable public fountains / cistern refill;
- sanitation.

## Mill / workshop users

May depend on:
- flow;
- diversion timing;
- sediment management.

## Canal workers

Need:
- access across the system;
- labor agreements;
- safety;
- pay.

## Landowners

Care about:
- water rights;
- land value;
- inheritance;
- expansion.

## Local officials / irrigation associations

Need:
- records;
- schedules;
- maintenance;
- dispute resolution.

## Merchants / creditors

Care because harvest reliability affects:
- loans;
- contracts;
- food price;
- export.

## Religious / charitable institutions

May provide:
- mediation;
- emergency water;
- records;
- labor support.

These groups can prefer continued cooperation even when rulers prefer confrontation.

---

# 4. Water rights are not simply land ownership

A field can lie entirely inside one polity while its water depends on infrastructure outside it.

Therefore rights can be layered:

- land ownership;
- canal access;
- seasonal share;
- maintenance obligation;
- priority during drought;
- emergency household use.

This makes the political map and functional map diverge.

That is a strong worldbuilding result.

---

# 5. Shared maintenance creates unavoidable cooperation

Canals / headworks require repeated work.

Possible tasks:

- clearing silt;
- repairing gates;
- reinforcing banks;
- removing debris;
- inspecting leaks;
- maintaining diversion structures.

If maintenance on one side affects the other side, both polities need some process for:

- access;
- scheduling;
- cost sharing;
- labor responsibility;
- emergency repair.

That process can exist without political friendship.

---

# 6. Information matters

Water users need reliable information about:

- upstream flow;
- reservoir level;
- gate changes;
- canal damage;
- drought;
- flood;
- maintenance closure.

A downstream town can suffer before its ruler receives a diplomatic explanation.

Therefore the system naturally creates cross-border technical communication below high politics.

Possible carriers include:

- canal workers;
- irrigation associations;
- local officials;
- merchants;
- messengers;
- religious institutions.

---

# 7. Drought creates both cooperation and leverage

During ordinary years, the system may feel routine.

Drought reveals its politics.

Upstream actors can potentially:

- hold more water;
- delay release;
- prioritize their own agriculture.

Downstream actors can potentially:

- refuse maintenance payment;
- threaten trade retaliation;
- appeal to treaty / arbitration;
- sabotage or bypass infrastructure.

Therefore shared infrastructure creates **interdependence and coercive leverage at the same time**.

This is important.

Shared dependence does not automatically create peace.

---

# 8. The real stabilizer is a continuity constituency

Several groups on both sides lose if cooperation collapses.

Those actors may pressure their own rulers to preserve:

- minimum flow;
- maintenance access;
- warning procedures;
- dispute forums.

Their goal is not political union.

Their goal is:

> **keep the system functioning enough that ordinary life continues.**

This is more precise than "peace constituency."

---

# 9. Layered legal structure

The irrigation system fits the new Convergence model cleanly.

## Local law

Each polity can retain its own rules for:

- land;
- local branch canals;
- internal fees;
- inheritance;
- local water distribution.

## Attached agreement

The shared main infrastructure may require a specific cross-border agreement covering:

- maintenance;
- access;
- flow;
- emergency changes;
- dispute process.

## Convergence core

Wider obligations may matter if:

- one polity uses the system to attack recognized rights or territory;
- an attached agreement is treated as part of protected cross-border order;
- coercive water denial becomes a broader political crisis.

## Precedent

Repeated drought disputes can create accepted interpretations over time.

**Pass.**

The layered settlement model works outside commerce.

---

# 10. Ordinary-year politics

Most years should not feel like an international crisis.

Ordinary disputes can involve:

- who clears which section;
- whether one gate leaked;
- one village taking water early;
- who pays for a collapsed bank;
- whether measurement was honest.

These may be handled by:

- local irrigation officials;
- cross-border technical meetings;
- mediators;
- arbitration.

That keeps grand diplomacy out of daily water management.

---

# 11. Drought-year politics

Severe drought changes the scale.

Possible pressures:

- emergency rationing;
- upstream/downstream accusation;
- crop switching;
- orchard loss;
- urban demand competing with agriculture;
- wealthy landowners buying priority;
- illegal diversion;
- migration.

The shared system becomes politically visible because scarcity makes allocation explicit.

---

# 12. Sabotage accusation test

A gate breaks during drought.

Possible explanations:

- poor maintenance;
- flood debris;
- worker error;
- deliberate sabotage;
- false-flag sabotage;
- ordinary mechanical failure interpreted politically.

Before facts are known:

- rumors spread;
- downstream markets react;
- guards appear;
- workers may refuse cross-border access;
- rulers face pressure to retaliate.

The strongest stabilizing actors may be the people who know the infrastructure well enough to distinguish damage from attack.

**Strong pass.**

Technical expertise can restrain political escalation.

---

# 13. Unilateral upstream diversion test

The upstream polity begins a new diversion.

Possible positions:

## Upstream claim

The water originates in its territory and the diversion is lawful development.

## Downstream claim

The diversion violates established shared use / attached agreement.

Likely escalation:

technical complaint  
→ negotiation  
→ evidence over historical use  
→ arbitration  
→ commercial / political pressure  
→ targeted retaliation if unresolved

**Strong pass.**

The issue can be serious without requiring immediate war.

---

# 14. Maintenance strike test

Canal workers on one side stop work over wages or safety.

This is not automatically an international act.

Effects may still cross the border.

Possible responses:

- emergency local crews;
- support from counterpart workers;
- political pressure;
- employer concession;
- attempts to characterize the strike as foreign sabotage.

**Pass.**

Cross-border consequence does not erase local labor politics.

---

# 15. Flood test

A flood damages the shared headworks.

This can strengthen cooperation because:

- both sides need repair;
- blame is less central;
- costs are visible.

But disputes remain over:

- who pays;
- whose fields get emergency protection;
- which repair is first.

**Pass.**

Shared disaster can produce temporary cooperation without resolving rivalry.

---

# 16. Bypass test

Could one polity build an independent water system?

Possibly.

Options:

- new well / cistern;
- alternate canal;
- reservoir;
- changed crops;
- imported food.

But these may be expensive or slow.

The existence of alternatives affects leverage.

Important rule:

> dependence creates stronger cooperation pressure when exit is costly but not impossible.

If one side has an easy substitute, its continuity constituency weakens.

---

# 17. Asymmetry test

Suppose upstream can survive without downstream cooperation, but downstream cannot survive without upstream water.

Then the system may create:

- coercion;
- tribute;
- political domination;

more readily than mutual restraint.

Therefore Candidate 31 must not assume symmetry.

The stabilizing mechanism is strongest when:

- both sides bear meaningful costs from collapse;
- neither side can cheaply replace the system;
- enough local actors benefit from continuity.

This narrows the candidate usefully.

---

# 18. Cross-border social ties

Long-term shared infrastructure can produce:

- families of canal workers on both sides;
- professional vocabulary;
- reciprocal hiring;
- technical apprenticeship;
- marriages;
- shared practical festivals / maintenance seasons;
- old friendships among officials.

These ties may survive political hostility.

They are consequences of repeated function, not a "water culture" imposed from above.

---

# 19. Institutional memory

A functioning system needs records / memory about:

- old repairs;
- past drought allocations;
- gate settings;
- boundary markers;
- rights;
- exceptional emergency decisions.

That can reside in:

- local archives;
- irrigation associations;
- family records;
- religious archives;
- city offices.

Contested records can become political evidence.

---

# 20. Class politics

Shared infrastructure does not unite everyone equally.

A wealthy orchard owner may favor:

- expensive engineering;
- legal action;
- buying priority.

A tenant or smallholder may favor:

- minimum guaranteed household / field access;
- cooperative labor;
- debt relief.

A city household may prioritize drinking water over export agriculture.

Therefore a "pro-cooperation" coalition can still contain sharp class conflict.

---

# 21. Political manipulation

A ruler can gain support by claiming:

- the other city is stealing water;
- negotiators are weak;
- an old agreement was unfair;
- drought proves dependence is dangerous.

A merchant / landholder coalition can argue the opposite:

- conflict will destroy harvest and credit;
- technical cooperation is not surrender.

Both positions can be sincerely held.

---

# 22. Council relevance

Council-linked actors may try to stabilize the system because water failure threatens:

- food;
- credit;
- trade;
- urban order.

Possible tools:

- repair finance;
- emergency grain;
- quiet pressure;
- mediation support.

The Council should not own the canal.

Its advantage is seeing cross-domain consequences.

---

# 23. Villain relevance

The system offers manipulation opportunities:

- forged release records;
- sabotage;
- false sabotage accusations;
- rumors of secret diversions;
- funding a hardline faction;
- leaking genuine evidence selectively.

This is effective because the grievance can be real.

---

# 24. Wurdren-level relevance

A local story can matter through:

- protecting canal workers;
- carrying records;
- finding the actual cause of gate failure;
- helping a village during rationing;
- witnessing an illegal diversion.

The system is large, but evidence remains human-scale.

---

# 25. Breath seeds — non-canon

- Workers on each side claim the other side leaves gates harder to repair.
- An old boundary marker is used more often as a lunch seat than a legal marker.
- Children float twigs through side channels despite being repeatedly chased away.
- One city calls a particular maintenance week by a name the other side considers insulting.
- Older workers remember a drought allocation differently from the official archive.
- A downstream market proverb blames upstream weather for every bad price.
- One gate has been "temporary" for several generations.

None are canon.

---

# 26. Result for Candidate 31

The mechanism **does transfer** beyond transport.

But the test changes the best formulation.

Too broad:

> shared chokepoints create peace constituencies.

Stronger:

> **shared infrastructure can create cross-border continuity constituencies among people whose livelihoods depend on keeping a system functional.**

Those constituencies:

- can restrain escalation;
- can pressure rivals toward limited cooperation;
- do not eliminate rivalry;
- can coexist with coercive leverage;
- weaken when dependence is highly asymmetric or alternatives are cheap.

This is a stronger and more falsifiable rule.
