# 1. Core rule

The setting should contain many stories of creatures whose truth lies somewhere between:

- real animal;
- extinct animal;
- rare surviving population;
- misidentification;
- embellished encounter;
- ritual symbol;
- historical memory;
- hoax;
- local spirit;
- genuine supernatural being.

The setting should **not normally resolve which explanation is correct**.

Strong default:

> **people know the stories more confidently than they know the creatures.**

---

# 2. Why this fits the world

The world already establishes:

- scarce / uncertain magic;
- no dependence on supernatural infrastructure;
- disputed historical memory;
- local folk belief independent of formal religion;
- oral traditions;
- occupational superstition;
- local interpretation of strange animal behavior;
- legendary saints / heroes / spirits whose literal truth is unresolved.

A legendary-creature layer therefore does not require changing the setting's low-fantasy baseline.

It deepens it.

---

# 3. Avoid a conventional fantasy bestiary

Do not build:

> wolf  
> bear  
> goblin  
> troll  
> dragon  
> griffin  
> basilisk  
> etc.

as a universal catalog of objectively existing species.

That would change the setting's metaphysics and ecology dramatically.

Instead, build:

> **regional / occupational traditions about named creatures, with incomplete and contradictory evidence.**

A creature can be:

- famous without being common;
- culturally important without having existed;
- historically real but now extinct;
- still alive but badly described;
- one real encounter inflated into centuries of folklore.

---

# 4. The truth-spectrum model

Every legendary creature should receive an **author-side truth range**, even if the reader never does.

## Type A — ordinary animal, extraordinary story

Underlying truth may be:

- large predator;
- injured animal;
- unusual coloration;
- diseased animal;
- animal outside its normal range.

Over generations it becomes something else.

Example pattern:

~~~text
large wolf attacks travelers during famine
→ survivors emphasize unusual size / behavior
→ songs add intelligence / malice
→ later stories describe a distinct beast
~~~

The original event can be real even if the final creature is not.

---

## Type B — extinct / locally extinct animal

The creature may once have genuinely existed.

Possible evidence:

- bones;
- old hunting tools;
- cave images;
- place names;
- unusually consistent descriptions.

Modern people disagree whether:

- it vanished centuries ago;
- survives in remote pockets;
- was ever one species.

This is one of the strongest categories for the setting.

---

## Type C — rare relict population

A creature considered legendary may actually survive in:

- remote mountain valley;
- deep woodland;
- marsh;
- offshore island;
- Underpass system.

Sightings remain rare enough that:

- scholars argue;
- locals may know more;
- traders exaggerate;
- governments do not organize around it.

If confirmed, it should still behave ecologically like an animal.

---

## Type D — composite creature

Several real things become one legend.

Example pattern:

~~~text
large bird
+ mountain-cat attacks
+ falling-rock deaths
+ old ritual imagery
→ one winged cliff predator in folklore
~~~

This is especially useful where stories move between communities.

Different cultures may contribute different features.

---

## Type E — historical event personified as a beast

A creature story may encode:

- war;
- famine;
- epidemic;
- bandit confederation;
- mine collapse;
- flood;
- pirate fleet;
- abusive ruler.

Later listeners may debate whether a "beast" was:

- literal;
- metaphorical;
- a name for a group;
- both.

This fits contested historical memory extremely well.

---

## Type F — religious / spiritual being

The creature exists primarily inside:

- sacred narrative;
- pilgrimage;
- ancestor story;
- moral teaching;
- local theology.

Believers may consider it literal.

Others may see:

- personification;
- allegory;
- inherited symbol.

The setting does not need to decide.

---

## Type G — possibly genuinely supernatural

Rarest category.

Evidence may include:

- multiple independent witnesses;
- physical traces that do not fit normal explanation;
- recurring behavior over long periods;
- artifact / place associations;
- events that seem impossible but remain incompletely documented.

Even here:

> **uncertainty should survive.**

Do not let one confirmed supernatural animal force a global magical ecology unless deliberately intended.

---

# 5. The "somewhere in the middle" rule

The strongest legends should resist a binary:

> creature existed  
> versus  
> creature was invented.

Better:

> **something happened.**

Then ask:

- what happened physically?
- what did witnesses think happened?
- what did later generations need the story to mean?
- what did storytellers add?
- what evidence survived?
- which groups benefit from one interpretation?

Example author model:

~~~text
historical truth:
rare large cave predator existed

+

event:
several miners disappeared after structural collapse

+

social memory:
owners blamed "the beast" rather than unsafe supports

+

folk development:
miners turned the beast into a guardian that punishes greed

=

modern legend with several layers of truth
~~~

No single explanation invalidates the others.

---

# 6. Creatures should belong to communities, not regions

Do not assign:

> "North Coast monster"

as though every North Coast community shares one tradition.

Instead:

- one island may tell one version;
- neighboring harbor another;
- inland people may barely know the story;
- sailors may use a different name than priests;
- children may know a softened version;
- scholars may classify it under another legend entirely.

This follows the same cultural rule as the current analogue project:

> **region is not culture.**

---

# 6A. Shared creature, different cultures

A legendary creature does **not** need to belong to only one culture.

In fact, some of the strongest traditions should cross:

- language;
- region;
- profession;
- religion;
- trade route;
- migration network.

But the shared creature should **change** as it moves.

Example pattern:

~~~text
same underlying creature / event

North Coast fishers:
dangerous animal / weather sign

Port scholars:
named species reconstructed from records

Great Weaver communities:
local spirit / participant in ecological balance

Radiant Path storytellers:
moral parable about courage / restraint

High Roads carriers:
route omen associated with safe passage

children:
simplified cautionary tale
~~~

No version needs to be declared the single "correct" culture.

The creature becomes a cultural object as well as a possible animal / supernatural being.

## Variation dimensions

Different communities can disagree about:

- name;
- body shape;
- size;
- temperament;
- intelligence;
- sex / gender;
- whether it speaks;
- whether it is one being or a species;
- whether it is benevolent / hostile / indifferent;
- what it eats;
- where it lives;
- what behavior attracts it;
- what behavior drives it away;
- whether it is animal, spirit, ancestor, curse, omen, saintly sign, or metaphor;
- whether it still exists.

## Shared-core rule

If several cultures clearly refer to the same tradition, preserve **2–4 recurring anchors**.

Examples:

- same sound;
- same eye color;
- same season;
- same kind of track;
- same behavior before storms;
- same place-name cluster.

Everything else can vary.

This gives scholars / travelers a reason to suspect:

> these stories may share a historical core.

## Transmission rule

Ask how the story moved:

- migrating workers;
- sailors;
- caravan families;
- pilgrims;
- soldiers;
- refugees;
- guild branches;
- translated manuscripts;
- market storytellers;
- intermarriage.

Then change the story according to the receiving culture.

## Important consequence

The same creature can simultaneously be:

- sacred in one community;
- embarrassing superstition in another;
- occupational safety lore somewhere else;
- a children's story in Port;
- an active cryptid claim in a remote district.

This should be normal.

---

# 7. Occupational creatures

This world particularly benefits from creatures attached to work.

## Miners / tunnel workers

Stories may explain:

- bad air;
- rock sounds;
- collapses;
- missing workers;
- strange subterranean animals.

A creature may be:

- warning;
- scapegoat;
- taboo enforcer;
- practical hazard encoded as story.

## Sailors / fishers

Stories may arise from:

- whales;
- giant fish;
- squid;
- storms;
- phosphorescence;
- floating wreckage;
- fog.

## Foresters

Stories may arise from:

- rare predators;
- deer behavior;
- tree disease;
- missing travelers;
- old boundary customs.

## Caravan / road communities

Stories may attach to:

- passes;
- lights;
- scavengers;
- bandit traps;
- mirages;
- abandoned roads.

## River / marsh communities

Stories may attach to:

- large fish;
- drowned animals;
- flood debris;
- disease;
- lights over wetlands.

These should differ even inside one faith / region.

---

# 8. Creature stories can preserve real knowledge

Like taboo:

~~~text
real hazard
→ repeated story
→ supernatural explanation
→ practical survival behavior survives
~~~

Examples of class:

- never camp in a particular hollow;
- leave a mine when a certain sound begins;
- avoid one shoal after a specific tide;
- do not follow lights into marsh ground;
- do not drink where a "river beast" nests.

The explanation may be false.

The behavior may still save lives.

---

# 9. Creature stories can also conceal wrongdoing

Not every legend deserves reverence.

A creature may be used to explain away:

- disappearances;
- murder;
- unsafe work;
- smuggling;
- forced displacement;
- disease caused by pollution;
- military violence.

Later investigation may show:

> **the monster story was partly true and partly convenient.**

This is especially useful for:

- Council-era historical disputes;
- mine / industrial history;
- border violence;
- Port / piracy stories.

---

# 10. Extinction should matter

Some legendary creatures should almost certainly be remembered animals that are gone.

Possible causes:

- habitat loss;
- hunting;
- climate shift;
- disease;
- expanding settlement;
- industrial extraction;
- deliberate eradication after attacks.

This creates myths with ecological consequence.

Communities may disagree whether extinction was:

- progress;
- tragedy;
- necessary defense;
- sacrilege.

A faith like the Great Weaver could interpret the same extinct creature very differently from a mining or farming community.

---

# 11. Skeptics and believers should both have evidence

For every major creature tradition, build:

## Believer evidence

- old witness account;
- track;
- bone;
- song;
- relic;
- repeated location;
- family testimony.

## Skeptic explanation

- known animal;
- hoax;
- bad record;
- mistranslation;
- metaphor;
- geological / weather phenomenon;
- political convenience.

## Unresolved remainder

At least one fact neither explanation handles perfectly.

That is the part that keeps the legend alive.

---

# 12. Relationship to magic

Current [Magic Baseline](../Magic-Baseline.md) says:

> **magic is scarce, uncertain, limited, or costly.**

Therefore legendary creatures should not become:

- routine magical fauna;
- monster-hunting industry;
- common combat encounters;
- standard ingredient economy.

If one creature appears to be supernatural, that should be:

- rare;
- culturally destabilizing;
- disputed;
- difficult to reproduce / verify.

A traveler seeing something impossible should not automatically know what category of magic it belongs to.

---

# 13. Relationship to religion

Formal faiths can:

- adopt creature legends;
- reinterpret them;
- condemn them;
- use them in parables;
- declare them saints' companions;
- classify them as spirits;
- insist they are only metaphors.

Different branches of the same faith may disagree.

A local creature tradition does not automatically become universal theology.

---

# 14. Relationship to scholarship

Scholars should have their own disagreements.

Possible positions:

- creatures are misidentified fauna;
- some are extinct species;
- oral consistency indicates historical cores;
- religious sources contaminate natural evidence;
- old bones have been misclassified;
- categories like "dragon" combine unrelated animals.

Museums / archives / collectors may preserve:

- bones;
- hides;
- sketches;
- eggshell;
- alleged teeth;
- expedition journals.

Some are genuine.

Some are frauds.

Some are genuine objects attached to the wrong legend.

---

# 15. Legacy material already points toward this layer

The old sources contain fragments rather than a mature system.

Useful surviving ideas include:

- mythical sky-creatures;
- unusual giant birds / fantasy livestock as brainstorming;
- ancient monster / champion stories;
- natural spirits and animal-linked religious parables;
- lost civilizations / strange beings;
- supernatural-creature brainstorming that current Magic Baseline intentionally did not canonize.

The correct salvage is **not** to restore a generic fantasy fauna list.

The correct salvage is:

> **retain the possibility that old stories remember strange creatures while rebuilding each case under the current uncertainty / ecology rules.**

---

# 16. Recommended creature portfolio

A believable world does not need dozens immediately.

Initial target:

## 2–3 continent-wide famous legends

Stories almost everyone has heard, but versions differ dramatically.

These may connect to:

- the Spine;
- the Three Moons;
- ancient catastrophe;
- early legendary history.

Do not confirm their truth.

## 1–2 strong creature traditions per major ecological zone

Examples of zones:

- Spine / Underpass;
- North Coast / islands;
- Low Rivers / wetlands;
- Longwood;
- High Roads;
- Old Cities drylands / irrigation;
- Port / estuary.

These need not map one-to-one to regions.

## 1 creature tradition per major mobile circuit

Nomadic / itinerant peoples should carry stories that:

- move;
- change;
- merge local traditions.

## Several hyperlocal creatures

Known only to:

- one mining town;
- one island;
- one forest community;
- one road inn;
- one family.

These create more realism than making every legend world-famous.

# 17. Creature-profile template

Each future legendary creature should record:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Names | What do different communities call it? |
| Geography | Where is the story actually told? |
| Earliest evidence | Oldest song, bone, record, carving, legal case? |
| Description | What features recur? |
| Variation | What changes between communities? |
| Claimed behavior | What does it supposedly do? |
| Practical effect | What behavior does the story encourage? |
| Religious interpretation | Which faiths / branches care, if any? |
| Historical interpretation | What old event may sit underneath it? |
| Natural explanation | What known / extinct animal or phenomenon could explain it? |
| Supernatural possibility | What remains hard to explain? |
| Political use | Who benefits from believing / denying it? |
| Current sightings | Recent? rare? none? |
| Author truth range | A–G, possibly mixed |
| Canon status | research / provisional / established |

---

# 18. Design principle

The creature should remain interesting even if the supernatural explanation is false.

Test:

> If the creature never literally existed, does the legend still reveal something important about the people who tell it?

And:

> If the creature did exist, does that discovery complicate the legend rather than simply prove every story true?

If both answers are yes, the creature is strong enough.

---

# 19. Recommended next step

Before inventing names / creatures, perform a **mythic-creature slot audit** against:

- existing ecosystems;
- Underpass;
- Three Moons / Spine cosmology;
- regional / community culture work;
- old historical memories;
- religious traditions.

Then build only the first **8–12 high-value legends**.

Recommended spread:

- 2 Spine / Underpass;
- 2 maritime;
- 1 river / marsh;
- 2 woodland;
- 1 high-road / highland;
- 1 dryland / irrigation;
- 1 Port / estuary;
- 1 mobile-circuit legend;
- optionally 1 widespread primordial legend.

This is enough to establish the layer without turning the setting into a monster manual.
