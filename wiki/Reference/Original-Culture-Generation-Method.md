# Original Culture Generation — From Constraints to Living Institutions

## Status and authority

**NON-CANON method and worked design prototypes (2026-11-04).** Responds to the direction to **learn from obscure cultural systems and then invent new societies, not mash together borrowed cultures**. The [Obscure Cultural Systems Dossiers](Obscure-Cultural-Systems-Dossiers.md) and earlier [Cultural Practices](Cultural-Practices-and-Institutions.md) are research inputs, not a palette of names, costumes or recognizable rituals. [World Rules](../World-Rules.md), [Cultural Inspiration](../Culture/Culture-and-Influence.md), and specific established owners outrank every example here.

**Definition:** A cultural institution is original enough for this project when it arises from a Two Sons-specific physical and historical problem; has believable labor, knowledge, law and economics; changes through contact; and is *not* identifiable by simply renaming a real tradition.

## The invention loop

1. **Pick a micro-place and problem.** One watershed, dock neighborhood, seasonal camp, upland pass, workshop street or island. Avoid giving an entire region a uniform tradition.
2. **Specify material constraints.** Rainfall, daylight, stone, crops, animals, boats, road capacity, labor, spoilage, writing/record costs, available preindustrial technology.
3. **Identify recurring dependencies.** Who relies on whom every day, season or generation? Who can abandon an obligation, who cannot, and what happens if the system fails?
4. **Invent the social solution without a borrowed surface form.** Examples: an unusual contract, shared maintenance day, market entitlement, dispute hearing, teaching practice, or record of risk.
5. **Derive expressive culture second.** Only after the institution works should a distinctive song, proverb, dress detail, game, ceremony or architecture appear; trace it to *actual repeated acts*.
6. **Make social differences.** Landowner vs tenant, elder vs student, seasonal visitor vs resident, different faiths, recent migrants, opposing local interests.
7. **Add history and change.** An earlier disaster, disputed reform, migration, ruler's policy, union of small communities, changing technology or contested memory.
8. **Run a counterfactual.** If you remove the reference tradition's name, art style and ceremonial form, can the fictional system still be explained from its local conditions?
9. **Fail it deliberately.** Describe an accident, drought, court ruling, marriage, flood, theft or market change and follow consequences for at least three unrelated groups.
10. **Reconcile before canonization.** Confirm geography, active ownership, local naming, technology and timeline with the current wiki owner. Until then, proposals remain test cases.

## Anti-collage test

Reject or rework a draft if:
- it is **“Culture A + Culture B”** with a list of decorative objects;
- its entire population has the same temperament or occupation;
- it reproduces a named sacred practice or origin story;
- local material demands cannot support the custom;
- there are no economic costs, teachers, dissenters, or plausible reasons to stop doing it;
- the same practice appears unchanged after three generations of migration;
- its main interest is how 'exotic' it looks;
- one unique custom explains all politics, religion, work and cuisine.

Prefer one well-explained local institution over a dozen named tribes.

## Prototype A — The Shared-Delay Ledger

**Test location:** A river-and-road transfer town near, but not fixed at, the Greenvale/Highridge economic interface. Geography and route details **unconfirmed**.

**Problem:** Freight arriving from different routes waits for labor, boats, drying space and inspection. Storms make everyone blame someone else for spoilage and losses.

**Invented institution:** Each transfer crew tallies **days of delay suffered and days of labor saved for others** on durable paired tally sticks kept separately by a dock association and a market hall. These are not money and do not erase debts. The tallies establish priority for future emergency labor and public hearings about route failures. The system is deliberately coarse: exact arithmetic is disputed in bad weather, records can be manipulated, and powerful merchants press for priority exceptions.

**Why culture emerges:** A monthly open sorting of mismatched tallies becomes a crowded neighborhood event. Children learn to identify old flood years from the shape of repairs on the sticks. 'You only know the bridge from the waiting side' becomes a local saying, but not a regional character trait.

**People and tensions:** Fisher households, pack-animal workers, porters, shopkeepers, passing caravans and municipal officials benefit differently; the town's wealthier traders can hire clerks while others rely on witnesses. The dock association may exclude seasonal workers.

**Contact and change:** Migrant dock workers introduce a competing stamped-mark record; a younger faction wants standardized written ledgers, elders argue tampering becomes easier with centralized custody.

**Failure test:** A washed-out ford causes an emergency. Labor is redirected from a local fish harvest to unloading grain. Spoilage triggers a lawsuit, a tax shortfall and an argument over who had a valid tallied claim.

**Research stimulus, not copy:** Portable records (khipu, printing genealogies), seasonal route negotiation (Yaaral/Degal), but **the tally institution follows this particular transfer economy**, not their outward design.

**Current owners to review:** [Movement](../Movement-and-Information.md), [Trade](../Economy/Trade-and-Dependencies.md), [Records](../Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md), [Law](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

**Unresolved:** Whether such a transfer junction exists; cost/availability of writing; property jurisdiction; whether trade guilds have authority.

## Prototype B — The Last-Light Apprentices

**Test location:** A cluster of steep, weather-exposed foothill communities near Ironcrest/Highridge; **no named settlement established**.

**Problem:** Repairing retaining walls and water routes requires skilled inspection, but adult craftspeople are away for seasonal work and repairs often become urgent at dusk.

**Invented institution:** In the hour before light fades, older workers take several learners on a **walk of weaknesses**, calling out where stone seams, water lines, hoof-damage and small slips reveal future collapse. Learners take turns proposing a fix, while others challenge the diagnosis. This is practical education, not a sacred chant or a 'secret technique'. Only a licensed builder may approve dangerous repairs.

**Why culture emerges:** Disagreement becomes stylized into quick rhythmic call-and-response *speech*, timed to make group inspection memorable. Families use versions of the questions to teach children ordinary care (doors, roofs, carts) without invoking the trade itself.

**People and tensions:** A traveling master charges wages; residents complain that the repair guild teaches apprentices who then leave; a widowed tenant has sharp observational knowledge but no formal license; the seasonal labor workforce is divided over who pays for walls.

**Contact and change:** Repair crews from another valley begin marking danger points with painted chips, reducing reliance on memory. The old timed walk persists as a community social event, though professional builders increasingly use records.

**Failure test:** A novice flags a crack in a wall holding a market road. The guild dismisses the observation; a collapse injures a caravan animal and interrupts winter food deliveries. Was the novice right, and who was allowed to stop traffic?

**Research stimulus:** Dry-stone craft apprenticeships and layered oral performance traditions. **Nothing in the original artistic or construction designs is copied.**

**Owners:** [Architecture](../Culture/Architecture.md), [Education](../Education-and-Knowledge-Transmission.md), [Guilds](../Politics/Guilds.md).

**Unresolved:** Relevant terrain, building materials, what constitutes licensing, maintenance authority.

## Prototype C — The Three-Bowl Shore

**Test location:** A small Northwind-associated coastal settlement with fishing, drying racks and periodic visiting boats. **Not a rule for Northwind as a whole.**

**Problem:** Wind shifts and uncertain catches cause households to hoard food just before storms. People owe one another help but cannot always measure it in coins.

**Invented institution:** A public drying house maintains three practical categories of preserved catch: **owner's stock, reserved voyage stock, and a weather reserve**. Families negotiate the size of the weather reserve each season according to boat damage and winter forecasts; contributions can be labor, fuel or fish. The division is not a mystical 'three bowls': it arose from counting storage bays in the first building, and the old name survived after expansion.

**Why culture emerges:** Newly launched boats receive a small portion of the reserve only in specific circumstances; disputes about rescue effort and obligation become stock themes of satirical dockside storytelling. A local saying condemns people who promise help only once the wind has dropped.

**People and tensions:** Boat owners, crew members, fish sellers, inland buyers, children on shore work and households with no boats all have differing claims. A family with an injured crewmember can contribute labor intermittently, testing whether 'fairness' means equal shares or equal sacrifice.

**Contact and change:** A visiting merchant offers a fixed grain exchange for the emergency stock. Younger households favor selling more; older rescuers argue against removing the reserve.

**Failure test:** Several boats shelter in a neighboring inlet after a storm. The reserve is released for a missing crew, but the fish was already promised to an inland mill's workers. Rescue ethics collide with contract enforcement and food scarcity.

**Research stimulus:** Bedu community knowledge and shared resource logic, maritime technology, reciprocal obligations. **Not a copy of a sea-nomad ceremony or regional stereotype.**

**Owners:** [Food](../Culture/Food.md), [Northwind](../Regions/Northwind.md), [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md), [Law](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

**Unresolved:** Winter preservation economics, salt/fuel supply, settlement's current civic law.

## Prototype D — The Returned-Question Schools

**Test location:** A mixed-language caravan stop on Highridge or another plausible inland corridor; **site not chosen**.

**Problem:** Visitors need to learn road hazards and local rights fast, but maps age badly, language varies, and a misleading guide may be well-intentioned.

**Invented institution:** Traveling specialists teach a short practical course by requiring each newcomer to **ask a local question and return with an answer verified by someone from a different occupation**. Completing it grants no official privilege, but earns a locally recognized introduction for lodging and work. Records are short and explicitly expire with changing routes and seasons.

**Why culture emerges:** People become proud of unusual questions, and a lively culture of trick questions and embarrassing public corrections grows around the course. The most famous stories celebrate *corrected false certainty*, not omniscient teachers. Some residents mock the program as a toll gate for outsiders.

**People and tensions:** Caravan drivers, interpreters, shepherds, road maintenance workers, innkeepers, pilgrims and merchants supply different knowledge. Families with local connections can satisfy the requirement more easily, leading reformers to demand accessible alternatives.

**Contact and change:** A Port trader introduces written course tokens; some useful questions are archived, while most seasonal answers become obsolete and must be retested.

**Failure test:** A young interpreter passes the exercise by trusting two merchants who share the same bad source. The resulting wrong route harms a herd; the community argues whether the problem was cheating, mistaken certification or a broken verification rule.

**Research stimulus:** Mahadra oral education, whistled language as specialized expertise, navigational pedagogy, social information networks. **No school layout, religion, recitation or maritime chart is copied.**

**Owners:** [Education](../Education-and-Knowledge-Transmission.md), [Movement](../Movement-and-Information.md), [Language](../Language-Families-and-Contact.md).

**Unresolved:** Who pays teachers, what recognition means legally, travel times and existing guild certifications.

## Prototype E — The Open-Repair Season

**Test location:** An orchard-and-workshop district affected by intermittent flooding and a mixed migrant workforce; **no fixed region/city**.

**Problem:** Repeated small failures—canals, carts, kilns, food-storage roofs—cause cascading damage. Guild specialists are too expensive for every family, yet unskilled repairs can create safety hazards.

**Invented institution:** Once per year, the district schedules several weeks of **supervised common repairs**. Households contribute a fixed number of labor days or materials according to means; masters certify only hazardous work; simple parts are standardized to be repairable by trained residents. A public sign distinguishes a temporary repair from an inspected permanent one.

**Why culture emerges:** The season produces temporary work teams, songs about famous failures, communal meals to support crews, and local contests for clever tool reuse. Residents argue about what is good craftsmanship and what is dangerous improvisation. A public roll of unfinished repairs becomes political ammunition.

**People and tensions:** Small landowners, renters, repair guild members, apprentices, elderly households, religious charities and merchants all have distinct incentives. A powerful mill owner can dodge common labor through contracted substitutes; poor workers are at risk of taking the dangerous jobs.

**Contact and change:** Migrant craftspeople introduce unfamiliar joint designs but adaptation must fit local timber and tools. A neighboring district adopts only the repair-labeling system and never the common-work schedule.

**Failure test:** A certified bridge inspection reveals the 'temporary' repair from last year was never replaced. A drought reduced timber supply, a mill needs deliveries, and a rival guild offers cheaper work. A court must determine responsibility for the missing labor.

**Research stimulus:** Wooden bridge craftsmen, yurt-making supply networks, the material demands of transhumance, and irrigation systems. **The actual district institution is derived from repeated repair failures and negotiated labor.**

**Owners:** [Architecture](../Culture/Architecture.md), [Guilds](../Politics/Guilds.md), [Law](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md), [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md).

**Unresolved:** Calendar, guild powers, emergency labor obligations, exact materials.

## Before promotion to the active wiki

- Check real terrain, seasonal economics, available technologies and laws.
- Confirm an owner for the local institution and assign **Provisional**, **Working canon** or **Established** only through an explicit reconciliation.
- Check existing current wiki and history; do not revive superseded naming or geography.
- Develop at least two opposed perspectives from members of the same community.
- Specify one plausible migration/contact event and one reason the tradition changed.
- Test one grounded scene featuring ordinary life, not only lore explanation.
- Remove reference-case names from the fictional end product; keep inspiration attribution in provenance.
