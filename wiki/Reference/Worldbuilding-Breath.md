# Worldbuilding Breath

## Status

**Writing reference — not setting canon.**

This page is guidance for using the existing lore on the page without adding unnecessary new encyclopedic material.

Related owner: [World Rules](../World-Rules.md), especially the rule that the world exists when protagonists are absent.

## The problem

A world can be thoroughly documented and still feel staged.

The missing layer is often not more macro-lore. It is evidence that ordinary life was already in motion before the scene began and will continue after it ends.

The question to ask is:

> What was happening here yesterday that has nothing to do with the plot?

## Seven texture layers

### 1. Contested truth

Official history and local memory should not align perfectly.

Examples:

- a university account of The Convergence versus a dockside family story;
- one artifact with several incompatible legends;
- a border town using a name historians say is wrong.

This is ordinary disagreement, not necessarily conspiracy.

See [Contested Historical Memory](../History/Contested-Memory.md).

### 2. What people are arguing about right now

Readers should sometimes arrive mid-conversation.

Useful background:

- a tax or fee changed last month;
- a road is being rerouted;
- a guild election is ugly;
- an envoy caused a scandal;
- a bridge repair is late;
- a new song is already annoying everyone.

The issue does not need to become a plot hook.

### 3. What has faded

Time becomes visible when characters remember things that are no longer current.

Examples:

- old slang;
- yesterday's fashion;
- a vanished shop;
- a route people stopped using;
- the song everyone hated five years ago;
- a public official younger people barely remember.

Wurdren's age makes this layer especially available.

### 4. Wrong information that circulates anyway

People in the field rarely possess the author's full model.

Use:

- folk remedies that fail;
- simplified history everyone repeats;
- incorrect theories about the Council;
- rumors that notice a real pattern but explain it badly.

Wrong information is strongest when it is plausible enough to survive.

### 5. Specifics with no plot relevance

A few concrete details imply a much larger world:

- the local price of bread;
- one tavern charging more than another;
- a lane named after someone no one remembers;
- a bridge that washed out years ago and is still “on the list”;
- a regional unit or bureaucratic form mentioned without explanation.

Do not force these details to pay off later.

### 6. Off-camera relationships

Minor people should have lives that do not orbit the protagonists.

A clerk has a sibling in another guild. A guildmaster's daughter is dating someone politically inconvenient. Two junior operatives have not spoken in years for a reason the story never investigates.

The lack of plot utility is part of the effect.

### 7. Mundane bureaucratic friction

Annoyance is intimate.

Forms are copied twice. Customs delays a shipment. One office says the stamp belongs to another office. A permit that should take a morning consumes three days.

This turns institutions into lived experience.

## Scene checklist

A chapter does not need all of these. Two or three quiet touches are often enough.

- [ ] Is there a rumor, folk belief or “everyone knows” claim that may be wrong?
- [ ] Does someone reference something that used to be common?
- [ ] Is there a current grievance mentioned in passing?
- [ ] Does an official account meet a conflicting personal memory?
- [ ] Are there concrete prices, distances, forms or named places that do not need exposition?
- [ ] Do minor characters have off-screen relationships?
- [ ] Is there ordinary bureaucratic or logistical friction?

## Guardrail

Do **not** respond to this method by creating fifty more canonical lore pages.

The existing wiki owns what the world **is**. Breath is primarily a prose technique for revealing that material incompletely, locally and incidentally.
