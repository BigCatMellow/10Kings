# 2. Social epistemology — knowledge is institutional and relational

## Source-derived point

OpenStax's social epistemology section emphasizes that human knowledge is rarely produced by isolated individuals.

People rely on:

- testimony;
- experts;
- inherited records;
- teachers;
- books;
- institutions;
- collective deliberation.

That dependence makes knowledge possible at scale.

It also creates a problem:

> the reliability of what a group "knows" depends partly on whose testimony it trusts and how its knowledge-producing processes are organized.

The source also discusses disagreement among epistemic peers and the possibility of attributing beliefs to groups.

## 10Kings application

This is extremely useful for the Council and Three Moons.

The Council's advantage is not that each member personally knows everything.

It has:

> **a superior process for combining testimony from several systems.**

Port gains similar power when guilds, merchants, sailors, clerks, and political representatives gather there.

That means information power can emerge from **network position and institutional process**, not secret omniscience.

It also provides a failure mode:

> a highly sophisticated information system can still produce bad knowledge if it systematically trusts the wrong people.

# 3. Standpoint epistemology — position changes what can be known

## Source-derived point

The text presents standpoint epistemology as a response to the way social power affects:

- who is treated as an expert;
- whose perspective is solicited;
- which observations enter collective knowledge.

Its practical example is organizational:

> managers attempting to understand a workplace can miss important knowledge if they exclude workers whose daily position gives them access to different evidence.

## 10Kings application

This is almost tailor-made for the project's three-scale narrative.

### Council

It can possess continental information while still missing:

- what a dock rule does to a laborer;
- what a route closure means to one town;
- what a debt restructuring means to a family;
- what a "temporary" access rule means to a Longwood community.

### Wurdren

Wurdren's value is not that he knows more than the Council.

He repeatedly encounters **knowledge the Council's position makes hard to see**.

This gives philosophical grounding to the existing structure:

> Council = system view  
> Villain = strategic view  
> Wurdren = lived view

None is complete.

# 4. Epistemic injustice — power can decide whose knowledge counts

## Source-derived point

OpenStax summarizes Miranda Fricker's distinction between:

- **testimonial injustice** — a person's or group's testimony is unfairly discounted or treated as untrustworthy;
- **hermeneutical injustice** — the available language / concepts do not adequately capture a person's experience, making that experience harder to understand or communicate.

The broader section also includes:

- silencing;
- systematic misrepresentation;
- unfair assignment of expert status.

## 10Kings application

This can become a major story mechanism rather than merely a theme.

Examples:

- island fishers' testimony about changing waters is dismissed as self-interest;
- a local rights-holder lacks the legal vocabulary recognized by an outside contract;
- dockworkers identify a logistics pattern before officials do but are treated as rumor sources;
- a guild's official report becomes "what the guild believes" even when branches disagree;
- the Council has excellent records of prices but weak language for the social harm created by its intervention.

This also strengthens Wurdren's counter-domino function.

Sometimes his contribution can be:

> **getting the person who knows the thing into a position where they are actually heard.**

# 5. Act versus rule utilitarianism — institutional trust is itself a consequence

## Source-derived point

OpenStax distinguishes:

- **act utilitarianism** — evaluate each act by whether it maximizes good;
- **rule utilitarianism** — evaluate rules by whether general adherence to them produces better outcomes.

The source uses examples in which an apparently welfare-maximizing individual act could:

- punish an innocent person;
- encourage vigilantism;
- undermine trust in the judicial system.

## 10Kings application

This is especially useful for the Council.

A Council member may rationally say:

> this exception prevents a famine.

But a system made of secret exceptions can eventually destroy:

- trust;
- predictable law;
- equal standing;
- political legitimacy.

The relevant calculation is therefore not only:

> Did this intervention work?

It is also:

> What kind of institution exists if this becomes a normal way of acting?

This fits the existing **intervention afterlife** rule.

# 6. Deontology / Kant — some constraints are about what may be done to a person

## Source-derived point

OpenStax presents Kantian ethics through universalizable maxims and the idea that humans should be treated as **ends in themselves**, not merely as means.

The moral test is therefore not reducible to aggregate outcome.

## 10Kings application

This gives a direct test for:

- Council triage;
- Villain manipulation;
- emergency powers;
- strategic sacrifice.

Ask:

> Is this person still being treated as someone whose own purposes matter, or only as a useful cost inside another actor's plan?

# 7. Virtue ethics — moral life can be about character, not only decisions

## Source-derived point

The text defines virtue ethics as a normative approach centered on **character**.

Its review material connects Aristotelian virtue with human flourishing and also treats Confucian practice / relationships as part of moral development.

## 10Kings application

This is particularly useful for Wurdren.

His philosophical role does not need to be:

> possess the correct theory.

It can be:

> repeatedly become the kind of person who acts well under uncertainty.

That fits a protagonist whose importance accumulates through:

- habits;
- relationships;
- trust;
- courage;
- judgment;
- willingness to listen.

It also makes his small acts philosophically distinct from the system-level calculations of the other two forces.

# 8. Normative versus descriptive legitimacy

## Source-derived point

OpenStax explicitly distinguishes:

- **descriptive legitimacy** — explaining why people accept or obey an authority;
- **normative legitimacy** — asking whether the authority is actually justified.

The text summarizes Weber's traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal sources of descriptive legitimacy.

It also notes the instability of charismatic legitimacy because it is attached strongly to a person rather than a durable office.

## 10Kings application

This distinction is essential.

The Council may have:

- enormous compliance;
- reputation;
- expertise;
- practical necessity.

None of those by itself answers:

> **Does it have the right to rule?**

Likewise the Villain may acquire mass support.

That shows political power and perhaps descriptive legitimacy.

It does not automatically justify his methods or his authority.

This is also useful for the century-later ending:

> the Villain's charisma can die while institutions descended from his movement become rational-legal and routine.

That is one way a revolutionary founder can stop "owning" the future.

# 9. Social contract — protection and obligation are reciprocal questions

## Source-derived point

The political-philosophy chapter uses Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau to frame government as a problem of:

- security;
- consent;
- rights;
- obligation.

It asks both:

- what citizens owe political institutions;
- and what those institutions owe citizens.

## 10Kings application

This helps frame the Convergence as a **settlement with reciprocal purposes**, not sacred ancient law.

If Port's restrictions were justified because they protected:

- peace;
- access;
- independence;
- shared use;

then later generations can ask whether the arrangement still performs those functions fairly.

That makes constitutional reform philosophically intelligible without requiring the Convergence's founders to have been wrong.

# 10. Communitarianism — people are socially embedded, not abstract units

## Source-derived point

OpenStax presents communitarianism as emphasizing that:

- identities and values arise within social environments;
- communities create obligations;
- shared practices and norms matter;
- rights and responsibilities must be considered together.

It also presents communitarian criticism of overly abstract models that imagine people detached from their actual social positions.

## 10Kings application

This helps prevent the successor settlement from becoming a purely procedural abstraction.

People are not only:

- rights-holders;
- taxpayers;
- contract parties.

They are also members of:

- neighborhoods;
- guild branches;
- religious communities;
- kin networks;
- ports;
- fishing communities;
- route towns.

A formally fair rule can still destroy a community whose value is not captured in a market calculation.

This is useful when testing:

- Port centralization;
- guild standardization;
- infrastructure decisions;
- the long-term successor order.

# 11. Civil disobedience and moral obligation beyond the state

## Source-derived point

OpenStax uses Gandhi to illustrate the idea that moral obligations to people can exist independently of an obligation to obey a government.

In the source's presentation, resistance is constrained by a commitment to non-harm rather than treated as unlimited permission once the state is judged unjust.

## 10Kings application

This provides a strong contrast to the Villain.

Both can hold:

> the existing political order is unjust.

The philosophical divergence is:

> **what limits remain binding while resisting it?**

This makes the Villain's moral boundary a real question rather than a choice between action and passivity.

# 12. Negative and positive liberty — Port's problem is not only interference

## Source-derived point

Using Isaiah Berlin, OpenStax distinguishes:

- **negative liberty** — absence of obstruction / interference;
- **positive liberty** — being one's own master and having meaningful control over one's life.

## 10Kings application

This is one of the strongest conceptual tools for Port.

Port can possess substantial **negative liberty**:

- no foreign ruler directly governs it;
- no one power owns the harbor;
- its neutrality protects it from overt capture.

Yet it can still lack **positive liberty** if it cannot effectively:

- raise resources;
- defend itself;
- make urgent agreements;
- respond to crises;
- control the systems on which its population depends.

This sharpens the Villain's grievance:

> Port's problem is not simply that outsiders interfere too much.

It is that Port may lack the **capacity to act as an agent**.

# 13. Rawls — fairness under uncertainty

## Source-derived point

OpenStax summarizes Rawls's:

- society as cooperation for mutual advantage;
- original position;
- veil of ignorance;
- equal basic liberties;
- fair equality of opportunity;
- difference principle.

## 10Kings application

Use this as a **stress test** for the next settlement.

Would the rule still look fair if the person designing it did not know whether they would be:

- a powerful Council-linked lender;
- a seasonal laborer;
- a guild localist;
- a Port citizen;
- a foreign merchant;
- a remote community;
- someone whose testimony is usually ignored?

It is especially useful for evaluating whether the postwar order solves Port's grievance merely by shifting the burden elsewhere.
