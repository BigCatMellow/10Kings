# 2. Prisoner's dilemma — individually rational action can create collective failure

## Source-derived point

The book presents the prisoner's dilemma as a conflict between **individual rationality** and **group rationality**.

Each player has reason to protect themselves by defecting.

When both do so, both can end up worse off than if they had cooperated.

The book also expands the structure toward multi-party problems such as the tragedy of the commons.

## 10Kings application

This maps directly onto the current escalation model.

Examples:

- one polity stockpiles because another might stockpile;
- one merchant reserves transport because somebody else might;
- one harbor arms escorts because rival escorts are appearing;
- one government introduces strategic controls because waiting might leave it exposed.

No actor needs to want war.

The dangerous state can emerge from:

> **reasonable self-protection under mutual uncertainty.**

This gives philosophical support to the current rule that the war should be a **security dilemma**, not a continent collectively behaving irrationally.

# 3. Utilitarianism — aggregate benefit can erase other moral values

## Source-derived point

Kleinman's treatment presents utilitarianism as a consequentialist theory in which right action is evaluated through the welfare / happiness produced for those affected.

The text also presents a standard problem:

> maximizing aggregate welfare can conflict with values such as justice, truth, friendship, or the innocence of a particular person.

The important structural question is therefore not only:

> how much good resulted?

but also:

> what was done to particular people in producing it?

## 10Kings application

This is relevant to **both** the Council and the Villain.

### Council version

The Council can plausibly save more people overall while still:

- choosing which borrower fails;
- allowing one town to lose traffic;
- favoring one route over another;
- concentrating scarce material where it protects the wider system.

### Villain version

The Villain can plausibly believe:

- suffering now;
- war now;
- institutional rupture now;

will create a better political order for generations.

The philosophical problem survives even if his prediction is correct.

That is useful because the story does **not** need to prove his strategy ineffective in order to challenge his methods.

# 4. Trolley problem and double effect — causing harm is not identical to allowing foreseen harm

## Source-derived point

The trolley section distinguishes:

- consequentialist calculation;
- direct participation in harm;
- foreseen harmful consequences;
- intended harmful means.

The book introduces the **doctrine of double effect** as a way of asking whether a bad consequence may be foreseen without being the intended means of the action.

It also emphasizes that people often perceive a moral difference between:

- redirecting an existing danger;
- and directly using a person as the instrument by which others are saved.

## 10Kings application

This is particularly useful for tracking the Villain's moral progression.

His escalation should distinguish:

1. **observing** a real crisis;
2. **taking advantage** of a consequence;
3. **withholding help** while knowing harm will increase;
4. **redirecting** an existing danger;
5. **deliberately creating** a victim because the victim is useful to the plan.

Those are not merely louder versions of one action.

They are different moral categories.

This supports the existing opening boundary in which he has begun tolerating preventable harm but has not yet made indiscriminate mass harm routine.

# 5. Hobbes — coordination, enforcement, fear, and the knowledge of consequences

## Source-derived point

Kleinman's Hobbes section presents society as an artificial structure created to restrain conflict among self-interested people.

Particularly relevant ideas include:

- social order as a response to insecurity;
- the social contract rather than divine right as a basis for political authority;
- the importance of consequences when judging action;
- the belief that uncoordinated self-interest can make judgment unreliable;
- the need for a structure capable of enforcing common rules.

## 10Kings application

This sharpens the best defense of the **Council**:

> coordination is not decorative; interdependent systems can fail when nobody can credibly bind anyone else.

It also sharpens the first **Convergence**.

The Convergence was not idealistic world government.

It was a negotiated answer to:

- unsafe passage;
- repeated conflict;
- unreliable agreements;
- piracy;
- resource disputes;
- the growing cost of mistrust.

Hobbes is therefore useful for asking:

> What minimum authority is actually required for cooperation to become rational?

The story then asks the next question:

> If that authority is required, who is entitled to possess it?

That second question is where Hobbes alone is insufficient.

# 6. Marx — institutions are grounded in material relationships

## Source-derived point

Kleinman's Marx section emphasizes:

- historical materialism;
- modes and relations of production;
- labor and ownership;
- alienation;
- the way economic organization shapes social relationships.

The key research value here is not importing Marx's entire political program.

It is the analytical move:

> **look at how society is materially organized, not only at what people say it believes.**

## 10Kings application

This is highly compatible with the current worldbuilding method.

Port may be called neutral while its actual life is determined by:

- who owns warehouses;
- who controls credit;
- who can finance voyages;
- who employs dock labor;
- who can reserve scarce transport;
- where profits leave;
- which guild credentials are recognized.

Likewise a Stone Hills dispute is not only about ideology.

It also concerns:

- who owns production;
- who gets priority orders;
- who bears safety risk;
- who can survive a work stoppage;
- who controls access to strategic material.

Use Marx here as a **material diagnostic**, not as a template that forces the setting into one predicted historical outcome.

# 7. Philosophy of language — political conflict can be conflict over definitions

## Source-derived point

The surveyed philosophy-of-language material includes Locke's concern that communication fails when speakers:

- use terms without clear ideas;
- assume different meanings;
- use terms inconsistently.

It also introduces later concern with how expressions refer and how the mode of presentation affects meaning.

## 10Kings application

The setting already contains politically loaded terms whose practical meanings can diverge:

- **neutrality**;
- **piracy**;
- **security**;
- **emergency**;
- **public good**;
- **guild recognition**;
- **strategic material**;
- **customary right**.

A strong political dispute may therefore involve people agreeing on the word while disagreeing on the rule the word actually carries.

This is especially useful for:

- copied contracts;
- multilingual agreements;
- treaty interpretation;
- competing public descriptions of the same armed incident.

# 8. Best use of this source

Use Kleinman when we need:

- a quick concept reminder;
- a simple thought experiment;
- a first-pass connection between story mechanics and philosophy.

Do **not** treat this book as the final scholarly authority on:

- Hobbes;
- Marx;
- utilitarianism;
- political legitimacy.

For those, route first to:

- [Nigel Warburton — *Philosophy: The Classics*](Nigel-Warburton-Philosophy-The-Classics.md);
- [OpenStax — *Introduction to Philosophy*](OpenStax-Introduction-to-Philosophy.md).
