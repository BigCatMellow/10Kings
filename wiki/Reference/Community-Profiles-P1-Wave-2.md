# 1. SH-3 — small specialized craft town

## Formation

The town likely became important because several related skills accumulated in one place over generations.

The strongest sequence:

~~~text
useful local craft / resource base
→ several specialist households
→ repeat merchant demand
→ apprenticeship concentration
→ quality reputation
→ guild / inspection / supplier institutions
→ town becomes difficult to replace even after some raw materials arrive from elsewhere
~~~

The town's advantage is therefore not merely:

> local material.

It is:

> **the local production ecosystem.**

## Livelihood

Possible roles include:

- raw / semi-finished material preparation;
- specialist shaping;
- fitting / assembly;
- heat treatment or other finishing where technically relevant;
- polishing / sharpening / surface finishing;
- inspection;
- repair;
- toolmaking;
- merchants;
- material suppliers.

Not every resident is a craft worker.

The town also needs:

- food sellers;
- transport;
- housing;
- religious / civic services;
- laborers.

## Household pattern

A workshop household may contain:

- master / senior craft worker;
- partner handling accounts / sales / unrelated work;
- apprentice;
- younger family workers;
- older specialist whose physical output has declined but whose judgment remains valued.

Other workshops may be non-family enterprises.

Do not make hereditary succession mandatory.

## Institutions

Likely:

- guild branch or multiple related guilds;
- inspection / quality marks;
- supplier relationships;
- apprenticeship networks;
- merchant brokers;
- mutual aid / burial / injury systems.

The town's institutions care about **reputation outside the town** because that reputation supports demand.

## Internal divisions

- old workshop families vs newer masters;
- guild-recognized vs independent producers;
- high-prestige finishing trades vs lower-status preparatory work;
- merchant / exporter vs craft worker;
- traditional product vs new demand;
- people who want stronger Port-centered guild standardization vs people who fear losing local control.

## Built environment

Strong expected pattern:

- workshops embedded in streets / courtyards;
- specialized ventilation / fire separation where needed;
- storage for materials;
- small merchant / inspection offices;
- apprentice lodging;
- shared service yards.

Architecture should follow the actual craft, not a generic "artisan aesthetic."

## Food / ordinary life

Food follows Stone Hills town logic:

- grain;
- legumes;
- dairy;
- portable work meals;
- market food.

A prosperous master may eat very differently from:

- apprentice;
- day laborer;
- subcontractor.

## Cultural identity

Outsiders know one famous product category.

Locals recognize:

- workshop;
- process;
- maker;
- generation;
- quality tier;
- rival technique.

That local specificity is one of the town's strongest cultural traits.

## Cultural transmission

The town exports:

- objects;
- apprentices;
- quality language;
- tool / process vocabulary;
- styles later copied elsewhere.

A copied object can spread without copying the whole culture.

## Myth / folklore

No new major creature family is required.

The strongest folklore may attach to:

- impossible-quality old work;
- lost master technique;
- famous failed inspection;
- tool inherited through several workshops;
- old fire / rebuilding.

If Knocking Below appears, it should do so only where mine links justify it.

## Current-event vulnerability

Guild centralization / strategic demand can:

- increase orders;
- improve reciprocal credentials;
- pull apprentices away;
- favor large workshops;
- break local subcontract chains.

The town can profit while also becoming structurally more fragile.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

# 2. LR-2 — estate / tenant district

## Formation

This district developed where one or several large estates accumulated:

- land;
- storage;
- roads;
- credit;
- tenancy rights.

The district remains inside LR-A sovereignty.

The estate is not a state.

But for many households, estate institutions structure daily life more immediately than the distant central government.

## Livelihood

Households may work through different arrangements:

- fixed rent;
- crop share;
- wage labor;
- mixed tenancy + seasonal estate work;
- livestock arrangement;
- seed / tool advance.

Do not make one tenancy contract region-wide.

## Household pattern

The key derived rule:

> **farm size / contract can influence household composition.**

A household may remain multi-generational because:

- labor is useful;
- rent is tied to one holding;
- splitting the household threatens viability.

Another household may fragment because:

- debt;
- eviction;
- lease loss;
- insufficient land.

Do not assign patriarchal / patrilocal household rules without independent 10Kings support.

## Estate institutions

Possible:

- estate office / agent;
- rent / share records;
- storage;
- seed / equipment lending;
- private road;
- mill;
- work gang administration.

Some services can be genuinely useful.

That makes dependency more politically interesting.

## Internal divisions

- secure tenant vs precarious tenant;
- tenant vs landless wage worker;
- estate employee vs ordinary renter;
- large tenant vs smallholder;
- estate agent vs absentee / distant owner;
- households favoring purchase / reform vs households fearing debt from ownership.

## Credit

Especially important.

An estate may provide:

- grain advance;
- seed;
- equipment;
- delayed rent.

That can save a household one year while deepening dependency the next.

## Food / ordinary life

Even in agricultural abundance:

- best produce may be sold / owed;
- household may eat cheaper staples;
- debt may rise while granaries remain full.

This fits the current Low Rivers abundance crisis extremely well.

## Architecture

Likely district contrast:

- estate center / records / storage;
- tenant farmsteads;
- labor cottages / rented rooms;
- roads oriented toward estate storage / mill.

No "Big House" aesthetic is required.

## Historical memory

This community may preserve stories about:

- changed rent measure;
- generous / cruel agent;
- household removed from one farm;
- boundary dispute;
- old debt forgiven or exploited.

This is stronger than forcing a creature.

## Cultural transmission

Tenant workers moving to:

- river markets;
- harvest work;
- Port;
- Stone Hills

carry:

- food;
- rural songs;
- grievances;
- contract vocabulary.

## Current-event vulnerability

The abundance crisis can trigger:

- falling prices;
- rent stress;
- debt accumulation;
- estate land consolidation;
- forced sale of livestock;
- tenant displacement.

A policy intended to stabilize food supply can therefore accelerate land concentration.

## P1 result

**STRONG PASS.**

# 3. OC-3 — coastal merchant city

## Formation

OC-3 developed around a maritime trade position rather than shared dependence on the OC-U / OC-D water system.

Its long history of trade has produced:

- merchant houses;
- harbor labor;
- imported goods;
- diasporic families;
- religious institutions;
- local civic forms.

The key rule:

> **outside influence has been metabolized into local culture over generations.**

## Livelihood

Major sectors may include:

- shipping;
- merchant brokerage;
- warehousing;
- finance / credit;
- ship repair;
- food / provisioning;
- crafts;
- coastal fisheries;
- clerical / contract work.

## Household pattern

A merchant household can include:

- locally born family;
- relative / factor from another city;
- apprentice clerk;
- servant / employee;
- visiting rural kin.

A harbor labor household looks completely different.

No one "cosmopolitan household" model.

## Institutions

Possible:

- civic harbor authority;
- merchant associations;
- contract / arbitration courts;
- religious charities;
- neighborhood associations;
- ship / pilot institutions.

Diaspora-origin institutions may persist even when their members are fully local citizens.

## Internal divisions

- old civic families vs recently risen merchant houses;
- harbor labor vs merchant elite;
- maritime interests vs hinterland interests;
- local traders vs foreign / outside factors;
- faction favoring OC-U / OC-D cooperation vs faction fearing a bloc.

## Built environment

Should reflect layers of:

- seafront trade;
- shop / residence;
- warehouses;
- religious buildings;
- reused older structures;
- dense neighborhoods.

Do not design it as "Port but warmer."

It is a sovereign city-state with its own political history.

## Food / ordinary life

Likely unusually broad access to:

- coastal fish;
- Old Cities agricultural goods;
- imported oils / spices / preserved foods.

But class still controls actual diet.

Long-settled imported foods can become entirely local.

## Language / culture

Merchant / clerk culture likely supports:

- multilingual practice;
- contract vocabulary;
- loanwords.

Neighborhood speech can remain distinct from elite commercial speech.

## Mythic tradition

**Charter Beast** shifts here toward:

> remembers the agreement.

Possible uses:

- merchant seals;
- guarantee houses;
- arbitration imagery.

The **Wind Guardian** can also have a coastal storm / threshold interpretation.

These should remain local variants, not city-wide doctrine.

## Cultural transmission

OC-3 exports:

- commercial language;
- styles;
- food;
- religious / charitable models;
- merchant families;
- legal practices.

It may also import Port fashions and then claim they were improved locally.

## Current-event vulnerability

If OC-U and OC-D cooperate more strongly around water / defense:

- OC-3 can rationally fear exclusion or political consolidation;
- merchants may simultaneously profit from stability.

That gives the city a divided response rather than simple jealousy.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

# 4. HR-1 — central switching / arbitration city

## Formation

HR-C became important because multiple routes repeatedly require people to:

- stop;
- change animals / carriers;
- store goods;
- translate;
- arbitrate;
- re-contract.

Its political importance grew from **interoperability**, not conquest.

## Livelihood

Major urban occupations can include:

- warehouse / yard workers;
- animal sellers / keepers;
- carrier brokers;
- interpreters;
- clerks;
- arbiters;
- merchants;
- inn / lodging operators;
- repair workers;
- food vendors;
- religious / charitable hospitality.

## Neighborhood structure

Strong candidate for occupational / migration layering:

- caravan-yard district;
- animal market;
- clerk / arbitration streets;
- older local residential neighborhoods;
- merchant / diaspora communities;
- craft / repair districts.

Do not turn these into rigid ethnic quarters by default.

## Institutions

HR-C should contain many narrow but trusted institutions:

- weighing / measurement;
- contract witnesses;
- arbitration;
- interpreter certification;
- warehouse records;
- animal health / sale practices;
- lodging networks.

The city becomes useful because outsiders can **plug into systems they trust enough**.

## Household pattern

A household may mix:

- permanent resident;
- traveler boarding;
- apprentice;
- merchant relative;
- interpreter work;
- local craft.

Like Port, but with more route / animal logic and less maritime / neutrality logic.

## Internal divisions

- local merchants vs long-distance houses;
- arbiters defending neutrality vs patrons pressuring them;
- animal / carrier interests vs road authorities;
- residents resenting traveler prices / crowding;
- multilingual elite brokers vs local workers excluded from high-value contract roles.

## Food / leisure

Existing High Roads caravan-yard distinction remains useful:

- local household food;
- traveler food;
- wealthy merchant food.

The city also becomes a major diffusion point for:

- games;
- songs;
- stories;
- slang.

## Mythic tradition

**Night Road** can be particularly strong here.

But HR-C residents may treat it as:

- traveler nonsense;
- old caravan history;
- city entertainment.

The **Weather Animal** is more likely to be recorded / classified here than believed uniformly.

## Cultural transmission

HR-C is one of the world's strongest cultural switching points because:

- carriers;
- pilgrims;
- merchants;
- herders;
- officials

all pass through.

But different carriers move different things.

## Current-event vulnerability

Route attacks / closures create:

- storage backlogs;
- animal demand;
- price spikes;
- arbitration overload;
- rumor.

A false route report can materially change the city before violence reaches it.

## P1 result

**STRONG PASS.**

# 5. HR-3 — northern high-route settlement

## Formation

This is a permanent high-route settlement inside / near HR-N whose life combines:

- highland cultivation;
- animal economy;
- route traffic;
- seasonal movement;
- winter storage.

The core corrective:

> **mobility does not make the settlement temporary.**

## Livelihood

Households may combine:

- hardy crops;
- terrace / slope cultivation where terrain supports;
- sheep / goat-like stock;
- larger pack animals;
- guiding;
- lodging;
- repair;
- seasonal market work.

Different households move different amounts.

## Household pattern

Possible household:

- some members remain year-round;
- herding party moves seasonally;
- older relatives manage stored goods / children / household base;
- one member works periodically at roadhead or market.

This overlaps M-2 but is not identical to it.

## Institutions

Likely:

- local water / spring management;
- pasture access;
- storage;
- route maintenance;
- traveler lodging;
- winter emergency response.

HR-N polity authority overlaps these systems.

## Internal divisions

- route-service households vs pastoral households;
- year-round residents vs highly mobile branches;
- merchants wanting expanded road capacity vs people protecting pasture / water;
- young people seeking roadhead work vs households needing seasonal labor.

## Built environment

Strong requirements:

- storage;
- animal shelter;
- weather protection;
- compact / defensible siting where terrain supports;
- water capture;
- repair space.

Architecture must be derived from actual slope / material later.

## Food / ordinary life

Likely:

- hardy grain;
- dairy;
- legumes;
- preserved meat;
- roots;
- imported market goods.

Winter isolation makes storage socially important.

## Youth / education

This community is an especially strong test for:

- fixed schooling;
- route knowledge;
- seasonal absence.

Some young people can gain literacy at the base while still participating in herd movement.

## Mythic tradition

**Weather Animal** can be a strong resident tradition.

Resident version may differ from mobile pastoral version:

- resident: appearance tied to elevation / pass conditions;
- pastoral: appearance tied to when to move animals.

The same sighting can mean different actions.

## Cultural transmission

HR-3 sits between:

- NC-R / North Coast route;
- HR-C;
- pastoral networks.

It can transmit:

- weather knowledge;
- food;
- route stories;
- speech forms.

## Current-event vulnerability

North Shoulder pressure affects:

- lodging;
- storage;
- pasture;
- animal prices;
- security.

A convoy / closure policy designed elsewhere becomes household economics here.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

# 6. Cross-profile findings

## Craft town ≠ industrial district

SH-3:
- interdependent specialist workshops;
- reputation / quality cluster.

SH-1:
- large extraction / labor / civic system.

Both are Stone Hills, but their social structures differ.

## Estate district ≠ cooperative village

LR-2:
- estate / tenant dependency;
- contract can shape household.

LR-1:
- user institution built from shared water dependence.

Both can exist in the same Low Rivers polity.

## Cosmopolitanism has causes

OC-3:
- sovereign maritime merchant city.

HR-C:
- overland switching / arbitration city.

Port:
- neutral multimodal coordination hub.

Do not flatten them into one "diverse city" template.

## Settled / mobile remains a false binary

HR-3 + M-2 demonstrate:

- permanent village;
- partial household movement;
- seasonal pasture;
- route service

as one coherent social landscape.
