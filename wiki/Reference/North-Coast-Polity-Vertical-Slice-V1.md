# 1. Starting constraint

North Coast politics already requires:

- strong local harbors;
- island autonomy;
- clan / kin obligations;
- fishery rights crossing territorial lines;
- mission-specific convoy / piracy coordination;
- larger authorities capable of diplomacy and war.

A simple centralized coastal kingdom would flatten too much.

A loose collection of clans would not provide enough stable external authority.

The strongest V1 solution is:

> **NC-M as a maritime compact polity: a territorial mainland state whose central authority accumulated from recurring defense, convoy, harbor, and diplomatic coordination among older coastal jurisdictions.**

Its unity is real.

Its internal components remain politically visible.

---

# 2. Historical formation model

## SUPPORTED

Pre-Convergence North Coast contained:

- coastal clans;
- harbor confederacies;
- island rulers;
- fishing-right alliances;
- raiding fleets.

Maritime rights often crossed land control.

Modern politics still require larger-scale:

- diplomacy;
- anti-piracy action;
- convoy policy;
- strategic harbor investment.

## PROVISIONAL V1 sequence

~~~
independent coastal / inland authorities
→ repeated raiding, fishery disputes, storms, and convoy needs
→ temporary defense / rescue / access coalitions recur
→ some mainland harbors and territorial rulers coordinate more consistently
→ Convergence rewards authorities capable of binding larger areas to maritime obligations
→ recurring coalition offices / contributions become durable
→ one mainland-centered political compact develops territorial continuity
→ NC-M emerges as a real polity while older harbor / clan rights remain embedded inside it
~~~

This makes NC-M neither an ancient ethnic monarchy nor a temporary league.

It is a state built from institutions that originally solved shared maritime problems.

---

# 3. Territorial core

NC-M needs enough continuous mainland territory to support:

- taxation;
- inland farms / valleys;
- roads;
- military recruitment;
- major harbors;
- NC-R;
- external diplomacy.

Its central authority should be strongest in:

- connected southern / central mainland districts;
- major roads;
- strategic harbors;
- larger towns.

It may be weaker in:

- remote coasts;
- clan-dominated districts;
- small islands;
- seasonal maritime-use zones.

That unevenness is expected.

---

# 4. Central authority

NC-M needs one recognized external political authority capable of:

- diplomacy;
- war;
- Convergence representation;
- major taxation;
- strategic harbor investment;
- anti-piracy mobilization;
- convoy policy;
- serious cross-district law.

The exact office remains open.

V1 does **not** require that this authority directly administer:

- every harbor;
- every fishery;
- every clan;
- every vessel.

The central state is strongest when coordination has to extend beyond one locality.

---

# 5. Why the center exists

The center is legitimate because local autonomy alone cannot solve:

- coast-wide piracy;
- war;
- foreign diplomacy;
- major road investment;
- protection of imported food routes;
- disputes among powerful harbors;
- Convergence obligations.

Its political bargain is:

> local jurisdictions surrender some freedom of external action in exchange for larger-scale security, recognition, and bargaining power.

That bargain can be contested without making the polity fictional.

---

# 6. Internal compact jurisdictions

NC-M should contain several kinds of historically persistent jurisdiction.

## Harbor jurisdictions

Can possess real authority over:

- docks;
- pilotage;
- markets;
- local fees;
- repair;
- fire;
- quarantine;
- harbor watch.

## Clan / kin jurisdictions

Can retain recognized roles in:

- compensation;
- inheritance;
- rescue obligations;
- local leadership;
- vessel / labor networks.

Exact scope varies.

## Inland districts

May be governed through:

- local rulers;
- councils;
- estates;
- appointed officials;
- mixed systems.

## Roadhead jurisdiction

NC-R has strong local commercial / route authority while remaining inside NC-M.

This produces a state whose internal political map is institutionally varied rather than one uniform set of provinces.

---

# 7. Central revenue

NC-M needs revenue that does not depend on one fishery.

V1 supports a mixed base:

## Land / household obligations

Inland and coastal settlements contribute ordinary tax / levy.

## Harbor / customs revenue

Large ports can provide:

- customs;
- market fees;
- docking shares;
- negotiated contributions.

## Maritime levy

Convoy / anti-piracy needs can justify specific maritime contributions.

These may be assessed through:

- ship ownership;
- cargo;
- harbor traffic;
- local quotas.

## Extraordinary emergency levy

War, famine, or fleet loss can require additional contributions.

### Political consequence

A special maritime levy can become constitutionally sensitive because people remember whether it was created for a temporary emergency.

Writing the Sequel should preserve that history.

---

# 8. Maritime centralization limit

The center may declare policy.

It cannot physically implement maritime policy without:

- harbors;
- shipowners;
- captains;
- sailors;
- pilots;
- local repair capacity;
- credit.

Therefore:

> **NC-M central power is strongest when it can bind existing maritime institutions into common action, not when it tries to replace them.**

That is the defining difference from LR-A.

---

# 9. Convoy authority

V1 tests a two-layer structure.

## Political authorization

NC-M can:

- declare convoy policy;
- negotiate with Port;
- levy or fund defense;
- appoint / recognize overall command;
- define strategic objectives.

## Operational command

Actual convoy operations require:

- participating captains;
- harbor schedules;
- pilots;
- signals;
- repair;
- escort ships.

Operational authority can be temporary and mission-specific.

### Design rule

Convoy command does not automatically become a permanent navy ministry.

Repeated crises may nevertheless create increasingly permanent staff, records, and ships.

That is a Writing-the-Sequel pressure, not yet canon.

---

# 10. Standing naval capacity

NC-M probably requires **some** permanent maritime coercive capacity.

Otherwise central anti-piracy power is too weak.

V1 supports:

- a small standing core of state-controlled or continuously retained vessels;
- harbor patrol forces;
- temporary requisition / contracted vessels during major crises.

This does not establish a coast-wide standing navy large enough to dominate all private shipping.

### Why this balance works

Permanent core:

- protects state credibility;
- trains command;
- carries messages;
- responds rapidly.

Temporary expansion:

- preserves dependence on local maritime society;
- makes prolonged conflict expensive.

---

# 11. NC-I — island political actor

NC-I should remain more than a municipality.

It needs enough separate authority over:

- local taxation / fees;
- local law;
- harbor use;
- fisheries;
- rescue;
- local defense;
- political representation.

The strongest V1 model is:

> **NC-I as a self-governing associated island polity whose external obligations to NC-M are substantial but historically negotiated.**

This deliberately stops short of deciding whether lawyers call it:

- member;
- tributary;
- confederate;
- protectorate;
- dependency;
- allied polity.

The lived relationship matters more than the label at this stage.

---

# 12. NC-I bargain with NC-M

V1 tests a historical compact containing some combination of:

## NC-M obligations

- external defense;
- diplomatic representation in some matters;
- mainland market access;
- emergency food / shipping support;
- recognition of island rights.

## NC-I obligations

- ships / sailors or money for common defense;
- compliance with some convoy rules;
- recognition of common external agreements;
- contribution during major emergencies.

## NC-I retained powers

- local law;
- local taxation / fees;
- local officials;
- fishery administration where not cross-border;
- internal property / family matters;
- local harbor governance.

This is enough to make association real without absorption.

---

# 13. Constitutional ambiguity is useful

NC-M and NC-I should not necessarily describe their relationship the same way.

Mainland interpretation may emphasize:

- common defense;
- shared polity;
- inherited obligations.

Island interpretation may emphasize:

- compact;
- reciprocal rights;
- consent;
- retained self-government.

Both can cite real history.

That disagreement is stronger than simply deciding one side is legally correct now.

---

# 14. Fishery jurisdiction

Fishery rights are the strongest check on territorial simplification.

A bank may involve:

- NC-M crews;
- NC-I crews;
- outside North Coast communities;
- old seasonal access.

Therefore NC-M state law alone should not settle every fishery.

V1 supports:

- customary access;
- specific agreements;
- joint / reciprocal forums;
- emergency conservation rules.

Central government can participate without owning the sea.

---

# 15. Harbor autonomy

Major harbors inside NC-M need enough authority to be political actors.

They can control routine:

- berthing;
- market;
- local watch;
- pilotage;
- repair;
- local quarantine.

NC-M can claim stronger authority where harbor decisions affect:

- war;
- foreign access;
- national customs;
- Convergence obligations.

### Stress point

A harbor may argue:

> this is local port regulation.

The center may answer:

> this changes foreign policy.

That jurisdictional boundary should generate recurring cases.

---

# 16. Clan authority

Clan / kin institutions remain real but should not be made universal or ethnically total.

They can matter through:

- vessel finance;
- rescue;
- inheritance;
- compensation;
- marriage;
- political patronage;
- labor recruitment;
- local reputation.

A person can belong to a clan network and still be:

- town citizen;
- guild member;
- state subject;
- merchant;
- migrant.

### Central limit

NC-M should intervene when clan action becomes:

- private war;
- piracy;
- refusal of serious criminal law;
- obstruction of state diplomacy.

Exact boundary remains contested.

---

# 17. Succession / central-office legitimacy

The legacy reconciliation preserves:

> lineage plus political confirmation

as one possible mechanism.

For NC-M, however, the strongest fit is slightly different from SH-A.

V1 tests:

> **a narrow pool of legitimate high-level candidates plus confirmation by major territorial / harbor constituencies.**

This could be:

- dynastic line with harbor / clan confirmation;
- election among leading houses;
- another mixed system.

### Why this fits

NC-M grew from coalition.

A central ruler whose legitimacy ignores the major participating jurisdictions would contradict that history.

### Difference from SH-A

SH-A confirmation mainly protects inherited jurisdictions inside a composite territorial state.

NC-M confirmation would more directly express the polity's origin in **collective maritime coordination**.

Exact institution remains open.

---

# 18. NC-R inside NC-M

NC-R should have:

- local market / warehouse authority;
- route-status institutions;
- animal / transfer economy;
- local policing.

NC-M needs control over:

- military passage;
- foreign treaty obligations;
- major customs / strategic road policy.

### Political consequence

NC-R can become an internal lobby for:

- good relations with HR-N;
- predictable cross-border movement;
- avoiding maritime overdependence.

During piracy crises, NC-R gains political importance.

---

# 19. Food dependence

NC-M imports substantial grain and other goods.

That creates a structural constraint.

A central government considering:

- blockade;
- war;
- convoy escalation;
- Port confrontation

must consider food access.

Island communities may be even more exposed.

### Political consequence

Maritime security policy is never only about pride or naval strategy.

It directly affects:

- bread prices;
- winter stores;
- migration;
- debt.

---

# 20. Relationship with Port

NC-M needs reliable access to Port but cannot be allowed to monopolize it.

V1 gives NC-M legitimate interests in:

- shipping protection;
- merchant access;
- convoy coordination;
- treatment of its ships / citizens.

It does **not** give NC-M:

- unilateral right to station large forces in Port;
- right to govern Port;
- exclusive harbor privileges.

This creates permanent tension.

### If the legacy Port Seizure is later promoted

NC-M's constitutional / political predecessors become strong candidates for involvement.

But this slice does not require that history.

# 21. Internal constituencies

These are not necessarily formal parties.

## Central coordination interests

Want:

- reliable revenue;
- common defense;
- diplomatic consistency;
- strategic harbors.

## Major harbors

Want:

- local control;
- trade;
- favorable fees;
- state investment.

## Shipowners / merchants

Want:

- security;
- low disruption;
- contracts;
- credit.

## Captains / sailors

Care about:

- risk;
- pay;
- rescue;
- operational autonomy.

## Clan / kin networks

Want:

- recognition;
- local standing;
- retained obligations / rights.

## Island interests

Want:

- autonomy;
- reliable mainland support;
- fishery rights;
- resistance to one-sided levies.

## Inland communities

May resent a state that appears to spend disproportionate revenue on maritime interests.

This last constituency is important.

NC-M is not a state composed only of sailors.

# 22. Test 1 — piracy surge

Raids rise sharply.

NC-M can:

- coordinate intelligence;
- fund patrol;
- negotiate convoy rules;
- press harbors for contributions.

Harbors / shipowners can resist:

- cost;
- command;
- schedule.

NC-I may accept common defense while disputing levy formula.

**STRONG PASS.**

The compact structure turns piracy into politics rather than a simple royal order.

---

# 23. Test 2 — shifting fish stocks

Fish move toward grounds heavily used by NC-I.

NC-M mainland crews seek expanded access.

NC-I cites historical rights.

Central officials cannot simply declare mainland equality without destabilizing the compact.

Fishery-specific forums / agreements become necessary.

**STRONG PASS.**

Territorial statehood does not erase sea-use jurisdiction.

---

# 24. Test 3 — harbor refuses convoy restriction

A major NC-M harbor wants ships to sail independently.

The center requires convoy because of piracy.

Harbor merchants argue delay is economically worse than risk.

### Escalation

local regulation  
→ state strategic claim  
→ legal / political dispute  
→ negotiated exemption or enforcement

**PASS.**

This proves harbor autonomy has substance without making the harbor sovereign.

---

# 25. Test 4 — NC-I refuses maritime levy

NC-M imposes a levy to expand escorts.

NC-I argues:

- its existing ship contribution already satisfies the compact;
- the new levy changes the historic bargain.

The issue becomes constitutional.

Possible evidence:

- old contribution records;
- compact language;
- prior emergency precedents.

**STRONG PASS.**

Writing the Sequel turns an old emergency obligation into a modern constitutional dispute.

---

# 26. Test 5 — island supply failure

Storms interrupt NC-I food imports.

NC-I expects NC-M assistance.

Mainland officials argue conditions make relief dangerous / expensive.

### Test

If NC-M has no obligation, the association feels shallow.

If NC-I can demand unlimited aid regardless of cost, the arrangement becomes one-sided.

V1 supports a real but bounded duty of assistance.

**PASS.**

---

# 27. Test 6 — piracy accusation against a clan-linked vessel

Evidence suggests a vessel tied to a powerful clan aided raiders.

Clan leaders argue:

- crew acted independently;
- evidence is false;
- local compensation is enough.

NC-M claims serious security jurisdiction.

Harbor authorities hold records.

### Result

A state investigation can occur without treating clan affiliation as guilt.

**PASS.**

This gives the current accusation storyline a legal path.

---

# 28. Test 7 — Port convoy negotiation

Port merchants want coordinated escort but fear NC-M using convoy presence to gain military leverage.

NC-M needs:

- Port access;
- wider coalition acceptance;
- credible reassurance.

NC-I may have its own shipping interests.

### Result

NC-M can speak externally, but cannot claim to represent every North Coast maritime actor in every commercial detail.

**STRONG PASS.**

This fits Port neutrality.

---

# 29. Test 8 — succession dispute

A central leader dies.

Candidate A has strong lineage / mainland support.

Candidate B has stronger harbor / maritime backing.

NC-I insists the confirmation procedure protects its historic voice.

### Result

The succession process tests the compact itself.

A candidate can be legally plausible yet politically unconfirmable.

**PASS.**

This gives the state a constitutional structure distinct from both SH-A and LR-A.

# 30. Comparison with SH-A and LR-A

## SH-A

**Composite territorial polity**

Central problem:

> incorporating older concentrated civic / productive jurisdictions into one stronger state.

## LR-A

**Basin-centered administrative territorial polity**

Central problem:

> coordinating dispersed agrarian / water systems without replacing local competence.

## NC-M

**Maritime compact polity**

Central problem:

> creating durable external / strategic coordination while harbor, clan, island, vessel, and seasonal-use jurisdictions remain politically meaningful.

### Result

All three states can have:

- rulers;
- taxes;
- armies;
- diplomacy;

without being the same constitutional system.

That is the desired outcome.

# 31. Strong V1 polity conclusion

NC-M works best as:

> **a mainland-centered maritime compact polity whose central government grew from recurring defense, convoy, harbor, and diplomatic coordination among older coastal jurisdictions.**

Its center is strongest in:

- diplomacy;
- war;
- strategic maritime policy;
- major taxation;
- serious cross-district law;
- Convergence representation.

Its authority remains constrained by:

- harbor jurisdictions;
- clan / kin institutions;
- shipowners / captains;
- fishery agreements;
- local economic dependence;
- associated island rights.

NC-I works best as:

> **a self-governing associated island polity whose defense, diplomatic, and maritime obligations to NC-M are real but historically negotiated rather than evidence of simple absorption.**

# 33. Promotion gate

Alternative models:

## A. Centralized naval monarchy

Fails because it erases too much harbor / clan / island authority.

## B. Loose maritime confederation

Fails because NC-M needs stronger territorial, fiscal, legal, and diplomatic continuity than a purely temporary league provides.

## C. Clan federation

Fails because towns, harbors, inland districts, merchants, and state institutions cannot be reduced to clan government.

## D. Maritime compact polity

Fits:

- territorial continuity;
- strong central external power;
- harbor autonomy;
- clan obligations;
- island association;
- convoy politics;
- fishery jurisdiction;
- Port relations.

**V1 winner: maritime compact polity as the working NC-M design model.**

This remains provisional and unnamed.
