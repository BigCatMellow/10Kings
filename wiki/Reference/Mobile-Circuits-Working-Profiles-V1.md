# Mobile Circuits — Working Profiles V1

## Status

**Provisional worldbuilding — not final canon.**

This page instantiates the first three mobile systems required by:

- [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](../Culture/Nomads.md);
- [Cultural Analogue Coverage Assessment — V1](Cultural-Analogue-Coverage-Assessment-V1.md);
- [Mobile Peoples Comparative Research — V1](Mobile-Peoples-Comparative-Research-V1.md).

It deliberately avoids final:

- people names;
- languages;
- exact route maps;
- legal charters;
- ethnic boundaries.

The goal is to prove that three **different reasons for mobility** can produce three different mobile cultures.

---

# 1. Shared rule

These are not three "nomad tribes."

They are three mobile social formations whose members may differ by:

- family;
- language;
- faith;
- polity of citizenship;
- wealth;
- route;
- degree of mobility.

Some members may settle.

Some settled people may join.

Some households may move only part of the year.

---

# 2. Circuit A — portable repair / service network

## Working concept

A loose but durable network of families / households who travel recurring routes providing specialized work that many settlements need **periodically**, but not enough to support a permanent specialist everywhere.

The network is the strongest home for the old **Appalachian-influenced mobile concept**, but mobility itself comes from actual itinerant-service research rather than from treating Appalachia as nomadic.

## Comparative mechanisms

From Appalachian research:

- family oral history;
- ballads / stories;
- work memory;
- mountain / route storytelling;
- locally transformed inherited tales.

From Irish Traveller tinsmithing:

- compact portable tools;
- repair as mobile livelihood;
- recurring customer circuits;
- fairs;
- information exchange through repeated visits.

From Central European itinerant tinkers:

- multilingual skill;
- long-distance occupational migration;
- socially useful work coexisting with low status / suspicion.

From itinerant craft traditions:

- travel can also function as professional learning.

## Core economic reason to move

A small settlement may need:

- metal repair;
- household-container repair;
- harness / tack repair;
- small tool repair;
- wagon / cart fittings;
- basket / leather / wood repair;

but not enough of one service to employ a permanent specialist.

Different households specialize.

Do **not** make every family able to repair everything.

### Example household specializations

- light metal / vessel repair;
- harness / leather;
- wheel / wagon fittings;
- baskets / containers;
- clocks / small mechanisms where technologically appropriate;
- sharpening / edge maintenance;
- instrument repair.

A route becomes economically viable because one traveling network can serve many settlements.

---

# 3. Circuit A — mobility pattern

## Working rhythm

Not one universal circuit.

Common pattern:

~~~text
fixed / semi-fixed cold-season or family base
→ spring service route
→ summer fairs / agricultural / transport work
→ autumn repair circuit
→ return to base / kin concentration
~~~

Three Moons can disrupt the normal cycle because:

- Port demand spikes;
- fairs / trade concentrate;
- guild contacts gather.

Some families may detour to Port once every seven years.

## Anchors

Possible anchors include:

- one home settlement;
- kin households;
- annual fairs;
- mining towns;
- roadheads;
- river markets;
- Port;
- long-term customers.

This network should know settlements **relationally**:

> who lets us camp  
> who owes us  
> whose daughter married into which household  
> which inn stores our tools  
> which village wants us before harvest

rather than only by map.

---

# 4. Circuit A — household and social structure

## Working default

The network is **not one centralized polity**.

Authority is mostly:

- household;
- extended kin;
- route reputation;
- respected older workers;
- temporary coordination at large gatherings.

Some lineages may be closely related.

Others join through:

- marriage;
- apprenticeship;
- partnership.

## Internal class / status differences

Important:

- households with established customers / stopping rights;
- skilled specialists with strong reputations;
- poorer households doing labor / peddling around the craft economy;
- partly settled branches;
- young workers attempting independent routes.

This avoids treating them as socially flat.

---

# 5. Circuit A — relationship with guilds

This is likely one of their defining political tensions.

## Possible arrangements

Some local guilds:

- recognize their credentials;
- license temporary work;
- subcontract them.

Others:

- accuse them of avoiding dues;
- restrict market work;
- treat them as unregulated competition.

Port / guild centralization may therefore matter deeply.

A new reciprocal credential system could benefit established mobile specialists while also:

- making informal practitioners easier to exclude.

That gives the Villain's guild reforms another ordinary consequence.

---

# 6. Circuit A — culture / memory

## Oral history

Songs / stories can preserve:

- route changes;
- family marriages;
- mine accidents;
- famous customers;
- old disputes;
- lost stopping places;
- vanished villages.

Not every song is historical fact.

The value is that:

> family memory travels with the family.

## Repair ethic

A possible emergent cultural value:

> **worth is not exhausted when something breaks.**

This should arise from livelihood rather than become a slogan.

It can shape:

- attitudes toward objects;
- people;
- debt;
- reputation;
- old stories.

Do not romanticize poverty / reuse.

Repair exists because:

- replacement can be expensive;
- skills matter;
- continuity matters.

---

# 7. Circuit A — creature tradition

Primary carrier:

> **Road Hound.**

This version should probably become one of the **oldest living variants**.

Working meaning:

- route companion;
- ambiguous guide;
- sometimes associated with a traveler who will not return.

Because this network moves between cultures, it also collects:

- Knocking Below stories from miners;
- Marsh Lure stories from river settlements;
- Forest Owner stories at Longwood edges.

Their ballads may accidentally become one of the main ways creature stories move across the continent.

---

# 8. Circuit A — unresolved questions

Before canon promotion:

- exact home-base ecology;
- whether the network has one language or several related speech forms;
- exact guild legal status;
- education of children while moving;
- inheritance of tools / customer rights;
- burial when death occurs far from the home base;
- whether this is one people or several interlinked service lineages.

Working preference:

> **several related / intermarried networks rather than one homogeneous tribe.**

# 9. Circuit B — highland / lowland pastoral transhumance

## Working concept

Households whose livestock economy depends on **seasonal movement between ecological zones**, especially within / around the High Roads system.

Mobility is not cultural performance.

It is productive infrastructure.

## Comparative mechanisms

From Qashqai examples:

- meaningful summer / winter quarters;
- movement coexisting with agriculture;
- repeated seasonal bases.

From Rabari research:

- permanent village attachment;
- only part of the household moving in some cases;
- route adaptation to:
  - water;
  - fodder;
  - land access;
  - political change;
- negotiated relationships with farmers.

From Fulani / Wodaabe research:

- flexible mobility types;
- household / herd splitting;
- cross-border movement;
- route governance;
- use of:
  - crop residue;
  - water;
  - markets;
- corridor restriction creating conflict.

---

# 10. Circuit B — mobility pattern

## Working default

At least two major seasonal anchors:

### warm-season / high pasture

Higher High Roads grazing zones.

### cold-season / lower base

Lower valleys / shoulders / adjacent lower ecological zones.

Exact map remains open.

The same household need not move as one unit.

Possible pattern:

~~~text
older / younger / crop-managing household members remain at one base
+
herding party moves with most animals
+
later household recombines
~~~

A different year may require a different split.

This flexibility should be normal.

---

# 11. Circuit B — land and access

They must have more than:

> permission to wander.

At different stops they may possess:

- inherited customary pasture access;
- seasonal lease;
- water agreement;
- crop-residue exchange;
- kin hospitality;
- compact-recognized passage;
- paid grazing;
- disputed traditional use.

Different jurisdictions recognize different pieces.

This makes the circuit politically real.

## Important tension

A road or irrigation project can improve the wider economy while destroying:

- one pasture corridor;
- one watering sequence;
- one timing window.

This can become a serious current-world conflict without anyone trying to persecute "nomads."

---

# 12. Circuit B — household economy

Pastoralism should not mean:

> only animals.

Possible household income includes:

- live animals;
- wool / fiber;
- dairy;
- hides;
- transport animals;
- breeding services;
- limited seasonal cultivation;
- trade at fairs;
- guiding / local route knowledge.

Different households specialize differently.

## Wealth distinctions

Possible:

- large-herd households;
- small-herd households;
- hired herders;
- households partly dependent on settled farming;
- households that have mostly settled but retain seasonal rights.

This should create internal politics.

---

# 13. Circuit B — political representation

No single pastoral ruler is required.

Representation may emerge around:

- a threatened corridor;
- a water dispute;
- a High Roads multi-route meeting;
- animal disease;
- external taxation.

Delegates may be:

- respected herd owners;
- route elders;
- people skilled at negotiation;
- representatives chosen only for one issue.

This fits High Roads polycentric politics well.

---

# 14. Circuit B — education / knowledge

Highly developed knowledge should include:

- weather;
- pasture condition;
- animal health;
- water;
- route timing;
- legal access;
- market conditions.

Children can learn through:

- household work;
- seasonal teaching;
- religious / guild schools at fixed bases;
- temporary instruction at recurring stops.

Exact education system remains open.

---

# 15. Circuit B — creature tradition

Primary:

> **Weather Animal.**

This population's version is likely:

- less monstrous;
- more observational.

Working interpretation:

> the animal does not summon weather; it knows before we do.

This may be the variant that preserves the strongest ecological truth.

Secondary:

> Road Hound

as:

- route companion;
- crossing omen.

---

# 16. Circuit B — cultural identity problem

Settled outsiders may describe them as:

- High Roads people;
- herders;
- nomads;
- foreigners.

They may identify more specifically by:

- seasonal base;
- kin network;
- herd tradition;
- route.

Avoid one universal name until the actual naming community is built.

---

# 17. Circuit C — freight / caravan service households

## Working concept

Mobile households / companies / kin networks specializing in **moving other people's goods** across difficult routes.

They are not necessarily:

- merchants;
- pastoralists;
- guild traders.

Their capital is:

- animals;
- carts / wagons;
- loading equipment;
- route knowledge;
- labor;
- trusted crews;
- stopping relationships.

## Comparative mechanisms

From Banjara research:

- freight transport as primary mobile livelihood;
- caravans / camps;
- bulk goods;
- carrier groups independent of the merchants whose goods they moved.

From Persian / Central Asian caravan research:

- temporary caravans organized under leaders;
- guard / route / provisioning responsibilities;
- subunits of animals / loads;
- stopping infrastructure.

From High Roads current canon:

- corridor compacts;
- pass towns;
- route arbitration;
- animal / guide systems;
- multilingual markets.

---

# 18. Circuit C — caravan versus community

This distinction is essential.

## Caravan

Temporary operational organization.

Can contain:

- several carrier households;
- unrelated merchants;
- guards;
- pilgrims;
- hired labor.

## Carrier community

Durable people / households whose work repeatedly creates caravans.

A single household may join different caravans in different years.

One caravan therefore should not equal:

> one tribe.

---

# 19. Circuit C — economic organization

Possible actors:

- wealthy transport households owning many animals;
- small carrier families;
- hired drovers;
- route guides;
- loaders;
- animal specialists;
- wagon / tack repair workers.

A large caravan may appoint / hire a leader responsible for:

- route;
- timing;
- guards;
- provisioning;
- lodging / stopping arrangements.

The leader's authority ends with the operation.

This preserves current world preference for **function-specific authority**.

---

# 20. Circuit C — routes

Likely major relationships:

- Port;
- HR-C switching market;
- HR-W / Stone Hills corridor;
- HR-N / North Coast corridor;
- Low Rivers bulk-food routes;
- Old Cities trade routes.

Do not lock one giant continent loop.

Different carrier families may specialize in:

- one corridor;
- one cargo class;
- one terrain.

Long-haul movements can be created by **handoffs**.

That may be more realistic than every household crossing the continent.

---

# 21. Circuit C — relationship with merchants

Merchants own / finance goods.

Carrier households move them.

This separation matters.

It creates disputes over:

- damage;
- delay;
- theft;
- route changes;
- force majeure;
- animals lost;
- inspection;
- convoy fees.

Carrier testimony can therefore matter enormously in trade litigation.

They know what actually happened between markets.

---

# 22. Circuit C — social / political status

They may be:

- economically indispensable;
- socially looked down on;
- heavily regulated;
- courted during crisis.

Large carrier houses can become rich.

Small drovers may remain precarious.

Guild centralization in Port could create:

- standardized contracts;
- credential recognition;
- easier dispute resolution;

but also:

- increased control by merchant houses;
- pressure to join formal systems.

Again:

> reform can help and centralize at the same time.

---

# 23. Circuit C — creature tradition

Primary:

> **Night Road.**

Why:

- caravans move in groups;
- bells / animals / voices carry far;
- routes preserve memory;
- travelers repeatedly hear movement they cannot identify.

Possible carrier interpretation:

> not the dead, not spirits — **the road remembering traffic**.

This should be one variant, not universal truth.

Secondary:

- Road Hound;
- Weather Animal;
- regional creatures collected from clients.

Carrier households may be major **folklore brokers** because they hear stories everywhere.

---

# 24. Relationship among the three mobile systems

They should interact without collapsing together.

## Repair network ↔ pastoral circuit

- repair tack / containers / tools;
- share seasonal fairs;
- intermarriage possible.

## Pastoral circuit ↔ freight carriers

- sell / rent transport animals;
- share water / pasture knowledge;
- compete for corridor capacity.

## Freight carriers ↔ repair network

- hire repairs;
- carry portable crafts;
- spread songs / stories.

## All three

Meet at:

- fairs;
- roadheads;
- Port;
- pilgrimage sites;
- seasonal markets.

This creates mobile social worlds without one "nomad culture."

---

# 25. First culture-to-legend payoff

The Road Hound can now be made more specific.

## Repair network version

- family-route memory;
- ambiguous protector;
- ballad subject.

## Pastoral version

- appears before a dangerous crossing / weather shift;
- less tied to death.

## Freight-carrier version

- appears outside camps / ahead of stalled caravans;
- some crews interpret it as warning of route trouble.

Then:

- North Coast;
- Port;
- religious networks

reinterpret those mobile versions.

The creature now has an actual **transmission engine**.

# 26. Stress test

## Does each circuit have a real reason to move?

- repair: YES;
- pastoral: YES;
- freight: YES.

## Can each survive without fantasy magic?

YES.

## Does each have fixed attachments?

YES.

## Are they socially / economically distinct?

YES.

## Do they overlap enough to exchange culture?

YES.

## Does any one become "the nomads"?

NO.

## Are exact names / ethnic identities premature?

YES.
