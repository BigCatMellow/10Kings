# Prose test

The guild coordinator had spent six years arguing that Port needed more permanent offices.

They had spent almost as long arguing that permanent offices should not be allowed to become government.

Most people found the distinction tiring.

The Villain did not.

That was one reason they still worked with him.

The other was that he got things done.

A disputed credential recognized.

A branch introduced to a buyer.

A copied standard corrected.

A room found.

Useful things.

That had been safe so far.

---

He asked them to meet in an ordinary office.

No guards.

No closed shutters.

Two clerks worked in the next room.

He put a copied contract abstract on the table.

"Stone Hills."

The coordinator read it.

The first page was ordinary.

Protected industrial work.

Inspection requirement.

Guaranteed purchase.

Confidential customer designation.

The second page contained the first-call clause.

They read it twice.

"Option."

"Yes."

"Not current allocation."

"No."

They looked at him.

"The shops are negotiating tomorrow."

"Yes."

There it was.

Timing.

The coordinator folded the abstract.

"You could send this yourself."

"Yes."

"Why haven't you?"

He answered:

"You believe branches should know what authority they are actually giving away."

That was enough.

The coordinator stood.

"Where is the register reference?"

He gave it to them.

They left without taking the copy.

---

The shared clerical office disliked people who arrived near closing.

It disliked them more when they knew exactly what they wanted.

The coordinator gave the register reference.

The clerk checked:

- number;
- date;
- mark.

"What do you need?"

"Clause confirmation."

"Authorized purpose?"

"Branch governance review."

The clerk looked at them.

"Which branch?"

They named it.

"Stone Hills?"

"Yes."

The clerk disappeared into the records room.

When they returned, they brought:

- the abstract;
- the annex reference;
- a confirmation slip.

The coordinator read the first-call clause again.

Same language.

Same option horizon.

Same contingency trigger.

Real.

"Copy?" they asked.

The clerk shook their head.

"Not under that access."

"Excerpt?"

"No."

"Confirmation that the clause exists?"

The clerk tapped the slip.

"That."

The coordinator looked at the page.

The clerk understood.

"Do not make me explain confidentiality to somebody whose office helped write half the reciprocal rules."

"I know the rule."

"Good."

The coordinator took the confirmation slip.

They knew exactly what would happen if they circulated more than the access allowed.

Maybe:

- branch complaint;
- loss of records access;
- guild discipline;
- nothing formal and years of nobody trusting them with a closed file.

The exact consequence mattered less than the fact that access worked only because people expected restraint.

They left.

---

The coordinator did not send the customer name.

They did not send the full annex.

They sent the Stone Hills branch:

- the exact first-call clause transcribed;
- the register reference;
- the Port confirmation that the clause existed;
- a note stating that the current allocation figures could still be accurate.

They added one sentence of their own:

> **Decide whether the proposed review is broad enough to include contingent priority claims before you accept it.**

Then they sent it.

Not publicly.

To the branch.

That distinction would matter to them later.

It would matter less to everyone else.

---

By the time the next Stone Hills meeting began, enough shop representatives had seen the clause that it could no longer remain outside the room.

The worker representative put the transcript and verification on the table.

The production supervisor read them.

Their face did not change.

"You knew."

"Yes."

"The clause is real."

"Yes."

"Would the review have included it?"

"The underlying reviewers would have seen the contract."

"Before shops accepted the package?"

A pause.

"No."

The small-shop worker laughed without humor.

The guild contract clerk intervened.

"The current protected share remains exactly what was represented."

The worker representative looked at them.

"And the first-call option?"

"Not active."

"It can become active."

"Yes."

"For longer than the review period."

"Yes."

"Then the review period was too narrow."

The supervisor answered:

"Or the option is not an allocation until it is exercised."

Both statements survived.

That was the problem.

---

The priority-line worker took the page.

"If it triggers, does that mean more work?"

The small-shop worker turned.

"That's your question?"

"It affects my house."

"So does everything."

"Yes."

The room split.

Not into workers and owners.

Into:

- people who saw hidden long-term strategic capture;
- people who saw employment security;
- people who believed the current figures remained honest;
- people who believed the compromise had defined honesty too narrowly;
- people mostly angry that somebody in Port had seen the contract before they had.

The worker representative spent several minutes correcting rumors inside the same meeting.

"No, it does not say they own the shop."

"No, it does not say permanent."

"No, we do not know the buyer from this."

"No, it does not prove every protected order has the same clause."

One worker said:

"Then what does it prove?"

The representative looked at the page.

"That 'current share' was not the only thing worth asking about."

That was enough.

---

The temporary package did not collapse.

It became harder.

Two shops said they would continue under the original review.

Another demanded that:

- contingent first-call;
- renewal;
- expansion options

be included in whatever reviewers examined.

The small neighboring shop would not accept the package until that happened.

The priority-line group opposed any action that might cause the protected buyer to withdraw.

The safety inspector reminded everyone that hours remained high regardless of contract interpretation.

For once, people listened.

Work continued the next day.

Not smoothly.

One ordinary line declined voluntary overtime.

Protected work continued.

Rumor outran the actual document by noon.

People said:

- the workshops had been promised away;
- outside buyers owned the output;
- military contracts were permanent;
- Port had ordered the whole thing.

The worker representative spent the afternoon saying:

> "That is not what it says."

Truth did not travel alone.

---

In Port, the shared clerical office sent the guild coordinator a formal note.

It was brief.

Their branch-governance access was being reviewed pending explanation of:

> **unauthorized dissemination of restricted contract substance.**

The coordinator read it.

Then read it again.

They had expected something like this.

Expectation did not make it pleasant.

A colleague stopped at their desk.

"Worth it?"

The coordinator looked at the note.

Then at the copy of the Stone Hills message.

"I don't know yet."

That was the honest answer.

---

The Villain received only a short result.

- settlement delayed;
- shops divided;
- contingent priority now inside the dispute;
- Stone Hills actors demanding clearer rules;
- no general stoppage;
- no violence.

He stopped reading there.

Enough.

The truth had not created the grievance.

It had changed what the grievance required an answer to.

He made no second intervention.

Stone Hills had the question now.

Let Stone Hills answer it.
