# 1. NC-1 — outer-island / seasonal fishing community

## Comparator A — Lofoten fishing villages

### Sources

Museum Nord / Lofoten Museum:

- https://www.museumnord.no/en/our-venues/lofoten-museum/
- https://visitlofoten.com/en/activity/museum/norwegian-fishing-village-museum-a/

Nordlandsmuseet:

- https://www.nordlandsmuseet.no/en/samling-1/rorbuer

Visit Lofoten historical overview:

- https://visitlofoten.com/en/the-history-of-lofoten/

### Source-derived mechanisms

The Lofoten material documents:

- large regular seasonal fisheries;
- local families living in fishing villages year-round;
- **traveling fishermen** arriving for the fishing season;
- seasonal fishermen's cabins / rorbuer providing:
  - sleeping;
  - cooking;
  - equipment storage;
  - workspace;
- fishing combined historically with small farming;
- village economies containing:
  - boat houses;
  - smithing / repair;
  - stores;
  - processing;
  - landlords / village owners in some historical periods.

This creates a useful distinction between:

> **resident maritime community**

and:

> **seasonal influx of fishing labor.**

### 10Kings application

NC-1 should not be an island where "everyone fishes in the same way."

Possible social categories:

- resident fishing households;
- visiting seasonal crews;
- boat owners;
- crew members;
- preservation / processing households;
- repair workers;
- farmers / herders who fish seasonally;
- harbor / lodging providers.

Three Moons or major fish runs can produce temporary crowding analogous in structure—not culture—to Port's festival strain.

### Myth consequence

The Great Mouth tradition may differ between:

- resident island households;
- visiting crews.

Residents may insist seasonal fishers misunderstand:

- water behavior;
- old place names;
- creature stories.

This creates variation **inside one harbor**, not only between regions.

### Do not borrow

- Norwegian ethnic identity;
- exact cod fishery;
- rorbu architecture one-to-one;
- fishing-village landlord system as default;
- modern Scandinavian imagery.

## Comparator B — Shetland local fishing / crofting heritage

### Source

Shetland heritage network:

- https://www.shetland.org/visit/do/history/museums-visitor-centres
- https://www.shetland.org/visit/do/family/the-cabin-museum

### Source-derived mechanisms

The Shetland heritage network emphasizes:

- many local museums / community history groups;
- distinct place-level histories;
- overlap among:
  - fishing;
  - crofting;
  - whaling;
  - maritime life;
- very strong local historical memory.

The useful comparative point is less a specific institution than:

> **small islands / districts can preserve distinct historical identities inside a wider maritime culture.**

### 10Kings application

NC-I should contain multiple island communities whose differences matter.

Do not write:

> island culture says X.

Prefer:

> one outer island tells the Great Mouth this way; a nearby island considers that version absurd.

### Research result

**NC-1 now qualifies as R2.**

# 2. LR-1 / LR-4 — water-management and floodplain communities

## Comparator A — Balinese subak

### Source

UNESCO World Heritage Centre:

- https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1194

UNESCO water-management summary:

- https://www.unesco.org/en/virtual-science-museum/voices-water/indonesia

### Source-derived mechanisms

UNESCO describes the subak system as:

- cooperative water management;
- canals / weirs linking multiple farmers;
- organized at water-source / watershed scale;
- managed by groups of farmers;
- combining:
  - practical water allocation;
  - community organization;
  - religious / cultural meaning.

The World Heritage description states that individual water collectives can include dozens to hundreds of farmers and that networks coordinate irrigation at a broader watershed scale.

### 10Kings application

Useful for LR-1:

- water association membership based on actual dependence / use;
- allocation rules distinct from ordinary territorial government;
- meetings around:
  - flow;
  - timing;
  - maintenance;
- nested coordination when one water problem exceeds one village.

This strongly supports the current Low Rivers rule that:

> state authority does not replace local water institutions.

### Important non-borrow

Do **not** import:

- Balinese Hinduism;
- water temples;
- Tri Hita Karana;
- rice-terrace visual identity;
- consensus practice as universal.

Use the institutional mechanism:

> shared water produces a durable user institution.

## Comparator B — Bangladesh floating gardens

### Source

FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems:

- https://www.fao.org/giahs/around-the-world/detail/bangladesh-floating-gardens-system/
- https://www.fao.org/giahs/giahs-around-the-world/bangladesh-floating-garden-agricultural-practices/20th-anniversary-celebrations-of-the-globally-important-agricultural-heritage-systems-%28giahs%29-programme/en

### Source-derived mechanisms

FAO documents flood / waterlogged communities developing floating cultivation systems using:

- plant residues;
- floating beds;
- submerged / flooded land.

The system provides:

- food production;
- seedlings;
- livelihood adaptation;
- integration of agriculture with persistent water.

### 10Kings application

Useful for LR-4 floodplain communities.

Do not make flooding merely:

> disaster that people endure.

Some communities should have ordinary practices built around:

- expected seasonal water;
- temporary / floating cultivation;
- movable equipment;
- changes between fish / crop use;
- knowing which land is temporarily water.

This can make the Marsh Lure tradition more specific:

> people who understand moving water best still tell stories about lights that make you misjudge where solid ground should be.

The folklore does not arise from ignorance.

It arises from living with a landscape that genuinely changes.

### Do not borrow

- exact floating-bed technology without checking local 10Kings plants / materials;
- Bengali religious / festival traditions;
- visual replication.

## Comparator C — Ahwar / Mesopotamian Marshes

### Sources

UNESCO World Heritage Centre:

- https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1481

United Nations in Iraq:

- https://iraq.un.org/en/308406-mesopotamian-marshes-world-heritage-brink

### Source-derived mechanisms

The sources support:

- long-term human life in a major inland-delta / marsh ecosystem;
- livelihoods tied to:
  - wetland ecology;
  - livestock;
  - fishing;
- cultural vulnerability when the wetland itself contracts.

### 10Kings application

Useful mainly as a reminder that:

> wetland culture disappears / changes when the wetland changes.

If Low Rivers engineering:

- drains;
- redirects;
- embanks;
- straightens;

a creature tradition like the Marsh Lure may disappear from one place and reappear somewhere else as water patterns move.

Myth can therefore become evidence of **historical landscape change**.

### Research result

- LR-1 cooperative irrigation village → **R2**
- LR-4 floodplain community → **R2**

# 3. LW-2 — interior Longwood use-right community

This is one of the strongest comparative matches found so far.

## Comparator A — Nepal community forest user groups

### Sources

FAO:

- https://www.fao.org/4/w9615e/w9615e0b.htm
- https://www.fao.org/4/ai412e/AI412E09.htm
- https://www.fao.org/4/xii/0321-c1.htm

### Source-derived mechanisms

The FAO case material describes systems in which:

- the state can retain land ownership;
- local forest users possess substantial management / product rights;
- users organize as groups;
- groups prepare:
  - charters;
  - operational plans;
  - rules;
- user assemblies can be more authoritative internally than small executive committees;
- access / management rights are connected to actual users;
- community institutions face internal equity problems:
  - wealth;
  - gender;
  - marginalization;
  - elite capture.

### Why this matters

This strongly validates the Longwood distinction:

> **sovereignty / land title / use / management / representation do not have to belong to the same institution.**

### 10Kings application

LW-2 could have:

- territorial polity sovereignty;
- community-specific use rights;
- different groups entitled to:
  - gather;
  - cut;
  - hunt;
  - graze;
  - pass;
- limited delegates for cross-community issues.

The key design improvement from the Nepal comparison is:

> **"community" itself must be politically contested.**

Questions:

- who counts as a user?
- who participates?
- who speaks?
- whose household use is invisible?
- can wealthy users dominate the representative body?

That will make Longwood rights substantially more believable.

### Myth consequence

The Forest Owner may function partly as a cultural memory of:

> the forest has users you did not bother to see.

That is stronger than:

> don't anger nature.

### Do not borrow

- Nepalese legal terminology;
- modern statutory structure;
- caste / gender relations one-to-one;
- community-forestry program history.

## Comparator B — Japanese iriai forest commons

### Sources

Masahiko Ota, *Forests* (2023):

- https://doi.org/10.3390/f14030572

Related common-property research:

- https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forpol.2016.10.012

### Source-derived mechanisms

The research describes **iriai** forest / grassland systems in which:

- residents of a particular community hold collective access / use rights;
- rights can exist separately from simple individual ownership;
- local rules can govern:
  - amount harvested;
  - time / season;
  - techniques;
  - resource categories;
- rights can include obligations to contribute labor to management;
- traditional commons have changed institutional form across time;
- state / market modernization can conflict with local common-right systems.

The 2023 study emphasizes that these institutions are not static.

### 10Kings application

This gives Longwood an especially useful rule:

> **a right to use one thing may carry an obligation to maintain another.**

Examples:

- firewood right ↔ path / firebreak labor;
- seasonal grazing ↔ boundary maintenance;
- timber right ↔ replanting / rotation;
- passage right ↔ bridge / trail work.

That makes use rights economically concrete.

It also reinforces:

> passage is not cutting.

Possessing one category does not imply all others.

### Myth consequence

The Forest Owner can be said to punish someone who:

> took a right without its obligation.

That is culturally specific enough to feel rooted in Longwood institutions.

### Do not borrow

- Japanese village forms;
- iriai terminology;
- modern cooperative law;
- Shinto/Buddhist associations;
- forestry species / aesthetics.

### Research result

**LW-2 now qualifies as R2.**

It is also close to **P1** once a particular Longwood community is selected.

# 4. P-1 — old Port harbor-worker neighborhood

## Comparator A — historical London dock labor ecology

### Source

London Museum:

- https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/jobs-that-made-londons-docks-run/

### Source-derived mechanisms

London's historical docks required many distinct occupations, including:

- stevedores;
- warehouse workers;
- tally / weighing workers;
- lightermen;
- riggers;
- general laborers;
- repair / underwater workers;
- customs;
- policing.

The museum stresses that "docker" could conceal substantial occupational specialization.

Work arrival was irregular.

Many workers depended on casual daily hiring.

### 10Kings application

P-1 should not be:

> dockworkers.

It should contain several overlapping work identities:

- shipboard loading;
- quay labor;
- warehouse handling;
- tally / record work;
- small craft / lighter transport;
- rigging / hoist operation;
- repair.

Those jobs create:

- different prestige;
- different risks;
- different guild relationships;
- different political interests.

This is useful for the Old Port Hand and Port labor factions.

### Important story consequence

A Port labor constituency can agree that Port needs:

- more revenue;
- better infrastructure;

while disagreeing over:

- automation / new equipment;
- guild credentialing;
- casual labor;
- who controls hiring.

## Comparator B — casual labor / employment tickets / call-on

### Sources

London Museum:

- https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-798737/extra-labourers-ticket/
- https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-798736/preferable-labourers-ticket/
- https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-777606/dockers-call-on/

### Source-derived mechanisms

Historical dock labor could involve:

- workers presenting themselves daily;
- no guarantee of being selected;
- formal / semi-formal priority categories;
- tickets / credentials affecting access to work.

### 10Kings application

Port's labor market could plausibly include some combination of:

- standing crews;
- guild-recognized workers;
- preferred casual labor;
- daily hires;
- festival temporary labor.

This gives Three Moons labor strain more texture.

It also makes credential centralization politically consequential:

> a better credential system can reduce fraud and simultaneously decide who gets to work.

### Do not borrow

- Victorian labor law;
- exact call-on system;
- exact ticket categories.

## Comparator C — labor solidarity / neighborhood mutual support

### Source

London Museum, 1889 dockers' strike:

- https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/blog/the-1889-london-dockers-and-tailors-strikes/

Dockworker oral-history project:

- https://www.thamesdockers.org.uk/

### Source-derived mechanisms

The London Museum material records:

- skilled and unskilled dock workers acting together during labor conflict;
- relief / food support during strike;
- support crossing occupational communities;
- docks possessing enough economic leverage that work stoppage affected the entire port system.

The oral-history project emphasizes that dock work shaped whole local communities.

### 10Kings application

P-1 can plausibly have:

- work clubs;
- burial / injury funds;
- meal / relief networks;
- kin-heavy crew relationships;
- neighborhood solidarity that becomes political quickly during crisis.

Do not automatically create a modern union.

The relevant mechanism is:

> people whose livelihoods depend on irregular dangerous shared work develop institutions for mutual protection.

### Myth consequence

The Estuary Captive version among old harbor families can survive partly because:

- harbor neighborhoods preserve occupational memory;
- stories belong to families / crews, not only archives.

The official Port record and harbor-family story can therefore diverge for centuries.

### Do not borrow

- East London identity;
- industrial-era union structure;
- specific strike history;
- English working-class dialect / imagery.

### Research result

**P-1 now qualifies as R2.**

# 5. Cross-community findings

This wave produces several useful world-level lessons.

## Seasonal population does not erase local community

NC-1 can simultaneously contain:

- permanent island households;
- temporary fishing crews.

That creates local-versus-seasonal cultural tension without xenophobia being automatic.

## Water institutions are communities of dependence

LR-1 culture can partly follow:

> who shares water

rather than:

> who lives inside one political boundary.

## Longwood rights need membership politics

The biggest improvement is:

> rights systems require decisions about who counts as a legitimate user.

That creates real conflict inside supposedly "traditional" arrangements.

## Port labor should be occupationally stratified

"Dockworker" is too broad.

Port's political constituency should include:

- different jobs;
- stable / casual work;
- credentials;
- neighborhood institutions.

This will improve both:

- opening-scene world texture;
- final Port coalition.
