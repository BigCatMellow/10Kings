# Culture Phase 4 — Architecture Vertical Slices

## Status

**Reference / design tests — not canon.**

These slices test the rebuilt [Architecture](../Culture/Architecture.md) owner against the same ordinary communities used in the daily-life and food passes.

Concrete layouts below are provisional.

The test is whether the built environment emerges naturally from:
- site;
- materials;
- labor;
- water;
- waste;
- fire;
- transport;
- class;
- property;
- history.

---

# 1. Stone Hills — mixed workshop street

## Established anchors

- mining / metalwork;
- mixed workshop-households;
- stone access;
- imported food in industrial districts;
- labor conflict;
- heavy goods movement.

## Provisional street history

The settlement began as:
- a river crossing;
- a small mine road;
- several farm / craft households.

Later:
- workshops expanded;
- one lane became the main cart route;
- houses were extended upward / backward;
- old yards became workshops;
- a guild hall replaced several smaller buildings.

The result is irregular.

## Water

Possible system:
- public well / spring;
- workshop water channel from nearby stream;
- household storage jars / tanks;
- conflict over dirty industrial discharge.

Water for quenching or washing should not automatically be drinking water.

## Waste

Problems include:
- ash;
- slag;
- animal waste;
- household waste;
- wastewater.

Poor districts may sit downhill from cleaner neighborhoods.

## Fire

High risk:
- forges;
- fuel;
- timber additions;
- attached workshops.

Plausible responses:
- masonry party walls;
- water barrels;
- regulated furnace placement;
- wider access near major workshops.

## Housing

Skilled household:
- workshop at ground level / yard;
- storage;
- family rooms above or behind;
- apprentice sleeping space.

Poor casual worker:
- rented room;
- shared privy;
- little storage;
- dangerous proximity to work.

Wealthy owner:
- residence separated from dirty production;
- private yard;
- cleaner water;
- larger storage;
- office / guest room.

## Current crisis

Labor unrest becomes visible as:
- deferred repairs;
- unsafe roofs / chimneys;
- overcrowding;
- owners investing in secure warehouses while worker housing deteriorates.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

The settlement looks industrial because of actual industrial pressures, not because the region has a “forge aesthetic.”

# 2. North Coast — secondary harbor

## Established anchors

- sheltered coves;
- fishing / shipping;
- smokehouses;
- storms;
- cold / wet weather;
- seasonal crowding.

## Site

The harbor likely exists because:
- one cove is safer than neighboring coast;
- boats can be pulled / repaired;
- fresh water is nearby;
- inland track connects to farms / pasture.

The oldest street should probably follow the shore or landing, not a planner's grid.

## Water

Fresh water is separate from seawater.

Possible sources:
- spring above town;
- protected well;
- cisterns;
- stream.

Salt spray can damage structures and contaminate poor shallow wells.

## Waste

Fish processing creates:
- offal;
- blood;
- smoke;
- brine waste.

That likely pushes some work downwind / downstream from dense housing.

## Fire

Smokehouses and tarred / timber structures create risk.

Possible responses:
- isolated smokehouse rows;
- stone bases;
- cleared work yards;
- strict hearth practice after remembered fires.

## Housing

Older harbor family:
- compact house;
- storage;
- gear drying;
- perhaps attached shed.

Seasonal boarders:
- crowded loft / room;
- temporary bunkhouse.

Wealthy ship owner:
- larger house above harbor;
- warehouse interest below;
- imported material / glass.

## Current crisis

Fish decline leaves:
- underused smokehouses;
- empty sheds;
- buildings converted to storage or housing;
- families taking boarders;
- maintenance deferred.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

The harbor's architecture is a record of maritime work and decline.

# 3. The Low Rivers — mill / market town

## Established anchors

- major rivers;
- mills;
- storage;
- market towns;
- flood risk;
- broad agriculture;
- current abundance / debt crisis.

## Site

The town likely grew around:
- ford / bridge;
- mill race;
- quay;
- market;
- storage.

The river creates both wealth and danger.

## Flood logic

Possible responses:
- raised floors;
- sacrificial ground-floor storage;
- raised paths;
- embankments;
- warehouses on slightly higher ground;
- old flood marks retained on buildings.

Not every building needs stilts.

## Water

River water may be unsuitable downstream from:
- tanners;
- slaughter;
- mills;
- dense settlement.

Wells / side channels / springs may become politically important.

## Waste

Market waste, animal manure and milling refuse accumulate quickly.

Possible systems:
- manure collection;
- riverside dumping in poorer districts;
- organized carts;
- drainage ditches.

## Housing / property

Farm family at edge:
- compound;
- animals;
- storage;
- yard.

Market household:
- shop below;
- rooms above;
- shared rear yard.

Poor laborer:
- rented room near quay / mill.

Estate agent:
- larger house with office and record storage.

## Current abundance crisis

The visual paradox:
- granaries full;
- warehouses busy;
- tenant house roof unrepaired;
- livestock pen empty because animals were sold;
- estate buys neighboring property.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

The crisis can be seen in buildings before it is explained.

# 4. The High Roads — caravan-yard quarter

## Established anchors

- route convergence;
- pack animals;
- inns;
- arbitration;
- multilingual markets;
- retaining walls;
- fuel / water pressure in some high districts.

## Site

The district forms where:
- several routes meet;
- water is accessible;
- animals can be held;
- goods can be weighed / stored;
- slopes are manageable.

## Street form

The main route must accommodate:
- animals;
- carts where terrain allows;
- baggage;
- crowds.

Side alleys can remain narrow and irregular.

## Water

Water is an economic service.

Possible system:
- cisterns;
- spring capture;
- public troughs;
- inn wells;
- water sellers in dry seasons.

Animal water can become as important as household water.

## Waste

Caravan traffic produces:
- manure;
- spoiled feed;
- animal carcasses;
- packaging waste.

A city that profits from caravans needs organized removal.

## Buildings

Caravan yard:
- enclosed court;
- stable;
- store rooms;
- sleeping rooms;
- secure gates;
- office / scales.

Poor worker housing:
- dense rooms nearby.

Local household:
- compact residence protected from traffic noise / animals.

## Current crisis

Attacks on routes lead to:
- empty inns;
- warehouses holding unsold goods;
- added gates / guards;
- temporary fortification;
- some streets losing traffic while alternate routes boom.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

Route security directly rewrites urban form.

# 5. Longwood — river town

## Established anchors

- river transport;
- timber / forest products;
- cultivation;
- wet / humid conditions;
- logging pressure;
- water / cutting rights.

## Site

The town likely exists at:
- landing;
- crossing;
- mill;
- tributary mouth;
- forest-product transfer point.

It should not simply be “hidden among trees.”

## Ground / moisture

Possible responses:
- raised floors in wet districts;
- stone / masonry footings;
- ventilation;
- covered work areas;
- steep roofs;
- drainage channels.

## Fire

Timber abundance increases fire risk.

Possible response:
- spacing;
- cleared yards;
- water access;
- masonry civic / warehouse cores;
- rules around kilns and smithing.

## Work buildings

River sheds / warehouses:
- timber storage;
- drying;
- tool repair;
- loading.

Processing creates pressure on:
- river access;
- waste;
- labor camps.

## Housing

Older local household:
- dwelling + covered work area + garden / animal space.

New logging worker:
- temporary camp / bunkhouse.

Merchant:
- warehouse-linked house closer to landing.

## Current crisis

Extraction expansion introduces:
- new road;
- worker camp;
- bridge;
- cleared bank;
- sediment / drainage change.

Architecture becomes the physical evidence that a political conflict is already underway.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

# 6. The Old Cities — irrigation-city neighborhood

## Established anchors

- dense urbanism;
- irrigation;
- water politics;
- city-state histories;
- heat;
- old walls;
- merchant / patron wealth.

## Site / history

A neighborhood may contain:
- pre-Convergence wall;
- later houses built against it;
- old canal;
- market gate;
- former estate courtyard subdivided among several families.

The city should feel older than its current government.

## Water

Possible hierarchy:
- public fountain / channel;
- neighborhood cistern;
- household storage;
- elite private cistern / garden supply.

Water access can encode class.

## Waste

Dense settlement requires:
- drains;
- night-soil removal;
- cesspits;
- regulated dumping;
- market cleaning.

A shaded elegant street can still smell terrible around the wrong corner.

## Heat

Likely responses:
- shade;
- courtyard;
- thick wall;
- roof use;
- controlled openings;
- narrow streets.

These are climate solutions, not a regional decorative style.

## Housing

Poor renters:
- subdivided rooms around once-larger court;
- shared water;
- shared cooking.

Merchant household:
- mixed shop / residence;
- storage;
- guest room.

Elite:
- private court;
- garden;
- better water;
- servants;
- separation of public / private rooms.

## Current crisis

Drought anxiety becomes visible as:
- dry public basin;
- locked private cistern;
- guarded canal gate;
- abandoned fountain;
- resentment at watered elite garden.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

Water architecture gives the politics immediate physical form.

# 7. Port — dock neighborhood

## Established anchors

- high land value;
- docks / warehouses;
- mixed population;
- fire risk;
- boarding;
- shift work;
- refugees;
- repeated rebuilding.

## Historical layers

A single block may have been:

```text
old warehouse
→ merchant office
→ fire damage
→ rebuilt upper floor
→ subdivided lodging
→ workshop added below
→ refugee families in rear rooms
```

That is more Port-like than “six regional styles mixed together.”

## Water

Port needs:
- reliable fresh water despite salt environment;
- wells / cisterns / pipes or channels where technology supports them;
- high demand from ships;
- water for firefighting.

Water access may be a major municipal institution.

## Waste

Dense mixed use creates:
- household waste;
- fish / food waste;
- animal waste;
- ship waste;
- workshop waste.

Poor neighborhoods likely suffer the most from inadequate removal.

## Fire

One of Port's defining built-environment pressures.

Plausible:
- fire districts;
- masonry warehouses;
- regulated ovens / kilns;
- shared firefighting equipment;
- rebuilding rules;
- illegal subdivisions that violate them.

## Housing

Dockworker:
- rented room / boarding house;
- little storage;
- shared kitchen / privy.

Migrant family:
- subdivided apartment;
- household work spilling into alley / courtyard.

Merchant:
- residence elsewhere;
- warehouse / office near docks.

## Current crisis

Refugees / workers create:
- overcrowding;
- unofficial rooftop / yard additions;
- fire danger;
- rent spikes;
- warehouse conversion;
- new street food / services around dense housing.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

Port's architecture is produced by land value, migration, work, law and repeated rebuilding—not by decorative cosmopolitanism.

# Cross-slice findings

## 1. Architecture can reveal economics silently

A full warehouse beside a decaying tenant house says more about the Low Rivers crisis than a paragraph of exposition.

## 2. Water is one of the strongest social variables

Across:
- the High Roads;
- the Old Cities;
- Port;
- parts of Longwood;
- river towns;

water access can shape:
- property value;
- class;
- law;
- labor;
- political conflict.

## 3. Fire can explain city form

Especially in:
- Stone Hills craft districts;
- North Coast timber harbors;
- Port;
- Longwood towns.

Repeated fires can produce:
- wider streets;
- masonry districts;
- trade relocation;
- new codes;
- class displacement.

## 4. Migration should change interiors before skylines

A migrant household may preserve:
- room use;
- guest practice;
- cooking location;
- courtyard habits;
- storage;

inside a building constructed by another local tradition.

This is a strong way to show cultural mixing without architectural theme parks.

## 5. Infrastructure creates constituencies

People depend on:
- wells;
- canals;
- bridges;
- granaries;
- mills;
- docks;
- smokehouses;
- caravan yards;
- fire brigades;
- waste removal.

Who maintains these can become politically important.

## 6. Rebuilding should create historical evidence

Disaster, conquest, migration and wealth should remain visible in:
- reused walls;
- old foundations;
- abandoned gates;
- converted buildings;
- mismatched streets;
- buried infrastructure.

# What failed from the legacy model

Do not restore:
- one region-wide silhouette;
- thematic skylines;
- “forge motifs” because Stone Hills;
- “wave motifs” because North Coast;
- forest buildings designed to visually disappear because Longwood;
- imported Earth architecture pasted wholesale onto the Old Cities;
- Port as six ethnic quarters each displaying a separate regional style.

The useful legacy material is:
- climate response;
- city/town/home scale;
- local materials;
- water;
- courtyards;
- retaining walls;
- workshops;
- storage;
- route buildings.
