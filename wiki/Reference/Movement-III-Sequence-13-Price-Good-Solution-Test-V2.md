# Prose test

Three days after leaving Longwood, Wurdren reached a roadhead that had too many carts and nowhere obvious to put them.

The first sign was the animals.

More pack animals stood in temporary lines outside the yard than inside it.

The second sign was the shouting.

Not frightened shouting.

Administrative shouting.

Wurdren recognized the difference.

A carrier beside him looked at the queue and said:

"Not again."

Wurdren did not know them.

That had never stopped anyone from complaining to him.

"Again?"

"Yesterday."

"What happened yesterday?"

"This."

They pointed.

Useful.

Wurdren looked toward the gate.

A board had been hung beside it.

Several notices were pinned beneath:

- current route status;
- inspection requirement;
- guarded departure windows;
- recognized guide marks;
- temporary restrictions on heavy loads.

The newest notice added:

> **travelers dispatched before the new requirement may verify prior departure through recognized dated route, toll, stable, or station records.**

The carrier read only the first half.

Then swore.

Wurdren read the rest.

"Heavy loads delayed on the forest corridor."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Upper turn."

Wurdren looked at them.

"What upper turn?"

The carrier turned.

"The one they rebuilt instead of taking the lower ground."

Wurdren said nothing.

The carrier continued.

"Slower grade. Fine for ordinary carts. Bad for heavy trains. Everybody who doesn't want to lose half a day comes here."

There it was.

Not accusation.

Information.

Wurdren looked at the queue again.

He had helped keep one road from using somebody's wet ground.

Several days later, another yard had too many animals.

That seemed rude of the world.

---

The inspection line moved faster than the entry line.

That was not saying much.

Wurdren bought bad tea from a stall and waited because his next hired ride was somewhere beyond the gate.

The carrier who had spoken to him stood two places ahead.

Their wagon carried:

- two sealed chests;
- bundled cloth;
- three crates marked for repair goods;
- one cage containing birds that appeared to dislike commerce.

The inspector reached them.

"Route notice."

The carrier handed over a folded document.

The inspector read it.

"Old form."

"It was current when I left."

"Not current here."

"I left five days ago."

"The rule changed two days ago."

"Then I could not have used the new form."

"Correct."

The carrier stared.

Wurdren sipped his tea.

The birds screamed.

The inspector said:

"Current route notice or transition verification."

"I have the dispatch."

"From where?"

The carrier named a smaller market town.

"Recognized mark?"

"Yes."

"Show me."

The carrier opened a document case.

Then another.

Then checked a pocket.

Their face changed.

"No."

The inspector waited.

"No what?"

"The dispatch is with the factor copy."

"Where is the factor?"

"Half a day behind."

"Then you wait."

The carrier's face reddened.

"I am booked on the second guarded departure."

"Not without verification."

"You can see the cargo."

"I can see boxes."

"The seals are valid."

"That tells me who sealed them, not when you entered the route."

The carrier turned toward the people waiting behind.

"This is theft."

Nobody agreed.

Nobody disagreed.

The inspector said:

"You can wait for the factor."

"I miss the window."

"Then you take the next one."

"Tomorrow."

"Yes."

"Feed, yard fee, lodging, buyer penalty."

"Yes."

The inspector did not sound pleased.

That made the carrier angrier.

Wurdren looked again at the posted notice.

Then at the carrier's document case.

"You stopped anywhere after dispatch?"

The carrier looked at him.

"Who are you?"

"Waiting."

"Then wait."

Fair.

Wurdren pointed at the notice.

"It says stable or station record."

The carrier looked.

Then back at the case.

This time they found a folded receipt.

A stable charge.

Dated.

Marked.

The inspector held out a hand.

The carrier passed it over.

The inspector checked:

- mark;
- date;
- route station.

Then checked the cargo seal against the commercial papers.

They wrote on the old route notice.

"Transition verified."

The carrier blinked.

"That's enough?"

"It is exactly what the notice says."

The carrier read the notice properly for the first time.

Then looked at Wurdren.

"You could have said that earlier."

"You told me to remain useless."

The inspector handed the papers back.

"Second guarded departure. Do not leave the holding yard before signal."

The carrier drove through.

The birds screamed again.

---

Wurdren moved forward.

The inspector held out a hand.

"Your papers."

"I have no wagon."

"Passenger?"

"Yes."

"Carrier?"

Wurdren named the hired cart company.

The inspector checked a list.

"Third departure."

"My ride was supposed to leave at midday."

"Third departure."

"What happened to midday?"

"Too many loads."

"Because of the forest corridor?"

"Partly."

Wurdren looked at the queue.

"How partly?"

The inspector looked at him.

"Do you want a number?"

"No."

"Good. I don't have one."

They marked his travel chit.

"Stay in the passenger yard."

"Why?"

"So we know who is inside the guarded departure."

"Do people keep leaving?"

"Yes."

"Then how do you know?"

"We inspect again."

"Efficient."

"No."

The inspector handed the chit back.

"Safer."

That was harder to mock.

---

The passenger yard had:

- travelers;
- two families;
- a merchant apprentice;
- three hired guards;
- one man asleep on a grain sack.

Wurdren sat beside a woman selling flatbread from a basket.

She recognized his travel chit.

"Third window."

"Apparently."

"Bad day."

"Apparently."

She handed him bread.

He paid.

"You from the forest road?"

"Recently."

"They're the reason this place is full."

"Partly."

She looked at him.

"You know why they changed the turn?"

"Yes."

"They should have used the lower one."

"It crossed ground the road agreement didn't cover."

She shrugged.

"Road work."

"Not to the people using the ground."

"My cousin has six animals sitting in this yard."

"That's real."

"So is the buyer waiting on him."

"Yes."

She studied him.

"You were there when they chose it?"

Wurdren hesitated.

"I was there."

"You agreed with it?"

"Mostly."

"Then you agreed with this too."

He looked around the crowded yard.

"No."

She frowned.

He corrected himself.

"I mean I didn't choose this. But I suppose this is part of it."

"That's a very careful sentence."

"I have been around clerks."

She sold bread to someone else.

The conversation might have ended there.

Instead she said:

"The lower ground worth that much?"

"To the people using it."

"More than the delay?"

"I don't know."

"Useful."

He had heard that word enough lately.

The woman continued.

"My cousin would say no."

"The people there said yes."

"Of course they did."

"They actually didn't all."

That made her pause.

He told her only:

- the lower turn was cheaper;
- the upper turn was slower;
- the wet ground belonged to other uses;
- one local person still opposed widening entirely.

No speech.

No moral.

She listened.

Then said:

"I still prefer the lower turn."

"Fair."

That was as far as they got.

Wurdren ate the rest of the bread.

The Longwood decision still seemed right.

That was the irritating part.

---

The third departure did not leave until late afternoon.

Before the gate opened, inspectors checked:

- cargo marks;
- carrier list;
- guard assignments;
- passenger count.

One guard complained.

The inspector ignored them.

The road outside the yard was busy but orderly.

For the first several miles, Wurdren could see why the system existed.

Guarded groups moved together.

Unregistered wagons were held at side lots.

Route notices were posted at each station.

A rider came past with an updated warning about a damaged bridge farther on.

The information reached the whole departure before they reached the bridge.

That was useful.

Very useful.

Wurdren disliked that too.

At the evening stop, he saw the carrier with the birds.

They were feeding the animals.

"Buyer penalty?" Wurdren asked.

The carrier looked up.

"Smaller than overnight."

"Good."

"No."

"Reasonable?"

"Don't become a clerk."

Wurdren sat on a low wall.

The carrier finished pouring feed.

"Thanks."

"For what?"

"The notice."

"You had the evidence."

"I didn't know it mattered."

"Neither did I until I read the board."

The carrier looked toward the guarded wagons.

"You think these rules stay?"

Wurdren followed their gaze.

The inspectors were setting tomorrow's departure board.

"I don't know."

"They're useful."

"Yes."

"I hate them."

"Also yes."

The carrier nodded.

That seemed to be enough agreement for one day.
