# Community Institutions — Comparative Casebook

## Status

**Non-canon reference / design tests. Researched 2026-11-04.** This document follows the [Cultural Research Library](Cultural-Research-Library.md) and the [Cultural Practices and Institutions](Cultural-Practices-and-Institutions.md) cases. It investigates **what a practice accomplishes for a specific community**, how the institution persists, and which material constraints make it possible. The examples below are **new comparative research**, not recovered ChatGPT transcripts.

**No regional culture, specific local institution, named settlement, new route, property rule or fictional ceremony is promoted here.** Current owners—[Cultural Inspiration](../Culture/Culture-and-Influence.md), [Nomads](../Culture/Nomads.md), [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md), [Food](../Culture/Food.md), [Trade and Dependencies](../Economy/Trade-and-Dependencies.md), and [World Rules](../World-Rules.md)—remain authoritative. Treat proposed scenes as test cases to refine later, not instructions to make them canon.

## Case method

For each example:
1. **Grounded real-world institution** — where, who participates, why it exists and what the source does/does not prove.
2. **Necessary infrastructure** — labor, time, ecology, craft, routes, knowledge, records and material constraints.
3. **Rights and conflicts** — who allocates access, who bears costs, and what can break the system.
4. **Transmission** — family, apprenticeships, public repetition, teaching, itinerant labor, imitation and change.
5. **Two Sons test** — a hypothetical local community, its political friction, its contact history, and a human-scale scene.
6. **Exclusions** — features of the original tradition that should not be borrowed whole or declared universal.

A named real-world case does not imply an entire region of Two Sons should resemble that country or group.

## 1. Omani aflaj — water allocation as village governance

**Grounded:** Oman has multiple types of *falaj* water channels using gravity to transport groundwater, spring or surface water. UNESCO describes community time-share allocation, sluice operation, sundial-based timing, and shares supporting the system's own maintenance. This is a **living engineering and property institution**, not simply a decorative irrigation device.

**Source:** [UNESCO World Heritage — Aflaj Irrigation Systems of Oman](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1207/).

**Requirements:** Appropriate topography and water source; upkeep of channels and sluices; predictable allocations; skilled timekeeping; maintainers; means of enforcing shared rights; emergency rules when the flow drops.

**Power and friction:** A shareholder with good land may not control the water clock. A neglected channel can damage everyone. A shortfall is not purely a technical problem: it changes obligations, family wealth, and access to food. Do not extrapolate one local ownership system to all Omani settlements.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — a Sunplains hillside ward:** Families own different plots but depend on a shared gravity-fed channel. A clockkeeper allocates turns; a public record is challenged after a blocked intake. A visiting trader offers to finance repairs in return for priority water access. One household refuses because priority is governed by inherited obligations, not simply purchase. *No such ward, charter, or official exists in canon.*

**Contact pathway:** A visiting canal technician or marriage between watershed communities introduces a new timing method without importing the entire governance system.

**Owner questions:** [Economy](../Economy/Trade-and-Dependencies.md) for dependency; [Law and Jurisdiction](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md) for rights; [Food](../Culture/Food.md) for irrigation constraints.

**Do not copy:** Sacred practice, specific Arabic terminology as fantasy flavor, or the assumption that all water rights must be allocated by the same system.

## 2. Persian qanats — infrastructure depends on an invisible labor geography

**Grounded:** In arid Iran, traditional *qanat* systems tap aquifers and carry water through gently sloped underground tunnels. Access shafts aid construction, ventilation and debris removal. UNESCO describes associated reservoirs, mills, maintenance corridors and communal management.

**Source:** [UNESCO World Heritage — The Persian Qanat](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1506/).

**Requirements:** Viable aquifer, gradient engineering, skilled workers who can excavate safely, access and maintenance openings, compatible land use, protection of the catchment, and long-term cooperation. This is expensive infrastructure with failure risk.

**Power and friction:** A project may serve people far from the source. An upstream change, collapsed shaft or deliberate obstruction can jeopardize multiple landholders. A guild can monopolize excavation knowledge, but cannot conjure groundwater where none exists.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — a dry-edge market town:** A surface-road renovation blocks access to maintenance shafts. The town's mills and orchards lose water gradually. A contracted crew discovers that municipal officials and the road-building patron used incompatible maps. Locals disagree over whose records count. This tests Council leverage through dependencies without making the Council omniscient.

**Contact pathway:** Tunnel-builders travel across settlements under contracts, transmitting measurement techniques and terms for components. Some villages retain local water councils; others treat the channels as a landlord's asset.

**Owners:** [Architecture](../Culture/Architecture.md), [Records](../Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md), [Trade](../Economy/Trade-and-Dependencies.md).

**Do not copy:** A 'secret ancient tunnel network' without maintenance labor, material costs and geologic feasibility; no automatic relation to [the Underpass](../Places/The-Underpass.md).

## 3. Xochimilco chinampas — urban agriculture as ecological maintenance

**Grounded:** The FAO describes chinampa cultivation in Mexico City's Xochimilco area as intensive, historically rooted raised-field agriculture within canals and wetland systems. Farmers maintain soil fertility, water access, vegetation and drainage. The practice connects local ecological knowledge, food markets, households and civic life.

**Source:** [FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems — Chinampas Agricultural System](https://www.fao.org/giahs/giahs-around-the-world/mexico-chinampas-agricultural-system/en).

**Requirements:** Appropriate shallow wetland ecology; canal dredging and organic matter; local agricultural labor; boats; water management; transport to markets. A chinampa is **not a floating raft**. Avoid applying the model to an arbitrary deep-water harbor.

**Power and friction:** Productive gardens are also waterways. A boat passage can conflict with planting, water pollution or erosion. Urban land values may displace farmers. Land tenure, market demand and pollution are as important as crop names.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — Greenvale floodplain edge near a market:** Generations of growers have built raised wetland plots. A shipping interest proposes widening a canal. Larger vessels could shorten freight delays but destabilize banks and alter silt deposition. A neighborhood negotiates with market authorities, not a single 'Greenvale farmer' speaking for everyone.

**Contact pathway:** Workers and merchants introduce new crops, preservation methods and tools. Households may borrow techniques while retaining distinct rituals, inheritance customs and planting schedules.

**Owners:** [Food](../Culture/Food.md), [Geography](../Geography-and-Connections.md), [Daily Life](../Culture/Daily-Life-and-Customs.md).

**Do not copy:** Aztec labels as costume; treat the tradition as an evolving living agricultural system, not an unchanged relic.

## 4. Transhumance — a route is a multi-community institution

**Grounded:** UNESCO describes seasonal movement of people and livestock along pastoral routes, often in spring and autumn. Herders transmit environmental knowledge and animal-care practices by observation and participation. Settlements along the route contribute lodging, festivals, commerce and conflict-management.

**Source:** [UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — Transhumance, the Seasonal Droving of Livestock](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/transhumance-the-seasonal-droving-of-livestock-01964).

**Requirements:** Distinct seasonal grazing conditions; access to corridors, bridges, watering places and resting grounds; animal health, food storage, herders, dogs and sometimes horses; permission or custom across jurisdictions.

**Power and friction:** A convenient fence, new toll or reclaimed field can break a route much older than the present border. A village may depend on animal trade yet resent damaged crops. People who stay behind may own part of the herds, receive remittances or handle legal negotiations.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — Highridge seasonal passage:** Two related households follow different elevations because their animals differ. A bridge closure forces one to cross a market-town common with disputed access rights. Local shopkeepers profit from the livestock market while growers demand restitution. Neither group is automatically 'the nomads' or 'the settlers.'

**Contact pathway:** Seasonal return carries music, repairs, marriages, food preservation and gossip into villages that otherwise communicate slowly.

**Owners:** [Nomads](../Culture/Nomads.md), [Movement](../Movement-and-Information.md), [Law](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

**Do not copy:** A single uniform pastoral culture or unqualified 'ancient freedom' narrative; route rights and work are specific and negotiated.

## 5. Marshallese stick charts — maps as education rather than equipment

**Grounded:** Marshallese navigators developed stick-and-shell teaching charts representing islands and wave/swell patterns. Museum sources distinguish schematic training patterns from navigational aids depicting particular areas. The charts are associated with knowledge learned before voyaging; they are **not necessarily objects carried aboard as direct sailing directions**.

**Sources:** [Library of Congress — Navigating the Pacific Ocean](https://www.loc.gov/exhibitions/treasures-from-the-library-of-congress/about-this-exhibition/guiding-memory/navigating-the-pacific-ocean/); [Penn Museum — Mattang navigational chart](https://collections.penn.museum/collections/object/60518); [Cambridge University Library — Marshall Islands sailing charts](https://www.lib.cam.ac.uk/rcs/projects-exhibitions/marshall-islands-sailing-charts).

**Requirements:** Repeated observation of local sea conditions, physical practice, teacher-apprentice relationships, safe training contexts, and a reason for the technique to remain useful. Distinct charts serve distinct pedagogical functions.

**Power and friction:** An outsider who copies the object but lacks the practical training cannot necessarily interpret it. Expert navigators may disagree with officials relying on a more legible map. An apprentice's local skill may outperform a richly appointed captain's theoretical records in certain conditions.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — a Northwind island fishing network:** Training aids encode characteristic wave and shoal interactions. A merchant purchases a diagram and mistakenly assumes it grants passage knowledge. A fisher recognizes the chart as a mnemonic used to teach a specific route behavior, not a complete map.

**Contact pathway:** Visiting sailors learn a method of instruction while substituting local wave phenomena and their own terminology. Expert training changes first; the public symbolism of charts follows later.

**Owners:** [Movement](../Movement-and-Information.md), [Education](../Education-and-Knowledge-Transmission.md), [Records](../Records-Literacy-and-Authentication.md).

**Do not copy:** Specific Marshallese knowledge systems, names or restricted routes as fantasy 'magic cartography'; do not universalize a single chart interpretation.

## 6. Dry-stone construction — infrastructure as social apprenticeship

**Grounded:** UNESCO documents multiple European dry-stone construction traditions. Skilled builders select and arrange stone without binding mortar, adapting to local terrain and climate. Walls, bridges, shelters, paths and other structures require knowledge of stability and regular repair; training is socially transmitted across places.

**Source:** [UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — Art of Dry Stone Construction](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/art-of-dry-stone-construction-knowledge-and-techniques-02106).

**Requirements:** Suitable stone supply, experienced judgment, labor availability, safe foundation and drainage choices, and maintenance over time. It does not make every arch or high bridge easy or safe.

**Power and friction:** A farm boundary may be a skilled cooperative investment, but the wall can also carry a disputed land claim. A traveling mason may pass techniques between two villages while local craft norms diverge. Storm damage triggers obligations and accusations.

**Hypothetical Two Sons test — Ironcrest/Highridge foothill hamlets:** After a storm, several villages must rebuild a shared retaining wall on an old pack route. The labor-sharing custom conflicts with a guild contractor's fee schedule. One family has the needed stone expertise but a weak formal property claim.

**Contact pathway:** Seasonal workers marry into new villages and teach foundations, drainage and tooling while adapting style to local stone and politics.

**Owners:** [Architecture](../Culture/Architecture.md), [Guilds](../Politics/Guilds.md), [Law](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

**Do not copy:** Regional stereotype that every mountain dweller is a mason; do not claim a shared legal structure across all the UNESCO-listed traditions.

## Cross-case design tests (still NOT fictional canon)

| Fictional community scale | Contact mechanism | Observable local practice | Dispute and counterexample |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sunplains hillside water ward | Visiting surveyor teaches an improved flow gauge | Public water-turn record and periodic channel upkeep | Poor household may have labor obligations but no voting share; rich landowner may lack priority |
| Greenvale wetland market | Migrating cultivators plus shipping merchants | Raised-field cultivation, canal maintenance and mixed local cuisine | Boat access and farmer irrigation needs conflict; adjacent dryland village follows different practices |
| Highridge pastoral corridor | Recurrent seasonal herds through three towns | Resting place rights, livestock fairs, shared animal-care vocabulary | New fence breaks path; a settled relative manages many herders' business |
| Northwind fishing harbor | Navigator apprenticeship exchanges among islanders | Training diagrams and oral lessons about waves | Copying diagram without practice fails; distant Northwind harbor uses different technique |
| Ironcrest/Highridge foothill ridge | Intermarriage and itinerant skilled workers | Stone-building maintenance society, shared repair labor | Property boundary dispute; some members earn wages instead of participating |
| Port immigrant neighborhood | Multi-generation arrival of fishers, record clerks, traders | Borrowed training, tools, foods and translated agreements | Second generation may be multilingual and urban, with no desire to return to ancestral occupation |

### A useful minimum for a **single community** profile

Define **one specific locale or circuit** (not an entire region), then test:
1. Who lives there and who is absent seasonally?
2. How does household income or food arrive?
3. Which infrastructure must work every week?
4. Who owns it, who maintains it, and who has voice?
5. What local knowledge is hard for an outsider to buy or copy?
6. How does a young person become skilled?
7. What neighborhood or occupational factions disagree?
8. Which custom changed within the last two generations, and why?
9. Who visits, marries, trades, studies or migrates between communities?
10. What accident or policy would produce **second- and third-order consequences**?
11. What is an in-world stereotype that locals themselves dispute?
12. Which underlying facts remain provisional and need author confirmation?
