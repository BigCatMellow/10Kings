# Stone Hills Functional Governance Test

## Status

**Provisional structural test.**

This page tests which political functions Stone Hills institutions need to perform before assigning a final constitutional form.

It does **not** establish:

- one Stone Hills kingdom;
- an absolute crown;
- a capital;
- exact borders;
- named mining districts;
- exact guild privileges;
- exact labor law;
- exact mine ownership patterns;
- one uniform tax system.

Stone Hills is not reduced to mining by this test. The purpose is to examine the distinctive political pressures already created by concentrated extraction, craft production, dangerous work, fuel, credit, food dependence, and strategic metal.

Current owners constraining the test:

- [Stone Hills](../Regions/Ironcrest.md)
- [Kingdoms and Politics](../Politics/Kingdoms-and-Politics.md)
- [Guilds](../Politics/Guilds.md)

The working question is:

> **How much authority can larger Stone Hills rulers consolidate while mines, towns, guilds, owners, workers, creditors, and infrastructure remain independently necessary to make the economy function?**

---

# 1. Existing constraints

Stone Hills already contains or historically contains:

- smith-chiefs;
- mine holders;
- fortified towns;
- valley rulers;
- merchant families;
- warbands;
- modern rulers with greater consolidation than the pre-Convergence baseline;
- guild centers;
- mines;
- workshops;
- farms;
- herding communities;
- old estates;
- forest and fuel dependencies;
- food imports;
- Council-linked credit.

The region owner already gives the central political constraint:

> a ruler can command soldiers but cannot simply order the economy to function.

The current labor unrest and unusual metal movements make that constraint active rather than theoretical.

---

# 2. The main distinction — sovereignty, ownership, and productive control are different

Stone Hills contains several powers that can look similar from a distance.

## Territorial authority

Can plausibly claim some combination of:

- taxation;
- policing;
- military force;
- land law;
- major roads;
- criminal jurisdiction;
- diplomacy.

## Resource ownership or tenure

A mine holder may control:

- extraction rights;
- shafts and works;
- housing;
- local stores;
- equipment;
- debt.

That does not automatically make the mine holder sovereign.

## Guild / professional authority

Guilds may control parts of:

- training;
- certification;
- quality;
- contracts;
- professional discipline;
- welfare;
- reputation.

That does not automatically make the guild the government.

## Productive control

Actual production also depends on:

- skilled workers;
- fuel;
- water;
- transport;
- food;
- credit;
- repair;
- safety;
- labor peace.

A ruler can possess legal authority while lacking direct control over several of these.

This is the central Stone Hills political problem.

---

# 3. Minimum functions by scale

## Household / workshop

Needs authority over:

- ordinary labor;
- tools;
- household property;
- small contracts;
- craft practice within recognized rules.

## Mine / estate / industrial holding

Needs capacity for:

- extraction;
- maintenance;
- work organization;
- safety practices;
- storage;
- local roads;
- housing where tied to the holding;
- private investment.

It should not automatically possess final authority over workers, nearby towns, forests, rivers, or outside roads.

## Guild / profession

Needs some role in:

- training;
- certification;
- workmanship;
- dispute mediation;
- professional reputation;
- mutual aid;
- perhaps safety standards where historically developed.

Exact legal privilege can vary by polity.

## Town / district

Needs authority over:

- markets;
- housing;
- fire;
- water;
- roads;
- local policing;
- public works;
- disputes;
- traffic created by mines and workshops.

A mining town should not be treated as merely an appendage of the nearest mine.

## Larger polity

Needs meaningful capacity for some combination of:

- taxation;
- military mobilization;
- serious crime;
- major roads;
- strategic metal policy;
- cross-district disputes;
- large infrastructure;
- food-security response;
- diplomacy;
- Convergence obligations.

## Cross-border

Trade, credit, food supply, forest/fuel access, and routes connect Stone Hills to neighboring regions.

The Convergence becomes relevant where protected passage, recognized claims, or interstate conduct are implicated.

# 4. Test 1 — major mine collapse

## Condition

A deep mine suffers a collapse, fire, flood, or ventilation disaster.

Workers are trapped.

Production stops.

Families demand rescue and accountability.

## Immediate authority

Mine management has:

- maps;
- tools;
- crews;
- technical knowledge.

Workers and guild specialists may have the best practical rescue knowledge.

A nearby town may have:

- healers;
- food;
- shelter;
- records;
- crowd control.

## Wider authority

A serious disaster may require:

- outside labor;
- legal investigation;
- relief;
- debt suspension;
- compensation;
- enforcement against an owner who hides evidence;
- closure of unsafe works.

## Result

**PASS for shared operational and public authority.**

Mine owners need substantial operational control.

They cannot plausibly be the only investigators, judges, and enforcers after a disaster.

This supports outside legal authority plus strong professional knowledge.

---

# 5. Test 2 — labor strike across mines and workshops

## Condition

Workers organize around:

- wages;
- safety;
- debt;
- hours or obligations;
- guild leadership;
- injury support.

Production slows across several sites.

## What mine owners can do

They can:

- negotiate;
- replace some labor;
- use reserves;
- pressure debtors;
- close works.

## What guilds can do

Depending on local structure, guilds may:

- mediate;
- discipline members;
- support workers;
- support masters;
- split internally.

Guild membership should not be assumed to map cleanly onto class.

## What towns and rulers can do

They may:

- police violence;
- protect roads;
- mediate;
- ban gatherings;
- compel reopening;
- pressure owners;
- provide relief;
- seize or purchase strategic output.

Every choice creates political consequences.

## Result

**PASS for labor politics that cannot be reduced to ruler versus worker.**

The conflict naturally creates coalitions among:

- workers;
- masters;
- mine owners;
- guild factions;
- towns;
- lenders;
- rulers;
- merchants.

That is stronger than a single centralized labor system.

---

# 6. Test 3 — ore body crosses ownership or jurisdiction boundaries

## Condition

A productive seam extends under:

- another estate;
- town land;
- a political boundary;
- a forest or watershed used by another community.

## Questions

Who owns the underground extension?

Who can sink a new shaft?

Who is liable if excavation damages another property or water source?

Who receives tax or royalty?

Can one authority block access to another's deposit?

## Result

**PASS for layered subsurface, surface, and political rights.**

Stone Hills cannot treat land ownership as a single indivisible right.

Mining naturally creates disputes where:

- surface ownership;
- extraction rights;
- water rights;
- political jurisdiction

do not perfectly align.

This requires records, surveying, courts or arbitration, and enforceable boundaries of authority.

---

# 7. Test 4 — fuel and forest shortage

## Condition

Metal production expands faster than available charcoal, timber, or other locally appropriate fuel supply.

Pressure moves toward:

- forests;
- Longwood margins;
- transport routes;
- alternative fuels;
- higher prices.

## Political conflict

Mine and workshop owners want fuel.

Rural communities need:

- building material;
- pasture;
- household fuel;
- watershed protection.

Forest-edge communities may reject rights claimed by distant rulers or old contracts.

## Result

**PASS for resource politics beyond the mine itself.**

Stone Hills industrial power depends on landscapes and communities outside extraction sites.

A ruler who protects metal output by simply granting unlimited cutting rights can create:

- erosion;
- water problems;
- rural resistance;
- cross-border conflict.

This makes forest and fuel rights part of industrial governance, not scenery.

---

# 8. Test 5 — Low Rivers food disruption

## Condition

A trade dispute, flood, price shock, or transport failure reduces food imports into industrial districts.

## Immediate effects

Food prices rise.

Workers demand higher wages.

Mine owners face:

- unrest;
- labor flight;
- higher operating costs.

Towns face:

- shortages;
- hoarding;
- relief needs.

## Larger political response

A ruler may try to:

- purchase grain;
- prioritize roads;
- reduce tolls;
- subsidize imports;
- release reserves;
- pressure merchants.

But success depends on:

- Low Rivers sellers;
- transport;
- credit;
- warehouses;
- local distribution.

## Result

**PASS for strong territorial authority constrained by external dependency.**

Military control of mines does not equal economic self-sufficiency.

Food access is a political limit on industrial power.

---

# 9. Test 6 — a mine or merchant house becomes locally dominant

## Condition

One owner or credit network controls:

- the largest mine;
- worker housing;
- local stores;
- debt;
- transport contracts;
- political patronage.

The nearby town becomes economically dependent on it.

## Risk

Private economic power begins to substitute for public authority.

Workers and small businesses may find that:

- losing a job also means losing housing;
- debt affects political loyalty;
- a private guard behaves like police;
- town officials cannot enforce judgments.

## Wider response

A larger ruler or neighboring town may intervene because private dominance threatens:

- tax collection;
- public order;
- military recruitment;
- political legitimacy.

## Result

**PASS for a boundary between ownership and sovereignty.**

Stone Hills can contain extremely powerful private holders without letting them become unchallenged miniature states.

The friction between private productive power and public political authority should be recurring.

---

# 10. Test 7 — central metal export restriction or requisition

## Condition

A ruler responds to war fears or unusual metal movements by attempting to:

- restrict export;
- reserve high-quality metal for domestic use;
- requisition weapons;
- license large contracts;
- inspect warehouses;
- ban private sales to rivals.

## What the ruler may control

Potential levers include:

- roads;
- customs;
- official markets;
- military force;
- major contracts;
- recognized mine rights.

## What the ruler cannot assume

The ruler may not know:

- every private contract;
- every stockpile;
- every smuggling route;
- every guild relationship;
- every Council-linked credit arrangement.

Owners and merchants may divert metal through:

- legal intermediaries;
- false grades;
- delayed records;
- neighboring jurisdictions;
- Underpass routes.

## Result

**PASS for meaningful central strategic power with incomplete economic control.**

This matches the Council model: rulers matter, but private networks can route around policy.

It also gives the current unusual-metal movement several plausible mechanisms without deciding which one is true.

---

# 11. Test 8 — cross-border route or metal dispute

## Condition

A Stone Hills polity is accused of:

- blocking protected road access;
- discriminatory metal export;
- seizing foreign cargo;
- financing armed groups across a border;
- violating a recognized mining or infrastructure claim.

## Convergence threshold

Not every commercial disagreement becomes a Convergence dispute.

The issue becomes cross-border treaty politics when it implicates:

- protected passage;
- recognized claims;
- attached agreements;
- another core settlement obligation.

## Result

**PASS for polity-level diplomacy plus specialized evidence.**

A ruler or other recognized political authority may answer internationally.

But the case may depend on records held by:

- mines;
- guilds;
- merchants;
- towns;
- lenders;
- route authorities.

Diplomatic authority and factual control are therefore not the same thing.

---

# 12. Functional map that survives all eight tests

| Level | Functions strongly supported | Functions that should not automatically be assigned here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household / workshop | ordinary labor, tools, small contracts, craft practice | mine regulation, diplomacy |
| Mine / holding | extraction, work organization, maintenance, private storage and investment | final criminal law, total control of workers/towns, sovereign foreign policy |
| Guild / profession | training, standards, reputation, mutual aid, professional disputes | all labor politics, territorial government |
| Town / district | housing, markets, fire, water, roads, policing, local disputes | strategic control of the whole region |
| Larger polity | taxation, force, major roads, higher enforcement, strategic metal policy, diplomacy | direct operation of every mine, workshop, guild, credit network |
| Cross-border / Convergence | protected passage, recognized claims, interstate disputes, agreed arbitration | ordinary industrial administration |

This is a functional distribution, not a constitution.

---

# 13. Strong structural conclusion

Stone Hills currently works best as:

> **a comparatively consolidated territorial political order layered over strong mine, town, guild, ownership, labor, and credit institutions that rulers cannot simply replace.**

The tests strongly support:

1. greater large-polity coercive and fiscal capacity than the old fragmented pre-Convergence baseline;
2. powerful mine and merchant interests without automatic private sovereignty;
3. towns that remain genuine civic actors rather than company settlements by default;
4. guild authority over professional life without assuming guild control over the whole economy;
5. recurring conflict between public political authority and private productive control;
6. dependence on food, fuel, water, roads, and credit as limits on military or industrial centralization;
7. strategic state interest in metal flows without complete visibility into private networks;
8. Convergence representation by actual political authorities while evidence and implementation remain distributed.

The tests do **not** establish one absolute monarchy or one regional government.

---

# 14. Political consequences

## A stronger state can coexist with stronger private institutions

Consolidation after the Convergence need not mean mine holders, towns, or guilds became irrelevant.

It may instead mean rulers became better able to:

- tax;
- arbitrate;
- mobilize;
- regulate;
- bargain with them.

That produces a negotiated state rather than either feudal fragmentation or modern bureaucracy.

## Productive capacity is not command capacity

A ruler may claim the region's metal while discovering that production still depends on:

- skilled labor;
- fuel;
- food;
- credit;
- transport;
- guild knowledge.

This turns economic coordination into political bargaining.

## Industrial crisis can become constitutional crisis

A strike, mine disaster, fuel shortage, or metal seizure can quickly become a dispute over:

- who may command labor;
- who may inspect private property;
- who pays for safety;
- whether a town can resist a mine owner;
- whether a ruler can requisition strategic goods.

That is useful middle worldbuilding because institutions reveal themselves through pressure.

# 15. Writing the Sequel pressures

Possible later tests:

- an emergency mine-safety office survives after the disaster that created it;
- a temporary wartime metal board becomes a permanent licensing institution;
- a mine town wins legal privileges after an owner becomes too dominant;
- a worker mutual-aid fund becomes politically powerful;
- an old forest concession remains valid long after the original furnace closes;
- a ruler's attempt to control smuggling creates a permanent road inspection system;
- a guild reform originally intended for quality control becomes a labor-rights precedent.

These are **development prompts**, not current canon.

---

# 16. Breath scene seeds

Keep these non-canon unless later promoted:

- workers argue over whether a mine is unsafe while everyone agrees the inspector is technically correct;
- a town resents the mine owner and still panics when the mine threatens to close;
- a guild master defends a striker's workmanship while condemning the strike;
- a ruler's metal inspector knows less about grades than the warehouse clerk being inspected;
- a village blames furnace demand for forest loss while selling charcoal because it pays better than farming;
- a rumor about hidden weapon stockpiles begins as a dispute over mislabeled ingots.

---

# 17. Still open

Do **not** settle without further testing:

- number of Stone Hills polities;
- extent of the most powerful ruler's territory;
- ruler / title;
- capital;
- exact mine-tenure systems;
- exact guild privileges;
- exact labor institutions;
- exact town charters or civic rights;
- exact forest / fuel law;
- exact strategic-metal regulation;
- exact military structure;
- exact Council penetration of ownership and credit;
- exact Underpass role in legal and illegal metal movement;
- exact Convergence signatories or delegates.

Those questions should be answered against this functional model rather than by assuming either an absolute crown or permanent fragmentation.
