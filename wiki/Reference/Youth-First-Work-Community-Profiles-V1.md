# Youth and First-Work Community Profiles — V1

## Status

**Provisional scene-capable community profiles — not canon.**

Research basis:

- [Youth, First Work, and Courtship — Comparative Research V1](Youth-First-Work-Comparative-Research-V1.md);
- [Community Internal Variation Pass — V1](Community-Internal-Variation-Pass-V1.md);
- current P2 community types.

This page does **not** establish universal biological age bands.

Terms such as:

- child;
- apprentice;
- new worker;
- young adult

refer to **social position / responsibility** more than exact years.

---

# 1. SH-1 — industrial apprentice / new-worker cohort

## Entry into work

Possible paths:

- formal craft apprenticeship;
- mine support / hauling work;
- family workshop labor;
- casual hiring;
- technical clerk / record training.

These are not equivalent in prestige.

## First-status thresholds

Strong candidates:

- trusted with expensive tool independently;
- allowed to sign / witness completed work;
- first dangerous shift without close supervision;
- first wage / piece payment kept partly under own control;
- first accepted repair / inspection.

No single threshold should define adulthood.

## Peer spaces

Likely:

- apprentice loft / yard;
- worker hall edge spaces;
- cheap food / drink shops;
- public well / market;
- sports / games where existing leisure pass supports them;
- evening music / storytelling.

A guild hall can be both:

- adult institution;
- youth social world.

## Status competition

Young people may compare:

- difficult jobs;
- speed;
- tool ownership;
- scars / risk stories;
- skilled master;
- wage offer;
- recruiter interest.

This can produce dangerous incentives.

## Adult anxieties

- poaching by rival owner / Blackbellow recruiter;
- young workers taking unsafe priority work for better pay;
- apprentices abandoning training;
- outside fashions / speech;
- labor agitation;
- lack of labor solidarity.

Different adults worry about opposite things.

## Myth use

**Knocking Below** among young workers:

- dare to ignore it;
- joke about hearing it;
- accuse frightened apprentice of inventing it;
- privately ask older worker what rhythm matters.

A young technically trained worker may say:

> it's rock stress

and still leave when an old crew leaves.

## Courtship / relationship layer

Likely social contact occurs through:

- work halls;
- markets;
- neighborhood gatherings;
- apprenticeships / connected households.

Do not make marriage guild-arranged by default.

Work reputation may matter socially because:

- income;
- danger;
- skill

are visible.

## Current-crisis effect

Strategic production offers an accelerated route to:

- money;
- status;
- independence.

It can also:

- increase injury;
- break apprenticeship;
- deepen owner control.

This makes youth one constituency in the Stone Hills escalation, not background dependents.

# 2. NC-1 — first-season / young island-worker cohort

## Entry into maritime work

Possible progression:

- shore errands;
- sorting / preservation;
- gear repair;
- short safe-water tasks;
- small-boat work;
- first full seasonal fishery.

The key threshold is increasing **trusted risk**, not age alone.

## Peer distinction

Young residents may distinguish:

- never gone out;
- short-water experience;
- full-season worker;
- visiting seasonal worker;
- inland youth boarding for work.

These categories can matter more than generic "teenager."

## Gathering spaces

Likely:

- repair yards;
- drying / processing areas;
- seasonal lodging edges;
- shore;
- closed-weather indoor spaces;
- festival / market gatherings.

Bad weather can concentrate youth social life sharply.

## Status competition

- seamanship;
- weather knowledge;
- crew invitation;
- gear skill;
- endurance;
- storytelling;
- willingness to take risk.

## Adult anxieties

Older households may worry that youth:

- take foolish risk to prove themselves;
- leave for NC-3 / Port;
- refuse a declining fishery;
- depend too heavily on convoy / merchant work.

Another adult may worry about the opposite:

> staying in a shrinking fishery.

## Myth use

**Great Mouth** is ideal youth culture material.

Possible adolescent behaviors:

- deliberate exaggeration;
- mock official Port image;
- dare someone to approach unusual fish concentration;
- insist one's island has the "real" version.

Important:

> mocking the story can be a way of participating in it.

## Courtship / social mixing

Seasonal influx expands social contact.

Potential tensions:

- resident family vs recurring seasonal worker;
- someone expected to leave after season;
- marriage / partnership pulling a resident away;
- seasonal visitor becoming permanent community member.

No universal island rule.

## Current-crisis effect

Fish decline turns first work into a question:

> **Is entering the fishery an inheritance or a trap?**

This is one of the strongest generational questions in the North Coast.

# 3. P-1 — Port dock-neighborhood youth / first-work culture

## Why Port is different

Young people in P-1 encounter more simultaneous social worlds than most communities:

- old Port households;
- recent migrants;
- boarders;
- sailors;
- guilds;
- warehouses;
- market workers;
- Three Moons visitors;
- faith institutions.

Their peer culture can therefore change faster than household culture.

## Entry into work

Possible first jobs:

- carrying;
- food stall / provisioning;
- warehouse errands;
- tally assistance;
- rope / rigging support;
- small-craft help;
- cleaning;
- festival temporary work;
- apprentice repair / clerk work.

Some are formal.

Some are not.

## Stable vs precarious youth

### Credential-track youth

May have:

- guild / office apprenticeship;
- predictable training;
- adult sponsor.

### Casual-work youth

May depend on:

- daily hiring;
- family contacts;
- neighborhood reputation;
- temporary festival demand.

### Household-business youth

May work:

- stall;
- boarding house;
- repair shop;
- boat.

These experiences produce different attitudes toward guild centralization.

## Peer spaces

Strong candidates:

- cheap food stalls;
- dock edges outside work hours;
- shared courtyards;
- market / festival setup;
- music / performance spaces;
- public steps / quays;
- neighborhood clubs where supported.

Do not invent a formal youth institution unless needed.

## Language

Port youth are a likely driver of:

- mixed slang;
- borrowed insults;
- shortened guild terms;
- new pronunciation;
- code-switching.

Adults can understand most of it and still complain.

## Clothing / material culture

Likely youth change happens through:

- secondhand clothing;
- workwear modification;
- foreign buttons / cloth;
- guild castoffs;
- cheap festival goods.

This is a better engine than a named "Port fashion."

## Music / leisure

Port's youth culture should probably become one of the fastest routes for:

- traveling games;
- songs;
- dances;
- jokes

to become locally transformed.

## Adult anxieties

Depending on adult:

- dock danger;
- casual labor;
- gambling / drinking;
- foreign influence;
- guild exclusion;
- political organizing;
- young people not learning family language;
- young people learning too much of it from outsiders.

## Myth use

**Estuary Captive** has at least three youth forms:

### child

Puppet / mask / scary harbor story.

### adolescent

Jokes, dares, political graffiti / slang, perhaps costume.

### young worker

Begins noticing why old harbor families tell the story differently.

A youth can first know the carnival figure and only later learn:

> the historical story is ugly.

That is exactly how myth becomes layered.

## Courtship / social mixing

Port creates contact across:

- language;
- faith;
- occupation;
- migration background.

Households may care about:

- religion;
- economic stability;
- family reputation;
- neighborhood;
- citizenship / legal status.

Young people have more opportunities to form relationships that ignore one or more of those categories.

This supports mixed Port households without making Port automatically socially liberal.

## Current-crisis effect

Three Moons gives young workers:

- money;
- temporary independence;
- contact;
- overcrowding;
- risk.

Guild centralization determines who can convert temporary work into a stable career.

This gives the opening festival another ordinary political layer.

# 4. M-1 — repair-network apprentice / first-route cohort

## Entry into work

A child in a repair family may know:

- tool names;
- customer stories;
- route songs

before being trusted with valuable work.

Progression can be:

~~~text
observe / fetch
→ simple repair
→ supervised customer work
→ repair accepted without correction
→ independent stop
→ independent route segment
~~~

Exact sequence varies by craft.

## First-route threshold

One of the strongest status changes:

> **being trusted to complete a stop without the senior worker present.**

That tests:

- craft;
- pricing;
- customer relationship;
- route judgment;
- social conduct.

## Peer culture

Young route workers may compare:

- difficult repair;
- best market;
- worst inn;
- fastest route;
- strange customer;
- song / story learned elsewhere.

Because they travel, they can accumulate status through:

> knowing things from somewhere else.

## Old knowledge vs written credential

Generational conflict:

### older worker

"My customers know my work."

### younger worker

"That does not help at a guild gate where nobody knows us."

Neither is wrong.

Formal recognition may:

- protect mobile work;
- undermine relationship-based authority.

## Myth use

**Road Hound** belongs strongly here.

Young workers may:

- collect variants;
- add verses;
- argue whether two sightings were "the same Hound";
- deliberately tell a frightening version to first-time travelers.

The route network itself becomes folklore infrastructure.

## Courtship

Repeated seasonal stops create a distinctive social possibility:

> a person can know another household for years while only seeing them at certain points in the route cycle.

Potential relationship networks include:

- other mobile households;
- recurring settled customers;
- fair / winter-base communities.

Do not assume marriage inside the network.

## Leaving / settling

A young person may:

- inherit route;
- change craft;
- settle in a customer town;
- take guild work;
- keep network identity while living permanently elsewhere.

This makes cultural membership durable without making it biological.

# 5. M-2 — pastoral youth / responsibility cohort

## Entry into work

Learning begins through:

- observing animals;
- play;
- water / pasture errands;
- supervised care;
- short independent responsibility.

Later:

- route section;
- night watch;
- larger herd group;
- market / bargaining responsibility.

## Knowledge status

Prestige may come from:

- identifying sick animal early;
- predicting weather;
- remembering water;
- handling difficult animal;
- knowing access rule;
- returning with herd intact.

Literacy / arithmetic creates another prestige system.

These can reinforce or compete.

## Education tension

Cold-season bases allow more fixed schooling.

Warm-season movement complicates it.

Possible family strategies:

- children stay at base longer;
- rotating attendance;
- traveling teacher / faith instruction;
- literate family member teaches during movement.

Exact system remains open.

Important:

> formal schooling should not automatically be framed as emancipation from pastoral life.

A young person may want both:

- contracts / literacy;
- route expertise.

## Peer culture

Possible:

- animal contests / skill comparison;
- route songs;
- weather stories;
- market-day dress;
- teasing around first independent responsibility.

Do not invent competitive rituals until scene testing.

## Myth use

**Weather Animal**:

child:
- creature knows the storm.

young herder:
- tests whether sighting matches weather signs.

experienced herder:
- may treat "sighting" as shorthand for several ecological cues.

The supernatural question remains open.

## Courtship

The circuit creates recurring meeting points:

- cold-season bases;
- fairs;
- shared pasture;
- market.

Relationships can connect:

- pastoral households;
- settled farming households;
- carrier networks.

Marriage can therefore alter:

- access;
- residence;
- identity

without being reducible to economic strategy.

## Leave / stay pressure

Young people may see options in:

- animal trade;
- route authority;
- veterinary / healing skill;
- caravan work;
- settled market work.

A family can worry about succession without treating departure as betrayal.

# 6. Cross-community youth transmission

Youth can move culture faster than formal institutions.

## SH-1 → Port / High Roads

Apprentices / workers move:

- technical slang;
- songs;
- labor politics;
- Knocking Below jokes.

## NC-1 → NC-3 → Port

Seasonal workers move:

- fishery slang;
- Great Mouth stories;
- music / games.

## M-1

Repair youth carry:

- songs;
- Road Hound variants;
- jokes;
- fashion fragments

between otherwise weakly connected communities.

## M-2 / M-3

Young route workers carry:

- weather vocabulary;
- market slang;
- games;
- stories

across High Roads systems.

This supports the existing Phase 7 rule:

> transmission is carrier-specific.

# 7. First-work and Wurdren

This pass also gives Wurdren more natural people to meet.

He can encounter:

- apprentice desperate for dangerous higher-paid Stone Hills work;
- island youth deciding whether to stay in fishery;
- Port temporary worker during Three Moons;
- repair-network young adult taking first independent route;
- pastoral young person trying to reconcile route knowledge with contract literacy.

These people should not exist as "lessons for Wurdren."

Their own life question echoes his:

> **What kind of life counts as one worth choosing?**

That can reinforce his complete-person arc without thematic dialogue.

# 8. Current result

The youth / first-work layer now passes at the **mechanism and scene-seed level** for:

- SH-1;
- NC-1;
- P-1;
- M-1;
- M-2.

It is not yet ready for owner promotion as named customs because:

- exact community names;
- local law;
- gender / inheritance;
- scene tests

remain open.

The important structural promotion candidate is narrower:

> **first-work status, peer culture, and generational adaptation are required dimensions of every future community profile.**

Research / profile:

- SH-3 craft town;
