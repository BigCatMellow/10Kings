# Minimal Naming Profiles — V1

## Status

**Provisional naming mechanics — not canon languages or conlangs.**

This page is the next step after:

- [Language and Naming Topology — V1](Language-and-Naming-Topology-Test-V1.md)
- [Naming](../Culture/Naming.md)
- [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](../Language-Families-and-Contact.md)

The purpose is narrow:

> **Create just enough sound and word-building constraint that future place names can be generated consistently from historical causes.**

These profiles do **not** establish:

- language names;
- exact pronunciation;
- complete phoneme inventories;
- grammar;
- scripts;
- family trees;
- universal regional speech.

The W1 / N1 / R1 / P1 / F1 / S1 IDs remain temporary design handles.

---

# 1. General generation rule

For any important name:

~~~
historical cause
→ ordinary local description / lineage / feature
→ local lexical form
→ natural sound change / shortening
→ administrative / Convergence spelling
→ foreign / commercial variants
~~~

Do not reverse this.

A pronounceable invented word is not a valid name merely because it fits the profile.

---

# 2. Common transliteration rule

For design work, V1 uses a plain reader-facing Latin transcription.

Avoid:

- apostrophes as decoration;
- doubled consonants without a historical reason;
- accent marks merely to look foreign;
- phonetic spellings that make prose hard to read.

A local script can later differ.

The common transcription should prioritize:

- stable identity;
- readable distinction;
- legal / merchant practicality.

# 3. W1 — western upland / valley naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels: a, e, i, o, u;
- many sonorants: m, n, l, r;
- ordinary stops: p, b, t, d, k, g;
- common fricatives: s, f, v, h;
- limited use of w / y-like glides;
- syllables commonly CV, CVC, or simple stop+liquid onsets;
- final n, r, l, s, t, and k are acceptable;
- long clusters are uncommon in ordinary modern forms.

## Rhythm

Names tend to remain compact:

- one to three strong syllables;
- longer administrative forms often shorten in speech.

## Place-name formation

Common historical sources should include:

- valley / river;
- ford;
- mine / quarry only when truly foundational;
- family / lineage;
- fort;
- market;
- upper / lower settlement.

## Compound behavior

Older compounds tend to **compress**.

A transparent original phrase can become opaque after:

- loss of an unstressed vowel;
- assimilation at the boundary;
- shortening by merchants / officials.

This is useful because a modern W1 name can be opaque **after** history exists.

## Administrative effect

SH-A standardization may preserve a conservative spelling while workers / local speakers use a shorter form.

## Foreign adaptation

R1 or P1 speakers may:

- insert a vowel into difficult clusters;
- simplify final stops;
- preserve the legal spelling anyway.

# 4. N1 — northern coastal / island naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels with frequent ordinary vowel sequences;
- p, b, t, d, k, g;
- m, n, ng-like nasal;
- l, r;
- s, sh-like sound, h;
- v / w-like sounds;
- syllables commonly CV or CVC;
- final n, r, s, k, and l are common enough;
- large consonant clusters are avoided.

## Place-name formation

Strong naming sources:

- bay / inlet;
- island;
- point / headland;
- sheltered water;
- current;
- anchorage;
- lineage / household;
- winter / seasonal use site where literally relevant.

## Compound behavior

Coastal names often remain transparent longer because navigators need descriptive clarity.

A name may have:

- full local form;
- sailor shortening;
- chart / treaty spelling.

## Island divergence

NC-I may preserve:

- older vowels;
- older stress;
- old substrate names

that mainland N1 speakers no longer understand.

Do not "correct" island names into mainland forms automatically.

## Foreign adaptation

Port / High Roads records may regularize:

- difficult vowel sequences;
- repeated local variants.

Several spellings can remain legitimate.

# 5. R1 — eastern river / lowland naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels;
- m, n;
- l, r;
- p, b, t, d, k, g;
- s, z, sh-like sound, h;
- v and y-like glide;
- syllables favor CV and CVC;
- word-final m, n, l, and r are common;
- unstressed vowels may reduce / disappear in rapid speech without disappearing from legal spelling.

## Place-name formation

Common sources:

- river;
- tributary;
- bank / bend;
- ford / bridge;
- soil / ground condition;
- estate / family;
- market;
- mill;
- upper / lower settlement.

## Compound behavior

R1 names often pass through **administrative reanalysis**.

An old local place name can later be treated as:

- district name;
- estate name;
- market name.

The original meaning may become unclear even when the form remains ordinary.

## LR-A effect

Basin administration may standardize one written form across several spoken variants.

## Foreign adaptation

Port / High Roads merchants may shorten long district forms to the settlement root.

# 6. P1 — plateau / interior High Roads naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels;
- p, b, t, d, k, g;
- m, n;
- r, l;
- s, z, sh / ch-like sounds;
- h;
- y / w-like glides;
- simple two-consonant onsets can occur;
- final clusters remain uncommon.

## Core distinction

P1 is not expected to dominate High Roads naming.

The important P1 feature is **adaptation of borrowed names**.

## Borrowing behavior

P1 speakers may:

- insert vowels into foreign clusters;
- simplify difficult endings;
- preserve route-important source words;
- adopt foreign family / settlement names with local pronunciation.

## Place-name sources

Local P1 names should favor:

- well / spring;
- pasture;
- ridge / shoulder;
- fort;
- gate;
- old toll / watch site;
- family / lineage;
- market.

## High Roads administrative effect

A legal / caravan record may preserve a foreign source spelling rather than the P1 pronunciation.

Therefore:

> written High Roads form ≠ proof of local linguistic origin.

This is central to future naming.

# 7. F1 — forest / river naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels;
- m, n, ng-like nasal;
- p, b, t, d, k, g;
- l, r;
- s, h;
- w / y-like glides;
- syllables favor CV, CVC, and CVN;
- consonant clusters are limited;
- names can remain three or four syllables without feeling formal.

## Place-name formation

Common sources:

- river / stream;
- wet / dry ground;
- grove / managed wood;
- crossing;
- species where locally distinctive and ordinary;
- lineage / community;
- older settlement;
- restricted / ritual site where historically real.

## Important caution

Do not make every name botanical.

A Longwood market town can be named for:

- a person;
- a road;
- a ford;
- an old fort

just as easily as a tree.

## Compound behavior

Older descriptive phrases can be reduced through frequent use.

Outsider translations may remain more transparent than local names.

## Political effect

LW-W / LW-S officials may standardize names more than LW-I communities do.

# 8. S1 — southern urban / irrigation naming profile

## Sound shape

Working tendencies:

- five common vowels;
- p, b, t, d, k, g;
- m, n;
- l, r;
- s, z;
- f, h;
- y-like glide;
- syllables favor CV / CVC;
- medial consonant length may matter in some branches but need not be written consistently in common transcription;
- old city names can remain longer than everyday speech.

## Place-name formation

Common sources:

- spring;
- well;
- canal gate;
- fort;
- market;
- founder / lineage;
- old estate;
- port;
- upper / lower city;
- civic / dynastic inheritance.

## Civic conservatism

Old city names may preserve archaic forms because:

- law;
- monuments;
- civic identity;
- diplomacy

keep them visible.

Spoken forms can shorten substantially.

## Rival exonyms

Neighboring city-states may use:

- older exonym;
- insulting shortening;
- translated form;
- formal treaty form.

Speaker choice can reveal politics.

# 9. Contact-zone naming behavior

## W1 ↔ R1 foothills

Expect:

- translated feature names;
- double names;
- hybrid family / settlement forms;
- old W1 name with R1 administrative suffix or the reverse.

## N1 ↔ P1 North Shoulder

Expect:

- separate local names for the pass;
- one caravan / legal form;
- roadhead names adapted differently on each side.

## W1 ↔ P1 Western Gate

Expect:

- fort / toll names in parallel forms;
- some names stabilized by Convergence records.

## R1 ↔ F1 G2 edge

Expect:

- old hydronyms surviving language shift;
- forest-community names in Low Rivers records.

## F1 ↔ S1 southern edge

Expect:

- market exonyms;
- botanical / agricultural borrowing;
- multiple forms for the same river or road.

---

# 10. Port transcription behavior

Port's common record style should favor:

- short stable spellings;
- few diacritics;
- resistance to changing an established commercial form.

This can fossilize mistakes.

A Port form may become internationally standard even when:

- locals pronounce it differently;
- the source language later changes spelling;
- local nationalists reject it.

That gives Port naming influence without one universal language.

---

# 11. Convergence transcription behavior

Convergence records need:

- repeatable spelling;
- reliable identification;
- cross-copy consistency.

V1 therefore supports a tendency to:

- choose one treaty form;
- list alternate names when ambiguity matters;
- preserve locally supplied spellings for major parties;
- translate ordinary boundary features when practical.

The legal form can outlive the spoken form.

---

# 12. What these profiles intentionally do not provide

No profile yet contains:

- a word for river;
- a word for king;
- a word for harbor;
- a word for city;
- a dictionary of roots.

Those should be generated **only when a real place history calls for them**.

This prevents a root list from becoming another fantasy-name generator.

# 13. Name-generation workflow

For an actual V1 actor:

## Step 1 — determine the naming object

Is the name for:

- city;
- polity;
- dynasty;
- river;
- route;
- island;
- market?

Do not assume one name covers all of them.

## Step 2 — determine the historical source

Why did people first need a name?

## Step 3 — choose the relevant local profile

W1 / N1 / R1 / P1 / F1 / S1 or contact combination.

## Step 4 — create only the lexical roots needed

No unused dictionary.

# 14. Test readiness by actor

## Ready for a naming-history slice now

- SH-M — older settlement + industrial-commercial layers already established.
- NC-R / HR-N — roadhead history is concrete.
- LR-M — transfer-market history is concrete.
- HR-C — function is concrete enough; original settlement cause still needs one local-site decision.
- OC-U / OC-D shared water — history supports old civic names but each city's original site cause still needs specification.

## Not yet ready for a final polity name

- SH-A — capital / ruling-seat / dynasty source remains open.
- NC-M — principal political center / compact naming source remains open.
- LR-A — whether polity name comes from river, seat, or dynasty remains open.
- LW-W / LW-I / LW-S — main political centers / old settlement sources remain underdefined.
- OC-C — original city site history needs development.

This distinction prevents forced naming.

# 15. Strong V1 conclusion

The project now has enough linguistic constraint to stop producing arbitrary fantasy phonetics.

The profiles are deliberately modest.

They make it possible to say:

> this candidate does not fit the naming history

without pretending a finished conlang exists.

The next useful action is to test them on the **three best-developed settlements**:

1. SH-M;
2. NC-R / HR-N paired roadheads;
3. LR-M.

Those places have enough material history to generate names from causes rather than aesthetic preference.
