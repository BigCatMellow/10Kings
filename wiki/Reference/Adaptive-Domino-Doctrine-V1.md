# 1. Core correction

The Villain does **not** possess a forty-step prophecy.

He cannot know that:

~~~text
A
→ B
→ C
→ D
→ war
~~~

will occur exactly as predicted.

Instead he understands:

- the world's existing pressures;
- institutional habits;
- historical fears;
- dependency networks;
- several plausible routes from economic insecurity to security competition.

He watches the system and intervenes selectively.

The design rule is:

> **control direction, not sequence.**

---

# 2. Strategic objective versus operational plan

## Strategic objective

Create a world in which enough major actors believe:

> **we cannot afford to be the only one who does not prepare.**

That produces the security dilemma capable of leading to war.

War then forces Port's neutrality / agency problem into an emergency no outside power can ignore.

## Operational plan

The operational plan is deliberately incomplete.

It consists of:

- pressures to watch;
- thresholds worth exploiting;
- capabilities kept available;
- relationships that provide access;
- several plausible paths toward the same strategic conditions.

It does **not** specify every future event.

# 3. Pressure states

The Villain is trying to increase several broad conditions.

## P1 — commercial insecurity

Actors become less confident that ordinary trade will remain available.

Indicators:

- credit tightening;
- canceled orders;
- reserve purchases;
- longer contract horizons;
- more political-risk clauses.

## P2 — dependency awareness

Actors become more conscious that specific routes, goods, or institutions can be withheld.

Indicators:

- strategic inventories;
- route diversification;
- emergency contracts;
- political arguments over access.

## P3 — attribution / mistrust

Actors increasingly explain ordinary disruption through rival intent.

Indicators:

- accusations of manipulation;
- political blame;
- intelligence gathering;
- reluctance to share information.

## P4 — bloc dependence

Actors increasingly rely on aligned partners for:

- supply;
- credit;
- route access;
- diplomatic backing.

Indicators:

- guarantees;
- preferred counterparties;
- coordinated purchasing;
- mutual exemptions.

## P5 — security conversion

Commercial / administrative precautions become physically useful for war.

Indicators:

- armed escorts;
- strategic export rules;
- guarded depots;
- repaired posts;
- reserve registration;
- military-use inventories.

## P6 — readiness asymmetry fear

Actors believe a rival may be able to move faster than they can.

Indicators:

- partial mobilization;
- forces moved toward chokepoints;
- emergency command discretion;
- shortened diplomatic deadlines.

## P7 — Port emergency dependence

War / near-war makes everyone need Port while Port's neutrality constrains action.

Indicators:

- military cargo disputes;
- protection proposals;
- emergency financing;
- armed-access arguments;
- pressure for exemptions;
- competing offers of "help."

The Villain does not need every pressure state to reach maximum intensity.

He needs enough of them to reinforce each other.

# 4. Information architecture

The adaptive plan requires broad awareness but not omniscience.

The Villain's strongest structural advantage is that Port causes information to come **to him**.

Two current developments amplify that advantage:

## Guild centralization

His Port-strengthening work has encouraged more guild branches / representatives to maintain a presence in the city and communicate across professions.

This creates legitimate information about:

- supply;
- labor;
- contracts;
- routes;
- standards;
- demand.

## Three Moons Festival

Once every seven years, [Three Moons Festival](../Culture/Three-Moons-Festival.md) brings an exceptional concentration of trans-regional networks into Port.

This creates a temporary high-information period.

The doctrine rule is:

> **Use the festival to update the model, not to execute a prewritten checklist.**

Information density should lead to:

- revised assumptions;
- discarded branches;
- newly noticed opportunities;
- contradictory signals.

If the festival merely tells him exactly what he expected, it is being used too conveniently.

# 5. Intervention rule

Before any deliberate action, ask:

1. Is this pressure already moving without him?
2. What outcome range is plausible?
3. Does he need to intervene now?
4. What is the smallest push that changes probabilities rather than dictates outcomes?
5. If it fails, is the loss survivable?
6. Can he learn from the failure without exposing himself?
7. Does another route already lead toward the same strategic condition?
8. Would doing nothing be better?

**Doing nothing is an active option.**

A mastermind who intervenes in every event creates evidence and makes unnecessary mistakes.

---

# 6. Three-horizon prediction limit

He should reason at different confidence horizons.

## High confidence — next move

He may know with reasonable confidence:

> if these lenders withdraw these marginal renewals, these merchants will need another source of credit.

## Medium confidence — next few consequences

He may estimate:

> tighter merchant credit probably worsens Low Rivers purchasing and increases pressure for emergency policy.

## Low confidence — distant outcome

He cannot know:

> therefore LR-A will pass exactly this rule, which will make OC-C do exactly that, which will eventually start war at this specific checkpoint.

Long-range planning should use **conditions**, not event predictions.

---

# 7. Branching rule

Every important pressure node should have:

- expected branch;
- benign branch;
- adverse branch;
- useful surprise branch.

Example:

~~~text
Villain biases route confidence
        |
        +→ EXPECTED: merchants reroute through corridor A
        |
        +→ BENIGN: authorities correct the information quickly
        |
        +→ ADVERSE: forgery suspicion creates tighter verification but not rerouting
        |
        +→ USEFUL SURPRISE: merchants choose corridor C instead,
                           creating a different bottleneck
~~~

The Villain's skill is recognizing **which new board he has**, not pretending every board was planned.

---

# 8. Failure is required

A believable plan should contain:

- failed nudges;
- interventions that have no meaningful effect;
- outcomes opposite what he expected;
- opportunities he notices too late;
- branches the Council successfully contains;
- reforms that genuinely reduce pressure;
- allies who refuse escalation;
- local actors who solve their own problems.

Some failures should cost him:

- money;
- access;
- a useful intermediary;
- time;
- credibility;
- a preferred route.

He should sometimes have to abandon a branch completely.

---

# 9. Redundancy without omniscience

The plan survives failure because several independent pressures can feed the same strategic condition.

Example:

~~~text
commercial insecurity can rise through:

piracy
OR route damage
OR credit tightening
OR food-security contracting
OR labor disruption
OR water anxiety
~~~

He does not need all six.

Similarly:

~~~text
security conversion can rise through:

convoys
OR route escorts
OR strategic export controls
OR depot protection
OR reserve registration
OR competing guarantees
~~~

The plan is robust because the **goal state has multiple entrances**.

That is different from predicting every domino.

# 10. Secrecy doctrine

A true conspiracy must minimize the number of people who know the real objective.

## Strategic knowledge

Ideally:

- the Villain;
- perhaps one or two trusted strategic confidants at most.

They know war is being cultivated to force Port reform.

## Operational knowledge

A coordinator may know:

- several related commercial or political tasks;
- the movement is trying to increase leverage;
- not the complete war architecture.

## Specialist knowledge

A specialist knows:

- move this cargo;
- acquire this document;
- buy this debt;
- collect this route report;
- introduce these two people.

They need not know why.

## Allies

Allies may sincerely support:

- Port reform;
- labor rights;
- producer relief;
- local autonomy;
- anti-Council transparency.

They are not conspirators merely because the Villain helps them.

## Ordinary actors

The overwhelming majority of consequential decisions are made by people who do not know the Villain at all.

This is essential.

---

# 11. Indirection rule

The Villain should rarely issue an instruction that can later be translated into:

> start the next domino.

Prefer actions that have a legitimate immediate explanation.

Examples:

- purchase information;
- invest in warehouse capacity;
- finance a distressed buyer;
- pay for legal representation;
- introduce a merchant to a carrier;
- ask a broker to find current risk reports.

The strategic effect comes from **when and where** he uses the capability.

---

# 12. Evidence rule

He cannot be perfectly untraceable.

Perfect invisibility would also feel artificial.

Instead:

- individual acts are explainable;
- some intermediaries can be linked to him;
- no single link proves the war plan;
- repeated patterns become meaningful only retrospectively.

The investigative problem should be:

> **we can prove he touched some of these events; we cannot prove he caused most of what followed.**

That ambiguity should survive even after the conspiracy becomes known.

---

# 13. Adaptation cycle

The Villain's actual recurring process is:

~~~text
OBSERVE
what changed?

→ MODEL
what are the likely local responses?

→ WAIT
will the world move usefully on its own?

→ NUDGE
only if leverage is worth exposure / cost

→ MEASURE
what actually happened?

→ ABANDON / REINFORCE / REDIRECT
choose based on the new state

→ OBSERVE AGAIN
~~~

This loop is the plan.

---

# 14. Mastermind standard

His brilliance should come from:

- systemic understanding;
- patience;
- information quality;
- willingness to abandon sunk-cost branches;
- maintaining several options;
- recognizing second-order opportunities quickly;
- knowing when **not** to interfere.

It should not come from:

- omniscience;
- perfect behavioral prediction;
- every accident secretly being his work;
- every failure secretly being part of the plan.

The test is:

> **When something unexpected happens, does he become more interesting because of how he adapts?**

If yes, the mastermind feels intelligent rather than magical.
