# Middle Worldbuilding

## Status

**Worldbuilding method — not setting canon.**

Source inspiration: [DragonSword — Middle Worldbuilding](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/DragonSword/blob/main/worldbuilding/MIDDLE-WORLDBUILDING.md).

The DragonSword note supplies the core distinction:

| Scale | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| **Macro** | Why is the world this way? |
| **Middle** | How does the world actually function because it is this way? |
| **Micro** | What is this particular person, place, object, meal, building, law, custom, or event like? |

Everything below that develops tests, workflows, domains, and examples is the **10Kings project expansion** of that core idea.

## Active redesign note

The setting is currently undergoing active redesign.

Names of regions, how regions function, foods, cultural identities, institutions, aesthetics, internal politics, and other specific setting details may change while this work is in progress.

That means:

- current detail should be treated as **working material**, not immovable canon;
- structural relationships can remain useful even when labels or cultural packages change;
- a later, better-supported design may replace an older working-canon detail;
- agents should not preserve a weak detail merely because it already appears in several wiki pages;
- when a redesign changes an owner, dependent pages should be reconciled rather than allowed to drift.

The goal right now is to understand and build the world, not to freeze it prematurely.

# 1. What Middle Worldbuilding Actually Is

Middle worldbuilding is the layer between **premise** and **flavor**.

It asks how a condition of the world becomes:

- a recurring problem or opportunity;
- a pattern of behavior;
- a need for coordination;
- an institution, custom, market, profession, route, law, or infrastructure;
- an ordinary expectation people organize their lives around.

It is where:

> **causes become systems, and systems become daily life.**

A useful chain is:

```text
world condition
→ pressure or opportunity
→ repeated behavior
→ coordination problem
→ institution / infrastructure / custom
→ ordinary daily function
→ concrete detail
```

Example:

```text
a region has unreliable winter passes
→ people cannot count on year-round movement
→ households and merchants stockpile before closure
→ communities need rules for reserves, prices, and emergency access
→ storage institutions, winter contracts, specialized couriers, and local obligations develop
→ seasonal planning shapes work, travel, debt, and hospitality
→ cellar design, preservation techniques, pack-animal markets,
  winter pricing, route offices, and local arguments become ordinary
```

The cellar, courier, contract, and preserved food are **micro details**.

What makes them convincing is the **middle layer that caused them**.

---

# 2. Why the Middle Layer Matters

A setting can have excellent macro ideas and excellent individual details while still feeling artificial.

For example:

### Macro

> This is a region of cold seas and dangerous winters.

### Micro

> People eat smoked fish, wear heavy wool, and carve wave patterns into doors.

Those details may fit aesthetically, but the connection is shallow unless the world also answers questions like:

- Who decides when ships stop sailing for the season?
- How are winter shortages anticipated?
- Who maintains rescue boats?
- What happens to debts if a ship is overdue for three months?
- Which settlements are rich because they remain ice-free?
- Who owns preservation facilities?
- What jobs only exist because seasonal risk exists?
- What laws govern salvage?
- How do inland households obtain coastal goods?
- What happens when one winter is unexpectedly mild?

Those answers create the **functional world** from which food, clothes, architecture, law, class, language, and custom can emerge naturally.

Middle worldbuilding therefore prevents the setting from becoming a collection of themed decorations.

---

# 3. The Middle Is Usually About Relationships

Macro worldbuilding often names **things**:

- mountains;
- kingdoms;
- religions;
- resources;
- climates;
- wars;
- magical rules.

Micro worldbuilding often names **instances**:

- a town;
- a soup;
- a shrine;
- a bridge;
- a tool;
- a person;
- a festival.

Middle worldbuilding usually names **relationships and systems**:

- who depends on whom;
- who regulates what;
- how a shortage travels;
- how people move;
- where jurisdiction overlaps;
- who stores surplus;
- who carries news;
- who maintains infrastructure;
- who benefits from a custom;
- what happens when a system fails.

A good middle-worldbuilding statement often contains a verb.

Compare:

> **Macro:** The valley is fertile.

> **Middle:** The valley produces enough surplus that storage, transport, land credit, and seasonal labor become major political concerns.

> **Micro:** Granaries are built on raised stone foundations and audited before harvest season.

The middle statement is the bridge.

# 4. A Better Causal Model

Not every world condition produces one obvious adaptation.

A stronger model is:

```text
CONDITION
↓
PRESSURES + OPPORTUNITIES
↓
MULTIPLE POSSIBLE RESPONSES
↓
WINNERS / LOSERS / TRADEOFFS
↓
REPEATED BEHAVIOR
↓
COORDINATION
↓
INSTITUTION / CUSTOM / INFRASTRUCTURE
↓
FEEDBACK INTO THE ORIGINAL CONDITION
```

This matters because cultures do not simply "obey geography."

Two communities facing the same environment may respond differently because they have different:

- histories;
- political structures;
- religions;
- technologies;
- neighbors;
- property systems;
- class relations;
- prior disasters;
- available trade routes.

That follows [World Rule 2](../World-Rules.md): geography creates pressure, but history decides how people respond.

## Example: same drought, different response

Two dry regions might both face irregular rainfall.

One develops:

- village-owned cisterns;
- communal rationing;
- strong local water councils.

Another develops:

- privately owned wells;
- water debt;
- powerful landholding families;
- hired guards protecting irrigation works.

The climate is similar.

The **middle layer** is different.

That difference is where culture becomes specific.

# 5. The Middle-Worldbuilding Pass

Use this as the standard working procedure.

## Step 1 — State the world condition plainly

Avoid aesthetic language.

Weak:

> A proud mountain culture values endurance.

Better:

> Most settlements are separated by steep terrain, several passes close seasonally, and pack transport is more reliable than carts.

The second version gives the world something to react to.

## Step 2 — Identify pressures and opportunities

Ask:

- What becomes difficult?
- What becomes valuable?
- What becomes dangerous?
- What becomes unreliable?
- What becomes easier here than elsewhere?
- What can people exploit?

Do not assume every effect is negative.

A dangerous mountain pass also creates opportunities for:

- guides;
- inns;
- toll collectors;
- pack breeders;
- smugglers;
- repair workers;
- fortifications;
- pilgrimage sites.

## Step 3 — Identify affected groups

At minimum consider:

- households;
- workers;
- merchants;
- landowners;
- local authorities;
- religious institutions;
- guilds/professional groups;
- migrants/travelers;
- criminals;
- neighboring communities.

Different groups should often want different solutions.

## Step 4 — Generate several plausible adaptations

Do not jump immediately to the coolest answer.

Ask what people could realistically do.

For a flood-prone river:

- build levees;
- move settlements;
- build on raised ground;
- accept seasonal flooding;
- use floodplain farming;
- regulate construction;
- create ferry networks;
- insure cargo;
- maintain emergency granaries.

Different communities may choose different combinations.

## Step 5 — Ask what must be coordinated

This is one of the most important middle-worldbuilding questions.

Whenever many people depend on the same solution, ask:

> **Who organizes this?**

Examples:

- Who maintains the bridge?
- Who opens the irrigation gates?
- Who keeps the reserve grain?
- Who verifies weights?
- Who decides a road is unsafe?
- Who pays the lighthouse keeper?
- Who records land boundaries?
- Who trains the midwives?
- Who certifies guides?
- Who buries strangers?

If the answer is "everyone just does it," the middle layer is probably incomplete.

## Step 6 — Create institutions, customs, or infrastructure only where needed

Possible answers include:

- local councils;
- guilds;
- kin networks;
- temple institutions;
- private businesses;
- customary law;
- rotating obligations;
- state offices;
- neighborhood associations;
- informal specialists;
- criminal organizations.

Do not invent a formal bureaucracy for every problem.

Some coordination can be customary, familial, contractual, religious, or improvised.

## Step 7 — Ask who gains and who pays

Every durable system creates distributional effects.

Ask:

- Who profits?
- Who is excluded?
- Who bears maintenance costs?
- Who gets first access?
- Who is legally protected?
- Who can evade the rule?
- Who resents the arrangement?

This creates class, politics, corruption, reform movements, and internal disagreement without adding arbitrary conflict.

## Step 8 — Derive ordinary life

Now move into micro detail.

Ask what the system changes about:

- meals;
- work schedules;
- housing;
- tools;
- clothes;
- childhood;
- marriage;
- inheritance;
- travel;
- debt;
- festivals;
- slang;
- etiquette;
- neighborhood layout;
- public buildings.

This is where detail should become abundant.

## Step 9 — Connect outward

Ask what the adaptation does to neighboring systems.

A storage law may affect:

- trade;
- lending;
- migration;
- crime;
- class politics;
- religious charity;
- foreign merchants.

Middle worldbuilding should produce **connections**, not isolated dossiers.

## Step 10 — Stress the system

Ask:

> What happens when the assumption stops being true?

Examples:

- an unusually warm winter;
- a new road;
- a failed harvest;
- a new competitor;
- a war;
- a cheaper preservation method;
- a river changing course;
- a ruler ignoring customary rights.

A system that only works under perfect conditions is not finished.

---

# 6. The Middle Test

A detail is probably genuinely "middle" if it answers several of these:

- Does it affect more than one household or individual?
- Does it recur?
- Does somebody have to coordinate it?
- Does it create a profession, institution, market, custom, or infrastructure?
- Does it create winners and losers?
- Does it change how people plan?
- Does it alter another system?
- Would people notice if it disappeared?
- Can different communities solve the same problem differently?
- Can it fail?

If the answer is mostly **no**, it may be micro detail.

If the answer is so broad that it only describes climate, ancient history, or world cosmology, it may still be macro.

# 7. Major Middle-Worldbuilding Domains

These are not separate silos. They should collide.

## Movement

Ask:

- How do people travel?
- What routes are seasonal?
- What can carts use versus pack animals?
- Where do travelers sleep?
- Who repairs roads?
- Who controls ferries?
- What documents are needed?
- What makes one route safer but more expensive?

Possible outputs:

- inns;
- road law;
- guide professions;
- toll systems;
- caravan seasons;
- pilgrimage routes;
- smuggling paths;
- transport insurance;
- roadside shrines.

## Food

Use:

```text
ecology
→ production
→ seasonality
→ preservation
→ storage
→ transport
→ class access
→ cooking constraints
→ habits
→ cuisine
```

Ask:

- What fills stomachs cheaply?
- What spoils quickly?
- What travels well?
- What requires a lot of fuel?
- What becomes famine food?
- Who has ovens?
- Who eats imported food?
- Who controls mills or presses?
- What is eaten at work?

Food is not just ingredients. It is an infrastructure problem.

## Work

Ask:

- What work is seasonal?
- What work is dangerous?
- What requires apprenticeship?
- Who owns tools?
- Who hires temporary labor?
- How are injuries handled?
- What work creates migration?
- What work is prestigious but poorly paid?
- What work is necessary but socially despised?

This produces:

- labor customs;
- guilds;
- hiring places;
- worker housing;
- debt;
- family strategies;
- class identities.

## Property and land

Ask:

- Who owns land?
- Who owns water?
- Who owns trees, mines, fish, grazing, roads, ruins?
- Can rights overlap?
- Are rights inherited?
- Can outsiders buy?
- What does "ownership" actually permit?

This can matter more than naming the ruler.

## Law and jurisdiction

Ask:

- Who can punish whom?
- Which disputes go to local custom?
- Which go to a ruler?
- Which go to guild or religious arbitration?
- What happens across borders?
- What counts as evidence?
- Who can appeal?

The interesting part is often the **overlap**.

## Information

Ask:

- Who knows prices first?
- Who carries letters?
- Who keeps records?
- Who can read?
- Which rumors travel fastest?
- Which institutions are trusted?
- How long does news take?
- What information is deliberately delayed?

Information should have geography.

## Security

Ask:

- Who protects roads?
- Who protects villages?
- Who responds to fire, banditry, piracy, or riot?
- Who can legally carry weapons?
- Who funds patrols?
- Where does authority become thin?

Security is not just armies.

## Religion

Do not stop at doctrine.

Ask what institutions actually do:

- burial;
- marriage;
- recordkeeping;
- charity;
- mediation;
- schooling;
- calendars;
- hospitality;
- medical care;
- sanctuary;
- inheritance;
- pilgrimage.

Then ask who depends on those services even when they do not share the belief.

## Family and kinship

Ask:

- Who is expected to care for elders?
- Who inherits?
- Where do newly married couples live?
- Who raises orphaned children?
- How important are godparents, clans, household servants, apprentices, or fictive kin?
- What happens when economic migration separates families?

Family structure is part of economics and politics.

## Health, injury, and death

Ask:

- Who treats injuries?
- What treatments cost money?
- Who supports an injured worker?
- What happens to contagious people?
- Who handles corpses?
- What professions carry unusual risk?
- What deaths create legal or religious complications?

A dangerous world should have institutions for surviving danger.

## Time and calendars

Ask:

- What determines the work year?
- Which dates matter economically?
- When are debts due?
- When do routes close?
- When do contracts begin?
- Which festivals coincide with labor cycles?

Calendars are infrastructure for coordination.

## Architecture and settlement

Ask what buildings must **do**.

- store food;
- withstand weather;
- separate animals and people;
- ventilate smoke;
- defend streets;
- collect water;
- host extended families;
- accommodate workshops;
- handle seasonal markets.

Architecture follows repeated needs before it becomes style.

## Magic

If magic remains in the setting, ask:

- Who trains users?
- Who hires them?
- Who regulates them?
- What professions changed?
- What mundane system still exists because magic is unreliable or expensive?
- What new risks require new institutions?

If magic has no middle layer, it is probably functioning as decoration.

# 8. Cross-System Worldbuilding

The richest material often appears where two systems collide.

Examples:

### Food × religion

- fasting rules alter seasonal demand;
- temple kitchens become relief infrastructure;
- pilgrimage creates travel foods;
- religious slaughter rules create specialist professions.

### Geography × law

- a river moves, but the legal border does not;
- grazing rights cross a political frontier;
- an old mountain route has customary immunity from tolls.

### Trade × family

- merchant marriages connect distant ports;
- children are fostered with commercial partners;
- inheritance law affects company continuity.

### Work × architecture

- mining creates washing yards and worker dormitories;
- textile production changes window size and light requirements;
- shipbuilding creates long waterfront plots.

### Information × politics

- rulers receive official reports slowly;
- merchants know market changes first;
- pilgrims carry rumor faster than state messengers.

### Preservation × class

- poor households salt or ferment for necessity;
- elites later adopt the same foods as nostalgic or prestigious cuisine.

The goal is not to force every domain to connect to every other domain.

The goal is to ask enough cross-system questions that the world stops behaving like separate folders.

---

# 9. Build Outward, Not Downward

Middle worldbuilding is not a command to completely finish one region before touching another.

Prefer **cross-system passes**.

Example:

1. define winter transport constraints across the world;
2. see how different communities adapt;
3. compare who maintains routes;
4. derive different storage, work, law, food, and travel patterns;
5. identify cross-border dependencies;
6. then deepen individual places.

This prevents regions from becoming self-contained themed boxes.

A useful sequence is:

```text
WORLD PRESSURE
→ compare regional responses
→ compare class/local responses
→ identify cross-border effects
→ derive concrete detail
```

---

# 10. Internal Variation Is Required

A middle-worldbuilding conclusion should rarely apply uniformly to an entire region.

Always test:

- urban / rural;
- rich / poor;
- coast / inland;
- center / border;
- old / young;
- local / migrant;
- guild member / outsider;
- religious / secular;
- settled / itinerant;
- elite version / everyday version.

Example:

> "This culture values hospitality."

That is too broad.

Better:

- mountain households may treat emergency lodging as a serious obligation;
- wealthy inns commercialize the same expectation;
- border towns resent travelers during shortage years;
- younger city residents may treat the old obligation as inconvenient;
- religious hospices may insist hospitality is sacred;
- smugglers exploit the custom to avoid questions.

Now the value behaves like culture rather than a personality trait assigned to a region.

---

# 11. Middle Worldbuilding Needs Friction

A system should not be perfectly optimized.

Realistic middle layers contain:

- obsolete rules;
- overlapping jurisdictions;
- local exceptions;
- bottlenecks;
- vested interests;
- corruption;
- informal workarounds;
- unequal access;
- redundant institutions;
- institutions solving yesterday's problem;
- reforms that create new problems.

Useful question:

> **What is annoying about this system even when it is working normally?**

That often reveals more than asking only how it fails catastrophically.

---

# 12. Every Solution Creates a New Worldbuilding Question

Middle worldbuilding should branch.

Example:

```text
winter isolation
→ communal grain reserves
```

Now ask:

- Who owns the grain?
- Who audits it?
- Can reserves be sold?
- What happens if one village contributed less?
- Can travelers receive it?
- Does a ruler have emergency seizure rights?
- Who guards it?
- Is old grain rotated?
- Do merchants hate the system?
- Does charity duplicate it?
- Can a corrupt official profit?

The purpose is not infinite detail.

The purpose is to notice where one answer creates another **systemic question**.

Stop when additional answers no longer materially change how the world functions.

# 13. Common Failure Modes

## Theme-first culture

> "This is the practical mining region, so everything is sturdy and industrial."

Problem: aesthetic repetition replaces causal worldbuilding.

Fix: derive institutions and habits from actual mining, labor, transport, fuel, injury, trade, and ownership systems.

## One-resource monoculture

> "They export timber, so the whole region revolves around timber."

Problem: a famous export becomes the entire society.

Fix: ask what most people actually eat, do, buy, repair, inherit, and worry about.

## Premise → costume

> cold climate → fur clothes  
> forest → green buildings  
> desert → spicy food

Problem: skips the middle.

Fix: derive material availability, fuel, work, preservation, trade, class, and institutions first.

## Perfect adaptation

> Everyone developed the ideal response to the environment.

Problem: removes history and politics.

Fix: include competing solutions, failed systems, inherited inefficiencies, and uneven access.

## Institution from nowhere

> There is a powerful Water Guild.

Problem: organization exists because it sounds useful.

Fix: specify what repeated coordination problem caused people to accept or require the guild.

## Culture as personality

> The people are stoic / joyful / suspicious / scholarly.

Problem: millions of people become one character.

Fix: identify the institutions and experiences that make certain behaviors common in certain contexts, then preserve disagreement.

## Detail with no owner

> A town has a complicated bridge-toll ritual.

Problem: interesting but disconnected.

Fix: link it to road maintenance, jurisdiction, trade, history, or religion—or leave it as minor flavor if it does not need systemic weight.

## Every detail becomes plot

Problem: the world feels constructed entirely for the protagonist.

Fix: much middle-worldbuilding should matter to ordinary people without ever becoming a story event.

# 14. Worked Example — Neutral Commercial Harbor

**This example is method demonstration, not new Port canon.**

### Macro condition

A strategically placed harbor is politically neutral and used by merchants from several powers.

### Pressures

- merchants need predictable law;
- rulers fear rivals controlling the harbor;
- foreign communities need protection;
- ships need repair and storage;
- currencies and measures differ;
- criminals can exploit jurisdictional gaps.

### Repeated behavior

- merchants hire local brokers;
- foreigners cluster near warehouses;
- rulers maintain envoys;
- disputes repeatedly cross legal systems;
- ships wait for cargo consolidation.

### Coordination problems

- contract enforcement;
- harbor scheduling;
- quarantine;
- fire control;
- warehouse ownership;
- weights and measures;
- currency exchange;
- policing foreign sailors.

### Middle layer

Possible institutions:

- neutral commercial courts;
- harbor masters;
- warehouse registries;
- licensed interpreters;
- exchange brokers;
- foreign enclaves;
- night watch;
- fire brigades;
- pilot guilds;
- bonded storage.

### Ordinary life

Now micro detail becomes grounded:

- neighborhoods speak mixed dialects;
- taverns post prices in several currencies;
- clerks specialize in foreign contracts;
- warehouse fires are a public obsession;
- dockworkers know phrases from six languages;
- children grow up eating mixed cuisines;
- local insults distinguish newcomers from Port-born families.

The middle layer explains why cosmopolitan culture exists.

# 15. Worked Example — Agricultural Surplus

**Method demonstration, not new regional canon.**

### Macro condition

A fertile area produces a major grain surplus.

### Shallow answer

> It is the breadbasket.

### Middle pass

Surplus creates:

- seasonal labor demand;
- storage needs;
- transport pressure;
- price volatility;
- lending against future harvests;
- land investment;
- milling bottlenecks;
- taxation opportunities.

That creates competing institutions:

- village granaries;
- private warehouses;
- merchant lenders;
- mills;
- harvest labor markets;
- land courts;
- road/river infrastructure.

### Unexpected consequence

A bumper harvest can create a crisis.

```text
excellent harvest
→ storage fills
→ prices fall
→ indebted farmers cannot cover loans
→ buyers delay purchases
→ land changes hands
→ labor unrest rises
```

The middle layer turns "abundance" from a static blessing into an economic system.

# 16. Worked Example — Forest Stewardship

**Method demonstration, not new Deepwood canon.**

### Macro condition

A large forest supplies timber, fuel, food, medicine, grazing, and hunting.

### Pressure

Different users need the same landscape for different purposes.

### Stakeholders

- village households;
- charcoal burners;
- builders;
- hunters;
- herders;
- healers;
- merchants;
- rulers;
- religious communities.

### Coordination problems

- cutting rights;
- seasonal access;
- replanting or rotation;
- fire risk;
- hunting rights;
- road building;
- sacred sites;
- outsider access.

### Middle possibilities

One area may use:

- village commons;
- licensed cutting;
- hereditary woodland rights;
- religious stewardship;
- crown forests;
- merchant leases.

These systems can coexist and conflict.

### Micro output

Only now derive:

- boundary markers;
- specialized axes;
- seasonal forest camps;
- charcoal settlements;
- wood-law sayings;
- ritual opening days;
- roadside timber yards;
- resentment toward surveyors;
- mixed border cuisine.

# 17. Relationship to World Rules

Middle Worldbuilding is one way to **apply** the [World Rules](../World-Rules.md).

Especially:

- Rule 1 — regions are ecosystems, not gimmicks;
- Rule 2 — geography creates pressure without dictating culture;
- Rule 4 — history is messy;
- Rule 5 — material limits matter;
- Rule 8 — institutions have constituencies;
- Rule 12 — events have second- and third-order effects;
- Rule 13 — culture is contested and changing;
- Rule 16 — food follows ecology, trade, class, season, and preservation;
- Rule 17 — architecture is accumulated history;
- Rule 20 — the world exists without the protagonists.

The rules state the constraints.

Middle Worldbuilding is the **working method for producing detail that obeys them**.

# 18. Relationship to MAPS_L

## Spiderweb

Middle worldbuilding should improve recoverability.

A detail should be traceable backward:

```text
detail
→ routine
→ institution / custom / infrastructure
→ coordination problem
→ pressure
→ world condition
```

And forward:

```text
world condition
→ pressure
→ adaptation
→ institution
→ dependency
→ downstream consequence
```

That makes Spiderweb useful for worldbuilding rather than merely documentation.

## E/I

Middle-worldbuilding passes create strong E/I opportunities.

When separate systems are compared, we may discover:

- an institution should exist that nobody explicitly invented;
- two regional practices probably solve the same problem differently;
- a trade dependency should create a political faction;
- a religious institution should affect inheritance;
- food preservation should change architecture;
- route law should create a specialist profession.

These are **candidates**, not automatic canon.

Capture them through E/I and promote only after they survive a concrete test.

# 19. Relationship to Writing the Sequel

[Writing the Sequel](Writing-the-Sequel.md) is related but separate.

Middle Worldbuilding asks:

> **How does this condition change the way the world functions?**

Writing the Sequel asks:

> **What accumulated after people lived with that reality for years or generations?**

Example:

### Middle

Dangerous winter pass  
→ seasonal closure rules  
→ reserve storage  
→ winter couriers  
→ travel contracts.

### Writing the Sequel

Those systems persist for generations  
→ old courier families gain status  
→ an emergency granary law becomes inherited property law  
→ a winter rescue custom becomes a civic ritual  
→ younger residents forget the original disaster that created it.

Middle builds the functioning system.

Writing the Sequel adds historical accumulation.

---

# 20. Relationship to Worldbuilding Breath

[Worldbuilding Breath](Worldbuilding-Breath.md) is a third concern.

- **Middle Worldbuilding** builds functional systems.
- **Writing the Sequel** builds accumulated aftermath through time.
- **Worldbuilding Breath** decides how little of either layer needs to be explicitly explained in prose.

A world may contain fifty pages of middle-worldbuilding so that a scene only needs one sentence about a delayed ferry, an old grain token, or a clerk arguing about jurisdiction.

Build deeply.

Reveal selectively.

# 21. Middle-Worldbuilding Quality Tests

Before promoting a major worldbuilding idea, run these.

## Causality test

Can we explain why the system exists without saying:

> "because this culture is like that"?

## Cost test

What does the system cost in:

- labor;
- time;
- money;
- materials;
- political capital;
- inconvenience?

## Constituency test

Who supports it?

Who dislikes it?

Who depends on it anyway?

## Variation test

How does it differ by:

- class;
- locality;
- profession;
- generation;
- border position?

## Failure test

What happens when it breaks?

## Bypass test

Can people avoid it?

If so, at what cost?

## Neighbor test

How does it affect another place or system?

## Ordinary-life test

Can we name at least three mundane consequences?

## Removal test

If this institution/custom/infrastructure vanished tomorrow, what would stop working?

If the answer is "nothing," it may be decorative rather than systemic.

# 22. Current Priority for 10Kings

While region identities are being redesigned, prioritize:

1. **causal structure** over names;
2. **ecology and geography** over aesthetic theme;
3. **systems and adaptations** over isolated facts;
4. **multiple plausible responses** over one inevitable cultural response;
5. **internal variation** over regional stereotypes;
6. **tradeoffs and constituencies** over perfectly optimized societies;
7. **cross-regional consequences** over self-contained region dossiers;
8. **ordinary coordination** over only kings, wars, and grand institutions;
9. **specific detail derived from causes** rather than arbitrary flavor.

This does **not** mean withholding detail.

It means creating more detail with stronger reasons underneath it.

# 23. Standard Working Questions

For any major premise, ask:

1. What is the actual material or social condition?
2. What pressure or opportunity does it create?
3. Who experiences that pressure most strongly?
4. What are three plausible responses?
5. Why did one response become common here instead of another?
6. What must people coordinate?
7. Who coordinates it?
8. Who pays?
9. Who benefits?
10. Who is excluded?
11. What profession, institution, custom, market, or infrastructure results?
12. What ordinary behavior changes?
13. What does the rich version look like?
14. What does the poor version look like?
15. What does the border version look like?
16. What does another region do differently?
17. What secondary problem does the solution create?
