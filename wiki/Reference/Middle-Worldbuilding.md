# Middle Worldbuilding

## Status

**Worldbuilding method — not setting canon.**

Source inspiration: [DragonSword — Middle Worldbuilding](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/DragonSword/blob/main/worldbuilding/MIDDLE-WORLDBUILDING.md).

## Active redesign note

The setting is currently undergoing active redesign.

Names of regions, how regions function, foods, cultural identities, institutions, aesthetics, internal politics, and other specific setting details may change while this work is in progress.

That means:

- current detail should be treated as **working material**, not immovable canon;
- structural relationships can remain useful even when labels or cultural packages change;
- a later, better-supported design may replace an older working-canon detail;
- agents should not preserve a weak detail merely because it already appears in several wiki pages;
- when a redesign changes an owner, dependent pages should be reconciled rather than allowed to drift.

The goal right now is to understand and build the world, not to freeze it prematurely.

## Three useful scales

| Scale | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| **Macro** | Why is the world this way? |
| **Middle** | How does the world actually function because it is this way? |
| **Micro** | What is this particular place, practice, object, meal, person, building, law, or custom like? |

10Kings currently has a great deal of macro material and a growing amount of micro material.

The important development layer is the **middle**.

Middle worldbuilding is where causes become systems and systems become ordinary life.

## Core method

A useful causal chain is:

```text
world condition
→ pressure or opportunity
→ repeated behavior
→ coordination problem
→ institution / infrastructure / custom
→ ordinary daily function
→ concrete detail
```

Example:

```text
a region has unreliable winter passes
→ people cannot count on year-round movement
→ households and merchants stockpile before closure
→ communities need rules for reserves, prices, and emergency access
→ storage institutions, winter contracts, specialized couriers, and local obligations develop
→ seasonal planning shapes work, travel, debt, and hospitality
→ cellar design, preservation techniques, pack-animal markets,
  winter pricing, route offices, and local arguments become ordinary
```

The final details are valuable because the system explains why they exist.

This method is about **functional consequence**: how geography, ecology, economics, politics, religion, technology, or magic alter the way people organize life.

For accumulated historical aftermath across generations, use [Writing the Sequel](Writing-the-Sequel.md).

## What to build in the middle layer

### Geography → lived systems

Do not stop at:

- mountains;
- forests;
- rivers;
- climate;
- coastlines.

Ask:

- where do people actually move?
- what becomes expensive?
- what becomes seasonal?
- which routes become institutions?
- where do people settle and why?
- which places become marginal?
- what does the environment force people to plan around?

Then derive:

- roads;
- bridges;
- ferries;
- passes;
- storage;
- settlement shapes;
- markets;
- migration;
- architecture;
- seasonal labor;
- political chokepoints.

### Ecology → food culture

Do not define cuisine by a list of themed dishes.

Use:

```text
ecology
→ available ingredients
→ seasonality
→ preservation
→ fuel and cooking constraints
→ trade access
→ class differences
→ religious/social rules
→ food habits
→ named dishes
```

A region's food should contain:

- ordinary food;
- poor food;
- rich food;
- travel food;
- work food;
- festival food;
- preservation food;
- imported/status food;
- diaspora adaptations;
- border mixtures;
- foods younger people reject or revive.

Food is an output of the world system, not a costume attached to a region.

### History → institutions and memory

A war, famine, migration, treaty, disaster, or political change should leave:

- laws;
- land claims;
- monuments;
- grudges;
- institutions;
- family stories;
- holidays;
- ruined infrastructure;
- place names;
- political taboos;
- contradictory memories;
- old emergency measures that became permanent.

This is especially important for the Convergence.

The question is not only **what happened?**

It is:

> What still exists because it happened?

### Economy → ordinary behavior

Trade should affect more than export tables.

Ask:

- who gets paid when?
- who carries debt?
- what is bought on credit?
- what must be imported?
- what happens when the route fails?
- what professions become prestigious?
- who is exploited?
- which goods are socially important despite being economically minor?

Then derive:

- contracts;
- markets;
- class habits;
- apprenticeships;
- bargaining customs;
- household storage;
- lending;
- migration;
- smuggling;
- corruption;
- status objects;
- stereotypes and resentments.

### Politics → jurisdiction

Do not stop at monarchy, council, clan, or city-state.

Ask:

- who can actually tell whom to do what?
- who collects money?
- who owns land?
- who resolves disputes?
- who controls roads, water, ports, forests, mines, or markets?
- what happens when two valid authorities disagree?

Then derive:

- offices;
- forms;
- courts;
- exceptions;
- permits;
- customary rights;
- corruption;
- appeals;
- rival jurisdictions;
- political rituals;
- mundane bureaucracy.

### Religion → social infrastructure

Do not stop at beliefs or gods.

Ask what a religious institution actually does.

Possible functions:

- burial;
- marriage;
- recordkeeping;
- education;
- charity;
- mediation;
- hospitality;
- pilgrimage;
- medical care;
- calendars;
- oaths;
- inheritance;
- sanctuary;
- archives.

Then ask how different regions adapt the same faith differently.

### Magic → normalization

If magic remains in the setting, the question is not only what magic can do.

Ask:

- who learns it?
- who pays for it?
- who regulates it?
- which mundane professions changed because of it?
- which problems did it fail to solve?
- what precautions are completely ordinary?
- what infrastructure exists only because magic exists?

If magic produces no ordinary consequences, it is probably sitting above the world instead of inside it.

## Build outward, not downward

Middle worldbuilding is not a command to fully detail one region before touching another.

Prefer **cross-system passes**.

Example:

1. define winter transport constraints across the world;
2. see how each region adapts differently;
3. derive different foods, work patterns, laws, and rituals;
4. connect those differences through trade and migration;
5. then deepen individual places.

This prevents six regions from becoming six isolated themed boxes.

## Use differences inside regions

A middle-worldbuilding answer should rarely produce one behavior for an entire region.

Always consider:

- urban / rural;
- rich / poor;
- coast / inland;
- old / young;
- center / border;
- local / migrant;
- guild member / outsider;
- religious / secular;
- settled / itinerant;
- elite version / everyday version.

Culture becomes believable when people argue over it.

## Use time

Ask the same middle question across generations.

A practice may move through:

```text
necessity
→ adaptation
→ tradition
→ institution
→ cliché
→ backlash
→ revival
```

This is where a great deal of cultural texture comes from.

Something people call "ancient tradition" may have started as a practical response only a few generations ago.

## Relationship to MAPS_L

### Spiderweb

Middle worldbuilding should improve recoverability.

A detail should be traceable backward:

```text
detail → cultural habit → institution/adaptation → pressure → world condition
```

And forward:

```text
world condition → consequences → adaptations → new dependencies → new details
```

This is exactly where Spiderweb becomes useful for worldbuilding rather than just documentation.

### E/I

Middle-worldbuilding passes create strong E/I opportunities.

When separate systems are compared, we may discover:

- an institution should exist that nobody explicitly invented;
- two regional practices probably share an older origin;
- a trade dependency should produce a political faction;
- a religious custom should alter inheritance;
- a food-preservation method should affect architecture;
- a historical event should still shape slang or law.

Capture those as E/I candidates before promoting them.

## Relationship to Writing the Sequel

[Writing the Sequel](Writing-the-Sequel.md) is related but separate.

Middle worldbuilding asks:

> How does this condition change the way the world functions?

Writing the Sequel asks:

> What accumulated after people lived with that reality for years or generations?

Use Middle Worldbuilding to build the functional system, then Writing the Sequel to build historical residue, normalization, inherited institutions, and cultural memory.

## Relationship to Worldbuilding Breath

[Worldbuilding Breath](Worldbuilding-Breath.md) asks how a finished or partly finished world feels alive when revealed in prose.

These are three separate layers:

- **Middle Worldbuilding** builds functional systems.
- **Writing the Sequel** builds accumulated aftermath through time.
- **Worldbuilding Breath** decides how little of either layer needs to be explained in a scene.

They are complementary.

## Current priority

While region identities are being redesigned, prioritize:

1. **causal structure** over names;
2. **ecology and geography** over aesthetic theme;
3. **systems and adaptations** over isolated facts;
4. **internal variation** over regional stereotypes;
5. **historical residue** over clean timelines;
6. **cross-regional consequences** over self-contained region dossiers;
7. **specific detail derived from causes** rather than arbitrary flavor.

This does **not** mean withholding detail.

It means making detail earn its place by connecting to the world.

## Working question set

For any major worldbuilding premise, ask:

1. What immediate practical problem or opportunity does this create?
2. How did people adapt?
3. Who organized or profited from that adaptation?
4. What institution or custom formed?
5. What became so normal people stopped explaining it?
6. How does class, geography, generation, or profession change the answer?
7. What concrete foods, buildings, jobs, laws, tools, rituals, slang, prejudices, and routines follow?
8. What happens when the original condition changes?
9. What older adaptation persists even after it stops being useful?
10. What does another region misunderstand about it?

Those questions are the current bridge between the macro world and the detailed world we want to build.
