## Status

**Provisional political-structure test — exact offices, capitals, votes, titles, and constitutional documents remain open.**

The purpose is to answer:

> **What political functions must exist for the High Roads to work, without inventing one tidy national government merely for convenience?**

---

# 1. Starting condition

High Roads power is already distributed among:

- route authorities;
- old families;
- merchant houses;
- caravan organizations;
- urban councils;
- religious institutions;
- herding communities.

Historically, authority was also fragmented among:

- pass lords;
- toll towns;
- fortress communities;
- caravan families;
- merchant confederations.

Therefore the design should begin with **distributed authority**, not a missing crown.

---

# 2. What actually requires coordination

The network creates recurring problems that cannot be solved by one household or one town alone.

At minimum:

1. major road / pass maintenance;
2. bridge / shelter repair;
3. route-status information;
4. toll predictability;
5. guide / caravan obligations;
6. cross-locality arbitration;
7. response to banditry / attacks;
8. treatment of stranded travelers / cargo;
9. recognition of some records / credentials;
10. representation when another polity needs to negotiate with the route system.

Any political architecture that cannot handle these functions is too weak.

Any architecture that controls far more than these functions without historical cause risks overcentralizing the High Roads.

---

# 3. Local government remains real government

High Roads settlements should retain meaningful control over:

- streets;
- local markets;
- land;
- housing;
- local policing;
- ordinary crime;
- local tax / fee systems;
- water;
- local building;
- household disputes.

A route compact should not need to decide:

- who inherits a house;
- which shop may open;
- how one town elects officials;
- local marriage law.

This preserves political variation.

---

# 4. Route districts / corridor compacts

The strongest middle layer is **corridor-specific cooperation**.

A major route can require agreements among:

- towns;
- pass authorities;
- bridge holders;
- caravan interests;
- local rulers;
- herding communities;
- maintenance organizations.

Those agreements can cover:

- road repair shares;
- toll expectations;
- closure procedures;
- emergency shelters;
- escort obligations;
- recognized guides;
- route arbitration.

Different corridors may have different arrangements.

This is preferable to one universal High Roads code.

---

# 5. Overlapping membership is a feature

A town can participate in:

- one east-west route compact;
- one northern pass agreement;
- one market league;
- one religious network;
- one caravan credential system.

Therefore High Roads politics can be **networked rather than nested**.

A settlement's strongest political relationship may depend on the issue.

For road repair:
- one compact matters.

For market law:
- another institution matters.

For external diplomacy:
- a larger delegation may form.

---

# 6. Route authority need not mean territorial sovereignty

A route authority can have limited competence over:

- maintenance;
- safety;
- toll rules;
- closure;
- signage / markers;
- shelters;
- guide recognition.

It does not automatically govern all people living near the road.

This helps distinguish:

- political territory;
- functional jurisdiction.

A local ruler may control land while a route compact controls what counts as safe commercial passage.

---

# 7. Arbitration is the strongest region-wide connective tissue

The current legal owner already supports strong arbitration traditions.

This suggests the High Roads can achieve wide political coherence through **recognized forums and procedures** before it achieves central government.

Possible system:

- local disputes stay local;
- route disputes go to corridor forums;
- disputes spanning several corridors seek a more prestigious neutral forum;
- parties choose recognized arbiters by contract / custom.

Exact halls or courts remain open.

The important point is that arbitration can produce regional interoperability without one supreme court.

---

# 8. Toll politics

Tolls are a likely source of both revenue and conflict.

A route system cannot function if every small authority can unpredictably change charges.

But total toll uniformity is unnecessary.

A workable model needs:

- known toll locations;
- some limits on arbitrary repeated collection;
- records / receipts;
- dispute methods;
- emergency exceptions.

Possible later standards can emerge through commercial practice.

Do not assume one High Roads tax code.

---

# 9. Maintenance finance

Roads require recurring money / labor.

Possible funding can mix:

- toll revenue;
- town contributions;
- merchant subscriptions;
- guild contracts;
- landholder obligations;
- ruler subsidy;
- emergency levies.

Different routes can use different mixtures.

This naturally creates arguments over:

- who pays;
- whose traffic causes wear;
- who gets priority repair;
- whether distant merchants should fund local roads.

Those arguments are politics.

---

# 10. Route closure

The North Shoulder test already shows that "open" or "closed" is partly institutional judgment.

High Roads therefore needs some way to recognize credible route-status reports.

Possible layers:

- local guides / maintainers observe;
- roadhead authority records;
- caravan networks retransmit;
- affected compacts accept or dispute the report.

No central weather ministry is needed.

But a false closure report can have political consequences.

---

# 11. Security

The High Roads should not have one standing continental police force by default.

Security can be layered:

- local guards;
- caravan guards;
- hired escorts;
- route patrols;
- temporary multi-polity operations;
- local militias.

A major attack on a shared corridor can trigger cooperation under:

- route compact;
- Convergence security obligations;
- commercial pressure.

The exact command structure can vary by crisis.

---

# 12. Herding communities must not be politically decorative

The road network crosses land used by people who are not merely service providers to merchants.

Herding communities may control:

- seasonal pasture;
- water;
- local paths;
- pack animals;
- guide knowledge.

A route project can therefore conflict with:

- grazing;
- migration;
- water access;
- enclosure.

A functional High Roads political order must provide some way for these communities to:

- negotiate;
- resist;
- bargain;
- hold recognized claims.

Otherwise "route authority" becomes merchant colonialism by default.

---

# 13. Urban councils and route compacts may disagree

A major market town may favor:

- lower tolls;
- road widening;
- more warehouses;
- year-round traffic.

A rural / herding district may favor:

- seasonal limits;
- slower expansion;
- protected pasture;
- fewer outsider rights.

These are not pro-trade versus anti-trade caricatures.

They are competing cost distributions.

---

# 14. Merchant houses should influence, not automatically rule

Merchants can supply:

- finance;
- information;
- political pressure;
- credit;
- repair funding.

But if merchants directly control every route authority, the region becomes too economically monocultural.

Counterweights can include:

- town governments;
- old landed / route families;
- guilds;
- religious institutions;
- herding communities;
- local rulers.

Exact balance remains place-specific.

---

# 15. Religious institutions

Religious institutions may matter because they can provide:

- traveler lodging;
- neutral meeting places;
- records;
- mediation;
- burial;
- charity;
- route memory.

Their authority should depend on specific faith / locality.

Do not create a universal religious route government.

---

# 16. External diplomacy creates a representation problem

Other polities sometimes need a High Roads answer to questions like:

- Is this corridor open?
- Who can bind this route compact?
- Who negotiates a cross-border security agreement?
- Who represents High Roads at a Convergence dispute?

Because there is no one obvious sovereign, representation may be **issue-specific**.

A plausible working pattern:

local / corridor issue  
→ relevant authorities negotiate directly

multi-corridor issue  
→ temporary or recurring delegate assembly

major external issue  
→ recognized delegation drawn from affected authorities

This is enough to make diplomacy possible without a permanent national executive.

---

# 17. A recurring assembly is plausible, but not required as a parliament

The network may eventually produce recurring gatherings for:

- route condition;
- toll disputes;
- arbitration precedent;
- major repairs;
- external negotiations.

Such a gathering could become politically prestigious.

But it does not need authority over all High Roads life.

Important distinction:

> **coordination assembly ≠ sovereign legislature**

This is the strongest current solution to the "who speaks for High Roads?" problem.

Exact location, membership, voting, and permanence remain open.

---

# 18. Representation should follow stakes

For one route problem, relevant representatives might include:

- town delegates;
- route maintainers;
- caravan organizations;
- local ruler;
- herding representative.

For another problem, the mix changes.

That creates a flexible but messy political culture.

It also creates disputes over:

- who counts as affected;
- who gets a seat;
- whether wealth buys too much influence.

---

# 19. Could one leader dominate?

Yes, temporarily or partially.

A powerful ruler / family / city might dominate:

- one corridor;
- one assembly;
- one crisis.

But durable region-wide rule would require control over too many independent systems:

- passes;
- towns;
- herding land;
- caravan finance;
- arbitration;
- local legitimacy.

That makes centralization possible as a political project, not the default condition.

---

# 20. Convergence relationship

The High Roads fits the layered Convergence model well.

## Core Convergence

May protect:

- major passage;
- cross-border exchange;
- arbitration;
- route security.

## Attached agreements

Can govern:

- specific passes;
- border roadheads;
- bridge rights;
- shared patrols.

## Later standards

Can include:

- caravan documents;
- date conversion;
- guide credentials;
- cargo marks.

## Local law

Still governs:

- most town life;
- local taxes;
- property;
- crime;
- internal political offices.

---

# 21. Council relationship

The Council benefits from fragmented coordination because it can:

- finance repairs;
- broker disputes;
- compare information;
- move credit across jurisdictions.

But it does not need to own High Roads institutions.

The stronger the public / guild route-coordination network becomes, the more it can substitute for some Council functions.

This is relevant to the wider post-Council question.

---

# 22. Villain relationship

The High Roads political architecture creates several manipulation opportunities:

- false closure information;
- bribed toll official;
- forged route authorization;
- funding one faction in a repair dispute;
- making one corridor believe another receives favoritism;
- attacks timed to discredit a route authority.

The system's strength—distributed authority—is also a vulnerability because responsibility can be ambiguous.

---

# 23. Stress test — major bridge collapse

A major bridge used by several jurisdictions collapses.

Needed response:

- local emergency control;
- route diversion;
- funding negotiations;
- repair contract;
- arbitration over responsibility;
- temporary toll / ferry adjustment.

No region-wide king is required.

**Pass.**

A corridor compact / delegate mechanism is sufficient.

---

# 24. Stress test — coordinated bandit attacks

Several caravans are attacked across two jurisdictions.

Needed response:

- shared information;
- temporary security coordination;
- escort changes;
- investigation;
- dispute over which authority failed.

Again, a permanent unified army is unnecessary.

**Pass.**

---

# 25. Stress test — one town imposes predatory tolls

A town raises tolls high enough to divert trade.

Possible responses:

- merchants reroute;
- neighboring towns benefit;
- corridor compact challenges the toll;
- arbitration;
- reciprocal restriction;
- local political pressure from businesses that lose traffic.

**Strong pass.**

The network can discipline one locality through exit / pressure without a central sovereign.

---

# 26. Stress test — herding route blocked by road expansion

A major road project disrupts seasonal animal movement.

Needed politics:

- local property / use-right dispute;
- route-development interest;
- affected herding communities;
- compensation / rerouting / seasonal crossing.

If the system has no forum for this, it is too merchant-dominated.

**Pass only if local / corridor institutions recognize nonmerchant claims.**

This is a design constraint.

---

# 27. Stress test — external treaty negotiation

A North Coast polity wants a formal security / access agreement for the principal North Shoulder route.

Who answers?

Strongest working response:

- the relevant High Roads-side roadhead authority;
- affected corridor compact;
- local political authority / authorities;
- possibly a wider delegate body if the agreement affects several routes.

No single "High Roads ambassador" is necessary.

**Pass.**

Issue-specific representation works.

---

# 28. Writing the Sequel — how the system could accumulate

A plausible historical sequence:

## Stage 1 — local control

Passes, tolls, towns, herding routes, and caravan families operate independently.

## Stage 2 — repeated conflict

Unpredictable tolls, attacks, bridge failures, and overlapping claims create expensive friction.

## Stage 3 — corridor compacts

Neighboring authorities negotiate limited shared rules.

## Stage 4 — arbitration reputation

Some forums become trusted beyond one corridor.

## Stage 5 — recurring multi-route meetings

Crises / major projects create periodic gatherings among several compacts.

## Present

People may talk about "High Roads law" or "High Roads authority" even though the actual system remains a patchwork of:

- local law;
- corridor agreements;
- recognized forums;
- professional standards;
- temporary delegations.

This is a strong inherited-present model.

---

# 29. Breath seeds — non-canon

- A delegate complains that a meeting "has no authority" immediately before arguing for three hours to change its decision.
- One town's toll receipt is accepted everywhere despite no one remembering why its format became standard.
- Caravan workers call a route assembly by the name of an inn where meetings stopped being held generations ago.
- Herding representatives deliberately arrive after merchant delegations have already rearranged the seating.
- A bridge-maintenance rule is universally blamed on a disaster whose details nobody agrees on.
- One road authority's marker is considered prestigious enough that counterfeit versions appear on routes it never inspected.

None are canon.

---

# 30. Strong structural result

The High Roads does **not** need one final constitution yet.

The world works with:

1. strong local polities / communities;
2. corridor-specific route compacts;
3. recognized arbitration forums;
4. professional / guild interoperability;
5. temporary or recurring multi-route delegate gatherings;
6. issue-specific external delegations.

This produces enough coordination for:

- trade;
- safety;
- repair;
- law;
- diplomacy;

without turning High Roads into a hidden centralized state.

---

# 31. What remains open

- exact named polities;
- exact corridor compacts;
- whether any assembly has a permanent name;
- whether one meeting place becomes prestigious;
- voting / representation rules;
- exact toll systems;
- exact route authorities;
- exact military arrangements;
- exact herding rights;
- whether any one city currently dominates the network;
- exact Convergence signatories from High Roads.

---

# 32. Disposition

**Strong functional pass.**

The best current political description is:

> **High Roads is a networked political order in which local authorities remain sovereign or semi-sovereign in ordinary matters, while route-specific compacts, arbitration forums, professional networks, and issue-specific delegations coordinate problems that cross local boundaries.**

This should replace the vague question "is High Roads a confederation?" with more useful questions about which functions are shared and by whom.
