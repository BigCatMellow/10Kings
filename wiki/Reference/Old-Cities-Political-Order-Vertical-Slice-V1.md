# Old Cities Political Order Vertical Slice — V1

## Status

**Provisional political-order test — not final canon.**

This page tests OC-U / OC-D / OC-C from:

- [Working Political Geography — V1](Working-Political-Geography-V1.md)
- [Old Cities Functional Governance Test](Old-Cities-Functional-Governance-Test.md)
- [Old Cities Shared Irrigation Test](Old-Cities-Shared-Irrigation-Test.md)
- [Political Border History Test — V1](Political-Border-History-Test-V1.md)

It does not establish:

- city names;
- exact constitutions;
- ruler titles;
- exact borders;
- one Old Cities league;
- permanent federation;
- exact irrigation agreement text;
- exact citizenship law;
- exact military alliances.

The purpose is to answer:

> **How can several fully sovereign city-states maintain durable shared institutions without those institutions becoming a regional government?**

---

# 1. Starting constraint

Old Cities needs:

- real city-state sovereignty;
- uneven hinterlands;
- strong private / merchant power;
- cross-border water institutions;
- temporary alliances;
- durable rivalry.

V1 therefore tests a **three-city minimum system**:

- OC-U — upstream / inland city-state;
- OC-D — downstream / lower-basin city-state;
- OC-C — independent coastal rival.

The triad is deliberately asymmetric.

Each city needs different material incentives.

---

# 2. Historical formation model

## SUPPORTED

Pre-Convergence Old Cities included:

- city-states;
- irrigation leagues;
- estate regions;
- ports;
- merchant dynasties;
- rural client territories.

Temporary alliances were common.

Waterworks often crossed political boundaries.

## PROVISIONAL V1 sequence

~~~
older settlements grow around water / trade nodes
→ civic governments consolidate surrounding districts unevenly
→ shared irrigation survives changes in political control
→ cities fight, bargain, intermarry, and sponsor rival rural clients
→ Convergence recognizes practical civic sovereignty
→ attached water / route arrangements preserve necessary cross-border systems
→ alliances become more institutional without becoming permanent union
~~~

Modern sovereignty therefore sits on top of older shared infrastructure.

---

# 3. OC-U — upstream / inland city-state

OC-U's leverage comes from:

- upstream position;
- headworks / diversions;
- inland estates;
- R5 / Longwood access;
- agricultural hinterland.

It needs real authority over:

- taxation;
- military force;
- city law;
- diplomacy;
- major water / land policy inside its territory.

## Political pressure

OC-U benefits from upstream control.

But abusing it can produce:

- downstream retaliation;
- arbitration;
- trade consequences;
- reputational cost;
- internal opposition from merchants / landowners dependent on stability.

This makes water leverage real without making it absolute.

---

# 4. OC-D — downstream / lower-basin city-state

OC-D depends more heavily on continued shared flow.

It also controls:

- dense irrigated territory;
- markets;
- lower-basin infrastructure;
- possible coastal / near-coastal access.

Its politics naturally emphasize:

- predictable water;
- maintenance rights;
- emergency warning;
- protection from upstream coercion.

## Political pressure

OC-D may support stronger shared-water institutions than OC-U.

That does not mean it favors broader federation.

It wants security in the system it depends on.

---

# 5. OC-C — independent coastal rival

OC-C is not bound to the same S1 water dependence.

Its strength comes more from:

- maritime trade;
- M2 access;
- merchant networks;
- civic wealth;
- external diplomacy.

### Why OC-C matters

If OC-U and OC-D cooperate, OC-C must decide whether that is:

- sensible water management;
- defense coordination;
- a trade bloc;
- the beginning of hegemony.

Its concern can be reasonable without either side plotting unification.

This creates genuine balance-of-power politics.

---

# 6. Each city remains sovereign

V1 assumes each city can independently:

- tax;
- legislate locally;
- raise forces;
- negotiate treaties;
- regulate citizenship / residence;
- control its markets;
- govern its hinterland.

Shared institutions do not override that sovereignty except within explicitly delegated functions.

This is the main design rule.

---

# 7. Uneven hinterlands

City-state sovereignty does not require uniform control outside the walls.

A city's wider territory can contain:

- estates;
- villages;
- rural councils;
- port towns;
- irrigation districts;
- client communities.

Some may possess old privileges.

Some may be governed through intermediaries.

### Political consequence

An external observer may describe OC-U as one state.

A rural tenant may experience power through:

- estate owner;
- local official;
- irrigation association;
- city tax collector.

Both are true.

---

# 8. Shared S1 water institution

OC-U and OC-D need a durable functional body or compact.

Its authority can include:

- maintenance access;
- flow records;
- warning procedures;
- gate schedules;
- drought rules;
- dispute procedure.

It does **not** govern:

- ordinary criminal law;
- taxation;
- foreign policy;
- unrelated land disputes.

### Strong rule

The water compact can outlive several governments because its practical function is older than any current administration.

That gives it historical legitimacy without sovereignty.

---

# 9. Technical officials as political actors

Water management produces specialists with unusual cross-border importance.

They may hold:

- flow records;
- repair history;
- gate measurements;
- precedent.

During a crisis, rulers may rely on people who are not powerful in ordinary politics.

### Political consequence

A canal engineer or record keeper can temporarily matter more than a noble speech.

This is useful Old Cities texture.

---

# 10. Inter-city diplomacy

Old Cities diplomacy is regular because the actors are close and interdependent.

Likely subjects:

- water;
- tariffs;
- labor;
- ports;
- road access;
- extradition;
- merchant disputes;
- defense.

This can produce permanent embassies / resident agents later if needed.

V1 does not require them yet.

---

# 11. Temporary leagues

Two or more cities can form a league for:

- defense;
- trade;
- water emergency;
- piracy;
- route security.

A league may possess:

- treasury contribution;
- joint command;
- delegate meeting;
- limited arbitration.

### Critical limit

League competence remains delegated.

A defense league cannot automatically:

- tax citizens directly;
- rewrite local law;
- appoint city officials.

If those powers appear later, that is constitutional change.

---

# 12. Why leagues cause fear

A temporary league changes bargaining power.

Even if OC-U and OC-D coordinate only for drought / defense:

- merchants may begin standardizing contracts;
- soldiers train together;
- delegates meet repeatedly;
- outsiders negotiate with them jointly.

OC-C may reasonably fear institutional accumulation.

Writing the Sequel matters here:

> temporary coordination can create durable capacity even when nobody originally intended federation.

---

# 13. Merchant / noble private power

Each city contains powerful private actors.

They may control:

- credit;
- warehouses;
- ships;
- estates;
- labor;
- patronage.

They influence foreign policy because their assets cross borders.

But they do not automatically replace civic government.

### Political consequence

A merchant house can favor a treaty its city opposes.

It can finance the opposition.

That creates internal foreign-policy politics.

---

# 14. Citizenship and residence

V1 does not define citizenship.

It does require city-specific political membership to matter.

A person can be:

- resident in OC-D;
- born in OC-U;
- employed by an OC-C merchant;
- married across the border.

This creates legal questions around:

- taxes;
- military obligation;
- inheritance;
- property;
- court standing.

Shared regional culture does not erase civic membership.

---

# 15. Military structure

Each city needs independent coercive capacity.

Possible layers:

- civic guard;
- citizen / district levy;
- hired troops;
- estate contingents;
- naval / harbor forces where relevant.

Joint league forces are temporary / delegated.

### Design rule

No permanent Old Cities army exists in V1.

If one appears later, that is evidence of real political consolidation.

---

# 16. Fiscal structure

Each city has its own:

- taxes;
- customs;
- market fees;
- estate obligations;
- water fees where applicable.

Shared institutions need separate funding arrangements.

The S1 compact might use:

- assessed contributions;
- maintenance labor;
- shared toll / water fee;
- emergency levy.

The exact mechanism remains open.

### Political consequence

Funding disputes can become more important than constitutional theory.

---

# 17. OC-U / OC-D ordinary relationship

Most years their relationship should be routine.

They may argue about:

- maintenance;
- measures;
- migration;
- tariffs.

But shared water gives strong reasons to continue talking.

### Important tone

They do not need to be eternal enemies.

Long rivalry can coexist with:

- marriage;
- trade;
- technical cooperation;
- shared jokes;
- mutual contempt.

---

# 18. OC-D / OC-C relationship

This rivalry should be more commercial / diplomatic than hydrological.

Possible issues:

- port access;
- merchant competition;
- alliances;
- cultural prestige;
- refugee policy;
- M2 trade.

Their border can be stable.

That prevents every Old Cities dispute from being about canals.

---

# 19. OC-U / OC-C relationship

They need not share a direct border in V1.

They can still interact through:

- trade;
- diplomacy;
- alliance;
- patronage.

If future geography gives them a border, that can be added later.

No need to force one now.

---

# 20. Test 1 — current drought anxiety

Water remains available but fear rises.

OC-U actors begin:

- hoarding;
- diversions;
- guard presence.

OC-D demands transparency.

The S1 institution produces flow information.

Merchants react before scarcity is proven.

**STRONG PASS.**

The system makes information politically consequential.

---

# 21. Test 2 — gate sabotage accusation

A gate breaks.

OC-D suspects OC-U.

Technical officials say failure may be ordinary.

Rulers face domestic pressure.

The shared institution provides:

- inspection;
- records;
- cross-border access.

**STRONG PASS.**

Institutional continuity can slow escalation without eliminating rivalry.

---

# 22. Test 3 — unilateral OC-U diversion

OC-U authorizes a new canal / diversion.

OC-D claims treaty / customary violation.

Possible escalation:

technical complaint  
→ negotiation  
→ arbitration  
→ commercial pressure  
→ retaliation

No federal court is required.

**PASS.**

---

# 23. Test 4 — OC-U / OC-D defensive league

Outside threat causes joint defense agreement.

The league develops:

- shared command;
- contributions;
- meetings.

OC-C becomes alarmed.

### Key test

Can the league remain limited?

Yes, if:

- cities retain separate taxes;
- separate diplomacy outside league scope;
- separate law;
- explicit exit / term rules.

**STRONG PASS.**

The current city-state coordination storyline has a concrete structure.

---

# 24. Test 5 — league institutions persist

Threat fades.

Some shared staff / records remain because they are useful.

OC-C argues the league is becoming permanent.

OC-U / OC-D disagree internally over whether to dismantle it.

**STRONG PASS.**

This is Writing the Sequel applied to alliance politics.

---

# 25. Test 6 — merchant dynasty cross-border influence

A major house finances:

- OC-D warehouses;
- OC-C shipping;
- OC-U estates.

Each government suspects divided loyalty.

The family can pressure all three but cannot legally govern any of them.

**PASS.**

Private networks cross sovereignty without erasing it.

---

# 26. Test 7 — refugee / labor movement

Economic crisis drives people from OC-U to OC-D / OC-C.

Cities respond differently.

Employers may welcome labor.

Workers may resist wage pressure.

Political membership matters.

**PASS.**

Shared culture does not eliminate migration politics.

---

# 27. Test 8 — OC-C maritime blockade pressure

OC-C restricts some coastal trade.

OC-D suffers.

OC-U sees opportunity.

Possible responses:

- alternate route;
- retaliation;
- coalition;
- Convergence complaint if protected access is implicated.

**PASS.**

The triad creates politics beyond water.

---

# 28. Test 9 — proposed permanent union

Reformers argue that recurring:

- water cooperation;
- defense leagues;
- trade standards

prove the cities should federate.

Opponents argue:

- local sovereignty works;
- shared bodies already solve shared functions;
- a federal center would create new domination.

### Result

Both positions have credible institutional evidence.

**STRONG PASS.**

Union becomes a real political question rather than an obvious historical destiny.

---

# 29. Test 10 — OC-U tries to dominate the S1 institution

OC-U uses upstream leverage to demand more control.

OC-D can respond with:

- treaty / customary claims;
- arbitration;
- trade pressure;
- outside support.

The institution does not have to defeat OC-U militarily.

It needs to make abuse costly enough that ordinary cooperation remains attractive.

**PASS.**

---

# 30. Strong V1 conclusion

Old Cities works best as:

> **a competitive system of sovereign city-states whose shared water, trade, defense, and arbitration institutions possess real delegated authority without constituting a regional government.**

OC-U, OC-D, and OC-C remain sovereign.

Their coordination is politically important precisely because it may accumulate.

The system's central constitutional question is:

> **When does useful coordination become a new government?**

The answer remains contested in-world.

---

# 31. Comparison with High Roads

## High Roads

Coordination links multiple local polities whose legitimacy grows from routes and overlapping compacts.

There is no strong expectation that they should become one state.

## Old Cities

The city-states are clearer sovereign units.

Shared institutions are more visibly **delegations by states**.

This makes federation:

- easier to imagine;
- more threatening;
- more politically explicit.

### Result

High Roads and Old Cities are both plural but structurally different.

That passes another anti-symmetry test.

# 33. Promotion gate

Alternative models:

## A. Regional federation

Fails because current world treats durable union as a major change, not established fact.

## B. Fully independent cities with ad hoc diplomacy only

Fails because shared water / repeated coordination need durable institutions.

## C. Water-state confederation

Fails because OC-C and other cities are not defined by one water system.

## D. Sovereign city-state system with delegated functional institutions

Fits:

- water;
- alliances;
- merchant networks;
- current drought;
- current coordination anxiety;
- continued civic sovereignty.

**V1 winner: sovereign city-state system with delegated functional institutions.**
