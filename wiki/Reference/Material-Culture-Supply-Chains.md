# Material Culture Supply Chains

## Status

**Reference / provisional synthesis.**

This page connects [Clothing and Material Culture](../Culture/Clothing-and-Material-Culture.md) to the established pattern-level trade system in [Trade and Dependencies](../Economy/Trade-and-Dependencies.md).

It does **not** establish exact crop species, textile quantities, monopolies, prices, or named guilds.

## Established pattern-level anchors

Current trade canon supports:

- **River Country:** textiles and other agricultural goods as meaningful exports;
- **North Country:** furs / animal products and maritime transport;
- **Forest Country:** dyes, forest products, timber and specialist ecological goods;
- **South Country:** craft / luxury goods and southern maritime access;
- **Western Uplands:** metalwork, tools, fittings and industrial craft capacity;
- **High Country:** movement, brokerage, records and caravan services;
- **Port:** processing, exchange, storage, shipping, repair and information.

That is enough to build a textile / clothing economy without deciding that one region supplies all cloth.

---

# 1. Fiber production

## River Country

**Strongest current candidate for large-volume plant-fiber and general textile production.**

Why:

- broad productive lowlands;
- diversified agriculture;
- established “textiles and other agricultural goods” export;
- mills, market towns and river movement.

Possible fiber families remain open:

- flax-like;
- hemp-like;
- cotton-like only where climate actually supports it;
- animal fiber from livestock districts.

Do not assign all of them automatically.

## High Country

Likely local supply:

- wool / hair from upland herding;
- felt;
- woven animal fiber;
- some low-volume local plant fiber where terrain allows.

High Country's larger importance may be **redistribution and finishing** rather than bulk fiber production.

## North Country

Likely local supply:

- wool / animal hair in pastoral districts;
- leather / hide;
- fur;
- possibly local plant / bast fibers in milder areas.

Its maritime routes also make it a major carrier of imported cloth.

## South Country

Likely candidate for:

- warm-climate plant fibers;
- fine woven cloth in some irrigated districts;
- luxury finishing;
- dye-intensive urban textile production;
- imported fibers processed in city workshops.

Exact crops remain open.

## Forest Country

Possible local materials:

- bast / bark / plant fibers;
- leather;
- some woven forest materials;
- dyes;
- tannins;
- specialty finishing materials.

Its strongest current system-level role is **dyes and forest-derived inputs**, not “everyone wears bark cloth.”

## Western Uplands

Likely local production:

- wool and other upland fibers where livestock exists;
- leather;
- coarse cloth;
- workwear production.

Its comparative advantage is more likely:

- metal fasteners;
- buckles;
- needles;
- shears;
- tools;
- looms / workshop hardware;
- protective leather / metal components;

than continental cloth dominance.

## Port

Port likely produces less raw fiber than it **sorts, cuts, repairs, resells, copies and recombines**.

It can become the world's deepest market for:

- secondhand clothing;
- damaged cargo textiles;
- imported luxury cloth;
- sailcloth;
- uniforms / guild issue;
- tailoring;
- buttons / fittings;
- specialty dyes;
- foreign clothing.

---

# 2. Dye and finishing network

Current trade already gives Forest Country a role in **dyes**.

That can connect to:

- River Country textile volume;
- South Country urban fashion;
- Port tailoring / resale;
- High Country caravan redistribution.

A plausible network is:

```text
Forest Country dye / tannin / forest inputs
        ↓
River Country or South Country textile workshops
        ↓
High Country / maritime routes
        ↓
Port markets and re-export
```

This is not exclusive.

Other dye sources should exist.

## Political consequence

Dye restrictions can become economically meaningful without making Forest Country “the dye region.”

A disease or extraction dispute affecting one valued dye plant could:

- raise fashion costs;
- encourage substitutes;
- create smuggling;
- make a previously cheap color prestigious;
- hurt a specific guild;
- spread counterfeit dyes.

This is a good example of material culture entering the world system.

---

# 3. Metal fittings

Western Uplands's craft capacity should show up in ordinary clothing through:

- buckles;
- pins;
- clasps;
- needles;
- scissors / shears;
- awls;
- hooks;
- small chains;
- belt hardware;
- shoe nails / fittings where appropriate.

High-quality metalwork can therefore influence clothing far outside Western Uplands.

A South Country elite garment may use an Western Uplands clasp.

A North Country weather coat may depend on Western Uplands needles or hardware.

A River Country weaver may resent rising tool prices even though no one in the household works metal.

---

# 4. Leather / hide / fur

Supply should be distributed.

Likely sources include:

- North Country maritime / pastoral animal products;
- Western Uplands upland livestock;
- River Country livestock;
- High Country herding;
- Forest Country hunting / domestic animals in some areas;
- South Country pastoral districts.

This prevents “North Country owns fur” or “Western Uplands owns leather.”

Differences should be about:

- type;
- quality;
- tanning;
- route;
- local law;
- prestige;

rather than absolute availability.

---

# 5. Textile movement

## River Country → Western Uplands

Already strong economically.

Likely material-culture flows:

- work cloth;
- household linens;
- agricultural fiber;
- finished garments;
- sacks / bags.

Return flows:

- needles;
- tools;
- buckles;
- shears;
- workshop fittings.

This makes clothing part of the Western Uplands–River Country “marriage of necessity.”

## North Country ↔ Port

Likely flows:

North Country → Port:
- hides;
- fur;
- wool / animal fiber;
- weather gear;
- sail / maritime materials.

Port → North Country:
- imported cloth;
- luxury trim;
- replacement hardware;
- fashions;
- secondhand garments.

## Forest Country → multiple markets

Likely:
- dyes;
- tannins;
- specialty fibers / forest inputs;
- wood for textile tools where suitable.

## South Country ↔ Port

Likely:
- fine cloth;
- dyes / finishing from southern sources;
- fashion goods;
- jewelry / decorative work;
- imported raw fibers processed in city workshops.

Port returns:
- northern materials;
- foreign fashions;
- secondhand luxury goods;
- metal fittings;
- mixed tailoring traditions.

## High Country

High Country acts as a redistribution system.

A garment or textile style can travel:

```text
River Country cloth
→ High Country caravan market
→ cut into local travel garment
→ worn to Port
→ copied by Port tailor
→ sold back into North Country
```

That is cultural circulation with a material mechanism.

# 6. Secondhand economy

Secondhand clothing should be structurally important.

## Why

Clothing is expensive.

Trade hubs concentrate:

- travelers selling surplus;
- dead / bankrupt household estates;
- military or guild surplus;
- damaged cargo;
- pawned garments;
- stolen goods;
- imported fashions;
- clothing abandoned by migrants.

## Best candidates

### Port

Likely the deepest and fastest secondhand market.

### High Country

Strong market for:
- travel clothing;
- caravan surplus;
- repaired gear;
- foreign cloth;
- practical exchange.

### Large regional cities

Each should have:
- used-clothing dealers;
- menders;
- dyers;
- resizers;
- shoe repair;
- rag / scrap trade.

## Social effect

A “foreign” garment does not necessarily mean its wearer traveled.

They may simply have bought it secondhand.

This makes visual culture less geographically deterministic.

---

# 7. Repair and reuse chain

A garment's life can be:

```text
new garment
→ ordinary wear
→ visible repair
→ resized for another person
→ work garment
→ child garment
→ patch material
→ bag / wrapping / stuffing / rag
```

Different parts of society enter the chain at different points.

This gives poverty and thrift concrete texture.

---

# 8. Fashion diffusion

The strongest system-level fashion mechanism is:

```text
wealth / novelty concentration
        ↓
Port / major city / court / festival
        ↓
performers + merchants + travelers
        ↓
High Country and maritime routes
        ↓
regional cities
        ↓
local copying / cheap substitution
        ↓
rural / working adaptation
```

The direction can reverse.

A practical worker garment can become fashionable after:

- a famous performer wears it;
- an Ascendant patron adopts it;
- Port turns it into a recognizable style.

This supports Port's ECW-like cultural role without forcing politics into every garment.

# 9. Current-event effects

## Western Uplands labor unrest

Possible ordinary effects:

- employers delay clothing allowances;
- workers repair protective gear longer;
- guild aid funds cover boots / gloves;
- Blackbellow recruitment includes equipment as part of compensation.

## North Country fish pressure

Possible effects:

- households postpone replacing imported cloth;
- maritime gear becomes more valuable;
- younger workers sell or pawn inherited clothing before migration.

## River Country abundance crisis

Possible effects:

- textile-producing households face falling agricultural prices;
- cheap fiber does not necessarily mean cheap finished cloth if credit / labor markets fail;
- land consolidation can disrupt household textile production.

## High Country caravan attacks

Possible effects:

- imported cloth prices rise locally;
- used travel gear becomes valuable;
- foreign fashion circulation slows or changes routes.

## Forest Country extraction conflict

Possible effects:

- specific dyes / tannins become restricted;
- smuggling or substitutes increase.

## South Country drought anxiety

Possible effects:

- water-intensive dyeing becomes politically controversial;
- elite fashion becomes a target of resentment.

## Port refugee / worker pressure

Possible effects:

- secondhand prices shift;
- tailoring labor expands;
- new garment forms and repair methods enter the city;
- neighborhood fashion changes quickly.

---

# 10. What remains open

Do not promote yet:

- exact fiber species;
- exact loom technology;
- exact dye chemistry;
- exact regional production shares;
- named textile guilds;
- sumptuary law;
- gendered textile labor patterns;
- exact fashion centers;
- exact garment terminology.

Those should emerge through concrete community tests and later food / household work.

## Result

The textile/material economy passes the Spiderweb test because it connects:

```text
ecology
→ farming / herding / forest extraction
→ textile production
→ guilds / tools
→ trade routes
→ class / repair
→ fashion
→ politics / current crises
```

Clothing can therefore become part of the world system rather than a visual appendix.
