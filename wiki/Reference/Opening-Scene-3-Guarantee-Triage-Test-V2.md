# Prose test

The guarantor's office was quieter than the records office and more expensive in every visible way.

The floor had been swept recently. The benches matched. The writing tables were wide enough that two people could spread papers without touching elbows.

Even the queue had been divided into separate lines.

Wurdren distrusted it immediately.

The grain agent did not appear to notice the furniture.

She was counting time.

"South Transfer closes intake at dusk."

Wurdren looked toward the windows.

"We have daylight."

"We have papers, another walk, unloading, weighing, receipt, and settlement."

"Less daylight."

"Considerably."

A clerk near the entrance glanced at the transfer sheet and sent them to the shorter line.

The grain agent read the board above it.

"Amendments."

"Promising."

"It sounds billable."

They waited.

At the far wall, runners moved in and out through a door marked for correspondence.

Every few minutes one returned with:

- a folded slip;
- a mark;
- another question.

The grain agent watched them.

"That's what we're waiting on?"

"Probably."

"You say that as if you understand this."

"I understand waiting."

"Useful profession."

"I have practiced widely."

Her mouth moved, almost a smile, then stopped when the next desk opened.

The guarantor's clerk held out a hand.

"Transfer."

The grain agent gave them the copied certification.

"Original buyer terms."

She produced those too.

"Warehouse release."

Another sheet.

The clerk read all three.

"Cargo remains grain?"

"Yes."

"Same quantity?"

"Yes."

"Same seller?"

"Yes."

"Same buyer?"

"Yes."

"Delay before arrival?"

"No."

She hesitated.

"Not before arrival."

"Current delay?"

"Berth and transfer."

"Damage?"

"No."

"Weight dispute?"

"No."

"Title dispute?"

"No."

The clerk put one finger beside the transfer certification.

"Recognized substitute warehouse. Clean underlying sale."

"Meaning?" the agent asked.

"Meaning this can qualify for the temporary staple-transfer backing."

"Can."

"Someone still approves it."

"How long?"

The clerk copied several lines onto a narrow sheet.

"Depends who answers."

"Can you narrow that?"

"No."

She exhaled through her nose.

The clerk sent the sheet through the correspondence door.

"You can sit."

"If I sit, will it be faster?"

"No."

"Then I'll stand."

Wurdren chose the bench.

His knee appreciated the commercial system more than the rest of him did.

The grain agent remained beside the desk.

"What exactly is temporary staple-transfer backing?"

The clerk looked at the papers rather than at her.

"Your original warehouse failed for capacity. The substitute is recognized. The buyer and cargo have not changed. The office has authority today to add supplemental backing to some staple transfers that meet those conditions."

"Because of Three Moons?"

"Because the conditions exist."

That was either precision or refusal.

Possibly both.

"What changes if they approve it?"

"The substitute warehouse can receive under the amended guarantee. The buyer can settle against the recognized receipt."

"And if they don't?"

"You already know."

The grain agent did.

She stopped asking.

A runner came through the correspondence door carrying several folded slips.

None came to their desk.

The agent began counting something on her fingers.

"Stop doing that," Wurdren said.

"What?"

"Making time visible."

"It is visible."

"Not normally this unpleasantly."

She ignored him.

Another runner.

Not theirs.

The grain agent checked the windows.

Then the folio.

Then the windows again.

Wurdren considered offering reassurance and decided she would correctly identify it as useless.

A third runner appeared.

The guarantor's clerk took one folded slip, opened it, and checked the mark at the bottom.

"Approved."

The grain agent did not move.

The clerk turned the sheet toward her.

"Supplemental backing through settlement today."

She looked at the mark.

Then sat.

Not gracefully.

Just suddenly, as though someone had removed a board she had been leaning against all day.

"We're covered?"

"For the warehouse substitution through settlement today."

"And tomorrow?"

"You do not want tomorrow."

"No."

The clerk added the endorsement to the transfer copy.

"This goes to South Transfer. They issue a recognized receipt. Buyer settles against that receipt."

The grain agent took the papers.

Her hand shook once.

She stopped it by closing the folio.

"What does this cost?"

The clerk named a fee.

She stared.

Then laughed once.

It was not amusement.

"Of course."

"Less than another day at berth."

"I know."

The clerk's tone did not change.

That made the answer easier to take.

Wurdren stood.

His knee objected.

He ignored it.

"South Transfer."

The grain agent nodded.

They had nearly reached the door when the clerk called after them.

"Settlement copy comes back here."

The agent turned.

"Why?"

"The supplemental backing ends when the buyer pays. If the closure is not recorded, the obligation remains open."

That explanation she understood immediately.

She nodded and they left.

Outside, Port seemed louder than before.

Carts passed in both directions. A man with a handbell was trying to clear a path for a load of timber and accomplishing mostly noise. Somewhere beyond the warehouses, festival music began and stopped after three notes.

The grain agent walked faster.

Wurdren kept up.

"How much have you lost?" he asked.

"Today?"

"Since the warehouse."

"Berth, runner fees, copies, amendment, transfer, guarantee."

"Approximately?"

She looked at him.

He remembered.

"Right."

She did the figures anyway.

"Less than if they had said no."

That was not the same as saying little.

South Transfer accepted the papers without argument.

That was the impressive part.

The clerk there checked:

- reciprocal mark;
- warehouse release;
- guarantor endorsement.

Then called for scales.

No one sent them back across Port.

No one asked Wurdren to find another witness.

No one argued about whether the substitute warehouse counted as a warehouse.

The grain moved.

Sacks came off the boat and onto the scale platform in a steady line.

Marks were checked.

Weights were entered.

A receipt was written.

The grain agent watched all of it.

When the final sack disappeared through the storage doors, she stood still for a moment.

"That's it?" Wurdren asked.

"No."

He waited.

She carried the receipt to a settlement desk at the far side of the yard.

That took another quarter hour.

Then she came back with:

- fewer papers;
- less tension in her face.

"Now that's it."

"Paid?"

"Enough."

Wurdren held out his hand.

She stared at it.

"My fee."

"I thought you were having a moment."

"I was."

She paid him.

He counted it.

She noticed.

"You made me count sacks twice."

"Consistency is a virtue."

"You don't know how many sacks there were."

"I knew there would still be the same number at the end."

She shook her head.

Then looked back at the warehouse.

"My brother gets paid."

Wurdren had not known she had a brother involved.

"Good."

"The boat crew gets paid."

"Also good."

"I can take the next contract instead of explaining why I lost this one."

"Better."

She tucked the remaining papers into the folio.

"For what it's worth, you could have left at the quay."

"I know."

"You could have left at the records office."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

The honest answer was not flattering.

Because he had not wanted his first job back on the road to end with him walking away while the person beside him lost money.

Because the problem had been his long enough that abandoning it felt like cheating.

Because none of that was the kind of reason he had imagined when he decided he needed to make something of the years he had left.

"Professional pride," he said.

She looked unconvinced.

"Very grand."

"I am trying to bring more grandeur into my work."

That finally got the smile.

They parted outside the yard.

The grain agent went back toward the river.

Wurdren turned toward the hiring boards.

For the first time since entering Port, he had:

- no cargo;
- no papers;
- no one waiting for him to fix something.

The day felt suddenly open.

Behind him, grain that had nearly become somebody's loss sat in a warehouse under a recognized seal.

A buyer would accept it.

A boat crew would be paid.

A brother upriver, apparently, would be paid too.

Wurdren considered the whole affair.

Respectable.

Useful.

Hardly the sort of thing a man came back to the road to be remembered for.

He headed toward the red-cornered notices.

# Short Records Worker handoff

By late afternoon, the transfer returned to the shared records office as three new entries.

The Records Worker checked them in order.

Alternate warehouse accepted.

Supplemental guarantee added.

Settlement completed.

They drew a line through the open transfer and wrote the closure reference beside it.

One problem finished.

The next file waiting on the desk belonged to a small Port merchant whose extension request had been returned from a different office.

Reduced term.

Higher uncovered share.

No allegation of default.

No dispute over the goods.

Just less backing than the request had assumed would be available.

The merchant had already written a complaint in the margin.

The Records Worker read it.

Then read the attached commercial notice.

A correspondent mark appeared at the bottom.

They looked back at the grain transfer.

Different immediate office.

Same correspondent mark.

That was not unusual by itself.

Large houses appeared on many papers.

The wording was newer:

> **temporary allocation terms**

on one.

> **supplemental staple authority**

on the other.

The Records Worker opened the day ledger and copied both exactly.

They did not circle the marks.

They did not start a separate page.

They did not decide they had discovered anything.

They filed the papers where they belonged.

Then they looked at the queue still waiting outside and reached for the next problem.

# V2 assessment

## System payoff

**PASS.**

The grain moves because several ordinary systems interlock:

~~~text
warehouse release
→ reciprocal recognition
→ temporary supplemental guarantee
→ recognized receipt
→ settlement
~~~

No single actor performs a miracle.

## Hidden triage

**STRONGER PASS THAN V1.**

The cost now appears:

- elsewhere;
- through records;
- without claiming one person's rescue directly caused one person's loss.

The pattern is:

> limited commercial backing is being reallocated.

That is enough.

## Reader-knowledge discipline

**PASS.**

Reader knows:

- somebody with broad commercial reach is changing terms across multiple transactions.

Reader does not know:

- Council structure;
- Council membership;
- whether this is political;
- how many sectors are being coordinated.

## Records Worker evidence seed

**STRONG PASS.**

They now have a plausible later memory of:

- repeated correspondent mark;
- repeated temporary-policy language.

Crucially:

> they do not notice it because the plot needs them to.

They record it because that is their job.

## Wurdren arc

**PASS.**

The scene gives him actual causal importance.

His error is not that he did nothing important.

His error is:

> he does not count the thing he did.
