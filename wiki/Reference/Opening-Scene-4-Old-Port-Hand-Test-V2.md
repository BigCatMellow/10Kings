# Prose test

Wurdren made it halfway to the hiring boards before hunger won.

This annoyed him.

The red-cornered notices would remain there for the length of a meal unless the world had become even less patient than he remembered.

He turned into a narrower street behind the warehouse belt, where the festival cloth had not yet reached every doorway and the shops still looked as though they expected customers to know what they sold.

The place he remembered was gone.

In its place stood a repair shop with half a cargo wheel propped against the wall.

Wurdren stared at it.

A woman carrying a tray of bowls stopped beside him.

"You waiting for the other half?"

"The other half of what?"

She nodded toward the wheel.

"I was looking for a cookshop."

"That was two owners ago."

"Of course it was."

She shifted the tray.

"Cookshop moved across the lane."

Wurdren looked.

There was, in fact, a cookshop across the lane.

He decided not to blame Port.

The room was full enough that he took the only open place at a long table near the back.

He had just started eating when somebody behind him said:

"You still sit where you can see the door."

Wurdren stopped.

The voice had changed more than he expected.

He turned.

The person standing there had more gray in their hair than he remembered and a dark work vest with a strip of chalk tucked through one seam. Their left sleeve was rolled to the elbow. The right was not.

Wurdren looked at the vest.

Then at the face.

"You're dressed responsibly."

"So are you."

Wurdren looked down at himself.

"No."

"Compared to before."

That was harder to argue with.

The old acquaintance sat opposite him without asking.

For several moments they looked at one another with the particular suspicion of people checking whether memory had been unfair.

"You got old," the acquaintance said.

"Port did it."

"You haven't been here."

"It works at distance now."

That earned a short laugh.

Wurdren had forgotten the sound.

They had worked together on and off for several seasons when both were younger and less selective about who promised to pay them.

Escort work.

Recovery work.

A winter contract involving stolen pack animals neither of them had understood well enough when they accepted it.

Then the acquaintance had stopped appearing on the road.

Not vanished.

Stopped.

There had been a letter once.

Port.

Steady work.

Staying for a while.

The while had apparently held.

"What are you doing here?" they asked.

"Eating."

"I can see that."

"I've just delivered grain."

They waited.

Wurdren knew that look.

"It was more complicated than it sounds."

"It usually is when you explain it."

"The warehouse was full."

"Festival."

"Then another warehouse counted but did not count."

"Records."

"Then the guarantee counted until it didn't."

"Money."

Wurdren pointed his spoon at them.

"You've become intolerably knowledgeable."

"I work here."

"What do you do?"

"Keep crews from being in the wrong place with the wrong equipment at the wrong hour."

"That sounds invented."

"It pays every week."

That silenced him.

The acquaintance stole a piece of bread from his bowl.

"Quay crews. Warehouse transfer. Repair scheduling when something breaks and everyone decides their problem is the emergency."

"That sounds more familiar."

"It should."

A young worker appeared at the end of the table.

"North lift is still down."

The acquaintance looked at them.

"Pins?"

"Not here."

"Chain?"

"Not the chain."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"The foreman told me to."

"Tell the foreman procurement already has it."

"He said you'd say that."

"Then he has the information."

The young worker grinned and disappeared.

Wurdren watched them go.

"You have people."

"I have problems."

"Same thing, sometimes."

"Usually."

The acquaintance leaned back.

"What are you really doing here?"

Wurdren looked at his bowl.

"Grain."

"That was not the question."

He ate another bite.

Around them, three people at the next table were arguing over whether the festival had raised room prices more this year than seven years ago.

Nobody had numbers.

Everyone had certainty.

A server shouted that the cheap stew was gone.

Someone shouted back that it was not yet properly afternoon.

The server said the stew had not been informed.

Wurdren smiled despite himself.

"I wanted the road again," he said.

The acquaintance waited.

"That simple?" they asked.

"No."

"Good."

Wurdren looked at them.

"You could pretend."

"We spent too many winters together."

He remembered one of them.

He wished he had not.

"I got tired of waiting for the next thing to feel like the thing," he said.

The acquaintance looked down at the bread in their hand.

"I got tired of packing every time a place started to feel ordinary."

Wurdren sat back.

"You hated Port."

"I hated being broke in Port."

"You complained about it constantly."

"I still do."

That sounded more like them.

"You just told a boy his broken lift wasn't your problem."

"I told him it was procurement's problem. That's different."

"Deeply."

The acquaintance ignored that.

"I meant to stay one season. Then somebody asked me to take over a crew for a month. Then I knew which men lied about being able to rig a load and which ones only looked like they lied. Then the ferry people started sending for me when they needed extra hands."

"And then?"

"I had keys to things."

"Keys."

"Several."

"Impressive."

"One opens a shed full of rope."

"Now I envy you."

"You should."

They smiled.

Wurdren did too.

That was the trouble.

Nothing in the life across from him looked like surrender.

The old acquaintance knew who worked which shift. Knew which lift was broken. Knew where to send the boy.

The cookshop owner called them by a shortened version of their not-yet-established name and put another cup on the table without asking.

A woman passing the door leaned in and said:

"Your niece says tonight means tonight."

The acquaintance called back:

"I know what tonight means."

"She says you don't."

"She has inherited slander."

The woman left.

Wurdren raised an eyebrow.

"Family?"

"Unfortunately."

"Your family?"

"Also unfortunately."

That was all the explanation he got.

It was enough to make the room feel suddenly fuller.

Outside, something thumped against the window.

A child's puppet pressed against the glass.

It had a huge painted mouth, webbed hands, and far too many teeth.

The child behind it made the puppet bite the window twice before an adult pulled them away.

Wurdren looked after it.

"What in the hell was that?"

The acquaintance glanced over.

"Netman."

"Should I know that?"

"Only if you're eight."

"Then I am safe."

"Festival story."

The puppet appeared again farther down the window.

Wurdren watched it attack another pane.

"Ugly thing."

"Didn't have teeth like that."

He looked back.

"You saw it?"

"No."

"Then how—"

"Nobody said it had teeth like that until people started making the puppets."

Wurdren waited.

None came.

The acquaintance drank.

He understood the limit and let it lie.

Outside, a crew dragged a length of timber past the window.

The acquaintance watched automatically.

"Wrong street."

"How can you tell?"

"They're going toward the old yard."

"Maybe they want the old yard."

"The old yard hasn't taken timber loads in three years."

"You going to tell them?"

"Somebody will."

The timber stopped.

Voices rose.

The acquaintance sighed and stood.

"I am somebody."

They went outside.

Wurdren stayed at the table.

Through the window he watched them:

- point once;
- listen to the driver;
- send one worker ahead;
- turn the load around without shouting.

No applause.

No danger.

No one drew steel.

The timber went where it was supposed to go.

The acquaintance came back inside.

"Heroic," Wurdren said.

"I hated every moment."

"You looked natural."

"That's age."

They sat.

For reasons Wurdren did not examine too closely, the joke bothered him.

The acquaintance looked toward the street.

"Three Moons is worse this time."

"More people?"

"More everything."

They counted on their fingers.

"Beds. Carts. Temporary crews. Outsiders asking why streets are closed. Outsiders asking why streets aren't wider. Outsiders discovering food costs money."

"Scandal."

"Last week a man told me Port should be grateful for all the business."

"What did you say?"

"I asked if he wanted to pay for the broken quay."

"Did he?"

"No."

"Missed opportunity."

The acquaintance looked toward the window.

"That's the part people don't see. Everybody needs the quay. Nobody wants to be the one paying when it cracks."

Wurdren nodded.

"There's talk about Port keeping more of what passes through."

"There is always talk."

"More now."

"Good talk?"

"Depends which Port you ask."

They said it without drama.

"Some of it is overdue. We need money that stays here long enough to fix things."

"And the rest?"

"The big guild offices want more desks, more shared forms, more permanent people in Port."

"They helped me today."

"Good."

"No argument?"

"Useful is useful."

The acquaintance shrugged.

"I just don't want every quay crew waiting on one office because the office decided it knew better than the people standing on the quay."

That was plain enough.

Wurdren thought of the Records Worker refusing to rewrite someone else's promise.

"Fair."

The acquaintance stole the last piece of his bread.

"Buy your own."

"I live here."

"I don't think that makes it yours."

"It should."

A runner entered the cookshop, looked around, saw the acquaintance, and came over.

"You know him?"

They pointed at Wurdren.

The acquaintance looked at Wurdren.

"I used to."

The runner seemed to decide that was sufficient.

"Shared hall needs a neutral hand."

"Then they should hire one."

"They're trying."

"Why him?"

The runner looked Wurdren over in a way he disliked immediately.

"Someone said he can keep people from making a bad argument worse."

Wurdren turned slowly toward the acquaintance.

They examined the ceiling.

"You said that."

"I may have described you generously."

"What argument?"

"Route people. Longwood delegate. Copy of a corridor agreement. They disagree about what it lets anyone do."

"That sounds like a clerk problem."

"The clerks established that both papers exist."

"Then it sounds solved."

"It is not."

The acquaintance snorted.

The runner continued.

"They don't need a lawyer. They need somebody the room doesn't already hate."

Wurdren considered the hiring boards.

Red-corner work.

North of Port.

Better pay, probably.

Something dangerous enough to sound like the reason he had come back.

He had not even reached the board.

"What do I get?" he asked.

The runner named a modest fee.

The acquaintance said:

"Take it."

Wurdren looked at them.

"You have become very interested in my career."

"No. I want you occupied until my shift ends."

"Why?"

"Then you can carry something home."

"What?"

"My niece bought three festival lanterns."

"Absolutely not."

"You spent years carrying worse."

"Those were weapons."

"Lanterns are lighter."

Wurdren stood.

His knee had stiffened again.

He waited the half second required for it to cooperate.

The acquaintance noticed.

They said nothing.

That was another reason he had missed them.

"Where's the shared hall?" Wurdren asked.

The runner gave directions.

Wurdren knew roughly where it was.

Or where it had been.

He looked at the acquaintance.

"Tonight means tonight?"

"So I've been told."

"Try to remember."

"I have people for that."

Wurdren left them with:

- the broken lift;
- the wrong timber street;
- the niece;
- the crew;
- the keys to a rope shed;
- a city they complained about as though complaining were one of the duties of belonging to it.

He reached the corner and looked back once.

The acquaintance was already talking to the cookshop owner about something that had nothing to do with him.

Wurdren kept walking.

The hiring boards could wait another hour.

Probably.

# V2 assessment

## Port-as-home

**PASS.**

The city has:

- ordinary memory;
- work;
- family;
- repair;
- food;
- children;
- petty annoyance;
- political disagreement.

## Old Port Hand

**PASS.**

The theme is now carried primarily by:

- what they do;
- who needs them;
- what they chose to remain responsible for.

Their explicit life-choice statement is narrower:

> "I got tired of packing every time a place started to feel ordinary."

It describes them.

It does not diagnose Wurdren.

## Port politics

**PASS.**

The political disagreement is now concrete:

- who pays for shared infrastructure;
- whether Port retains resources;
- how much authority permanent guild offices should gain.

No one states the story-level legitimacy thesis.

## Netman

**PASS.**

No expansion required.

**PASS.**
