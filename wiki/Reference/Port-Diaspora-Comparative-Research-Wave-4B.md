# 1. General diaspora rule

A diaspora profile should distinguish:

~~~text
reason for migration
→ first-generation institutions
→ work / housing pattern
→ second-generation adaptation
→ mixed households / new occupations
→ practices that survive
→ practices that disappear
→ practices that become locally Port-born
→ influence traveling back outward
~~~

Do not treat:

> ancestry

as equivalent to:

> current culture.

# 2. D-1 — North Coast-descended Port community

## Comparator mechanism A — Liverpool Irish port migration

### Sources

National Museums Liverpool:

- https://images.liverpoolmuseums.org.uk/2022-05/Liverpool_Irish_Community_Trail_WEB.pdf
- https://images.liverpoolmuseums.org.uk/2020-01/Liverpool-Irish-community-trail.pdf

Historic England:

- https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1392393

### Source-derived mechanisms

The Liverpool material documents:

- repeated migration over a long period rather than one single arrival;
- large migration waves intensifying an already existing community;
- migrants concentrating near:
  - docks;
  - cheap housing;
  - work;
- migrants participating in:
  - dock labor;
  - street trading;
  - industrial work;
- churches / clubs / social institutions becoming community anchors;
- later descendants becoming inseparable from wider city identity.

Historic building evidence also shows port housing changing over time:

- former higher-status houses;
- subdivision into tenements;
- seafarers / migrants / working families sharing dense port housing.

The useful mechanism is:

> **a migrant port neighborhood can begin as a practical labor / housing concentration and become a native urban culture over generations.**

### 10Kings application

D-1 can begin through several North Coast migration streams:

- sailors who remain ashore;
- fishing households displaced by stock decline / storm loss;
- seasonal workers who become permanent;
- ship / repair workers recruited into Port;
- spouses / kin following earlier migrants.

First generation may cluster near:

- harbor work;
- fish markets;
- lodging;
- North Coast shipping houses.

Later generations may work in:

- unrelated dock trades;
- clerical work;
- guilds;
- food selling;
- city services.

By third generation, someone can be:

> unmistakably Port-born

while retaining:

- family food;
- sea-loss customs;
- particular songs;
- North Coast surnames / names;
- kin ties to one island / harbor.

### Important consequence

A North Coast custom may survive in Port **after changing meaning**.

Example mechanism:

- island preservation food becomes cheap dock breakfast;
- mourning / rescue collection becomes neighborhood mutual aid;
- Great Mouth story becomes Port children's / worker lore.

### Do not borrow

- Irish ethnicity;
- Catholic denominational structure;
- famine history;
- sectarian history;
- Liverpool accent;
- exact dock occupations.

# 3. D-2 — High Roads merchant / clerk diaspora

## Comparator — New Julfa / Armenian merchant networks

### Sources

Sebouh David Aslanian, *From the Indian Ocean to the Mediterranean*:

- https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520266872.001.0001
- https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520266872.003.0005

Smithsonian Libraries:

- https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_975674

Encyclopaedia Iranica:

- https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/julfa-v-armenians-in-india/
- https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/julfa-i-safavid-period/

Metropolitan Museum of Art:

- https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2018/armenia/exhibition-galleries

### Source-derived mechanisms

The Julfan material is useful for several specific mechanisms:

- merchant communities can form **circulating networks** across many political jurisdictions;
- trust / cooperation can be maintained through:
  - family firms;
  - customary institutions;
  - correspondence;
  - couriers;
  - community arbitration;
- information itself becomes economically crucial;
- branch communities can retain ties to a nodal center while developing local institutions;
- long-distance trade can change:
  - architecture;
  - art;
  - language;
  - household organization;
- merchant families can establish branches across enormous distances.

The Met material also shows that trade-route contact can create material culture combining inherited and newly encountered forms.

### 10Kings application

D-2 is not simply:

> High Roads merchants who live in Port.

A stronger model is a network of:

- merchant factors;
- clerks;
- interpreters;
- contract specialists;
- carrier brokers;
- family / partnership branches

whose business depends on **information and trust moving between HR-C / route cities and Port**.

First generation may preserve:

- High Roads business language;
- route-house affiliations;
- marriage / family ties.

Second generation may become:

- Port court clerks;
- guild officers;
- mixed merchant-house members;
- people whose best language is Port speech but whose family correspondence remains High Roads-derived.

### Information consequence

This community can become one of the most important non-Council information networks in Port because it receives:

- route prices;
- closures;
- contract disputes;
- caravan arrival / nonarrival information.

Do not turn it into a spy network.

It knows things because that is commercially useful.

### Institutional consequence

Possible Port institutions:

- letter / courier houses;
- trusted witnesses;
- remittance / credit relationships;
- lodging for visiting merchants;
- family / religious charities;
- branch arbitration.

Exact form remains open.

### Do not borrow

- Armenian identity;
- forced deportation history;
- Christian minority institutions;
- specific family-firm structures;
- historical Julfan law.

# 4. D-3 — Low Rivers labor / food-market migration community

## Comparator A — immigrant food as a locally invented urban culture

### Source

Library of Congress research guide:

- https://guides.loc.gov/italian-american-periodicals/further-reading

The cited scholarship on Italian-American foodways emphasizes that immigrant food culture can become:

- a tool of identity;
- a neighborhood economy;
- a second-generation negotiation;
- a locally invented cuisine carrying older traces.

### Source-derived mechanism

The useful mechanism is:

> **diaspora food is not necessarily preserved homeland food.**

It can be created in the destination through:

- new ingredients;
- urban markets;
- work schedules;
- class;
- family identity.

### 10Kings application

A Low Rivers-descended Port neighborhood can create foods that no Low Rivers village eats.

Reasons:

- cheap Port imports;
- shift work;
- crowded kitchens;
- market surplus;
- street selling;
- new customer base.

Some foods later travel **back upriver** and are called "Port food" even though their grandparents were Low Rivers dishes.

## Comparator B — immigrant food businesses / market associations

### Sources

Smithsonian Anacostia Community Museum:

- https://www.si.edu/es/object/archives/components/sova-acma-01-007-22-ref13

Library of Congress foodways research:

- https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2023/10/homegrown-foodways-film-series-2023-mexican-food-in-baltimore-and-new-orleans/

### Source-derived mechanisms

The Smithsonian / LOC materials support:

- immigrant groups entering wholesale / food-market niches;
- family-run businesses;
- merchant / property associations;
- immigrant neighborhoods becoming successive landing places for different populations;
- food businesses acting as:
  - employment;
  - family enterprise;
  - cultural transmission;
  - neighborhood visibility.

### 10Kings application

D-3 may begin through:

- seasonal Low Rivers labor;
- produce sellers;
- mill / food-processing workers;
- warehouse workers;
- displaced tenant households.

Likely economic footholds:

- produce handling;
- cheap prepared food;
- milling / baking;
- dairy / vegetable trade;
- boarding-house cooking.

But second generation should diversify well beyond food.

### Current-story consequence

The Low Rivers abundance crisis can increase migration into Port **at the same time food is cheap**.

That paradox should be visible:

> the market is full; the grower's child leaves for warehouse work.

### Do not borrow

- Italian / Mexican / Korean / Chinese ethnicity;
- modern restaurant model;
- US immigration law;
- specific foods.

# 5. D-4 — Stone Hills skilled-worker diaspora

## Comparator — Cornish mining diaspora

### Sources

UNESCO:

- https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1215
- https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6777

### Source-derived mechanisms

UNESCO explicitly documents:

- international migration of skilled mining populations;
- transfer of:
  - technology;
  - industrial methods;
  - mining culture;
- migrant-descended communities abroad;
- physical / social landscapes reproduced or adapted in new environments.

The important mechanism is:

> **skills move with people, and migrant skill communities can transform the receiving economy.**

### 10Kings application

D-4 can include:

- miners;
- metalworkers;
- inspectors;
- toolmakers;
- engineers / practical technicians where tech level supports;
- guild-trained specialists.

They may settle in:

- Port repair / ship / warehouse districts;
- HR-C;
- major roadheads.

Their influence may appear in:

- workshop practice;
- safety terminology;
- inspection methods;
- guild organization;
- work songs / mutual aid.

### Second-generation consequence

Children of skilled migrants need not remain in the original trade.

Possible paths:

- Port clerk with technical vocabulary from home;
- guild inspector;
- ship repair specialist;
- merchant in metal goods;
- worker organizer.

The diaspora becomes more interesting as the occupational monopoly dissolves.

### Return influence

Skills / practices developed in Port can travel back to Stone Hills through:

- returning workers;
- guild communication;
- apprentices.

Migration is therefore two-way cultural transmission.

### Do not borrow

- Cornish ethnicity;
- engine technology;
- Methodist institutions;
- 19th-century colonial mining expansion.

# 6. General second-generation findings

## A. The second generation is not culturally halfway

A second-generation Port resident may be:

- fluent in Port slang;
- trained in a Port guild;
- married into another migration background;
- still eating family food;
- still visiting kin elsewhere.

That combination is itself a culture.

## B. Work can preserve ancestry — until it does not

First generation:

- North Coast → dock / fish work;
- High Roads → merchant / clerk;
- Low Rivers → market / food / warehouse;
- Stone Hills → skilled repair / metal.

Second / third generation:

- occupations diversify.

If they never do, the diaspora becomes a caste.

Avoid that unless intentionally built.

## C. Institutions survive longer than occupations

A community may stop dominating one job but retain:

- mutual aid;
- religious house;
- club;
- neighborhood feast;
- burial society;
- school;
- family correspondence network.

This can preserve identity after economic assimilation.

## D. Port changes the source region too

Return / circular migration can send outward:

- Port fashions;
- guild practices;
- food;
- language;
- political ideas;
- money / credit;
- marriage ties.

Diaspora should be a **two-way edge**, not a drain from homeland to city.
