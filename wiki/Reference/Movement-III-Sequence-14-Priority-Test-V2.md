# Prose test

The Port pins were finished before the bridge hardware.

Everyone knew because the bridge clerk came in person.

They stood in the workshop office with the old delivery slip in one hand and the new date in the other.

"This was ahead of Port."

The production clerk said:

"Yes."

"So why is Port leaving first?"

"Inspection slot."

"My order needs inspection."

"Different slot."

"Metal is metal."

The worker representative, standing by the door, said:

"No."

Both clerks looked at them.

They shrugged.

"Sorry."

The bridge clerk pointed at the yard.

"My crews are waiting."

The production clerk answered:

"So were theirs."

"Port has other suppliers."

"So does the bridge."

"Not for this work."

"Neither did Port."

The bridge clerk looked at the worker representative.

"You people on strike?"

"Not today."

"Helpful."

The production supervisor appeared from the inner office.

They had heard enough.

"The bridge order moves tomorrow."

"It was tomorrow yesterday."

"Then it has remained consistent."

Nobody laughed.

The supervisor tried again.

"We opened one inspection space for the Port pins because the lift was partially out of service."

"And what moved?"

The worker representative asked it even though they knew.

The supervisor looked at them.

"Mine-haulage repair."

The bridge clerk stared.

"So they moved mine work for Port and bridge work for mine."

"Not exactly."

"That is exactly enough."

The bridge clerk left.

The production clerk looked at the closed door.

"That went well."

The worker representative pointed toward the delay board.

"Four."

The supervisor said:

"I know."

"Five if the bridge moves again."

"I know."

"Good."

The supervisor leaned both hands on the desk.

"We have a meeting tonight."

"I know."

"Then can we avoid having it twice?"

The representative looked through the wall toward the shop floor.

Protected work was running.

Ordinary work was waiting.

Next door, a smaller shop had sent workers home early because expected repair orders had been postponed.

Here, people were staying late.

That was the entire argument in two buildings.

"No," they said.

"We cannot."

---

The meeting hall had been built by workers before anyone currently alive had been born.

It had hosted:

- funerals;
- technical lectures;
- injury collections;
- apprenticeship examinations;
- weddings;
- wage disputes;
- political speeches nobody admitted attending afterward.

Tonight every bench was full.

The town safety inspector sat near the front.

The guild branch had sent a craft officer and a contract clerk.

Several workshop owners attended.

So did workers from:

- priority lines;
- ordinary repair shops;
- nearby smaller workshops.

A woman from the neighboring small shop stood first.

"We lost another day."

One of the larger workshop owners said:

"Because your buyer delayed the order."

"Because their buyer cannot get the material when protected work takes first draw."

"That is not the same."

"It looks the same from home."

Several people agreed.

A skilled worker from the priority line stood.

"I have had full wages for six weeks."

The room changed.

They continued.

"My brother at the fitting shop has had three short weeks."

The small-shop woman nodded.

"So when you say stop the protected contracts, which one of us are you helping?"

Nobody had said stop them.

That did not make the question less useful.

The worker representative stood.

"We are not asking to stop them."

Someone from the back said:

"Some are."

"Then they can say it themselves."

No one did.

The representative continued.

"We are asking:

- how much of the schedule is protected;
- how long that lasts;
- what ordinary work keeps moving;
- what the hours are doing to people."

One owner said:

"You know we cannot publish customer contracts."

"Why not?"

"Confidentiality."

"Convenient."

The guild contract clerk said:

"Also ordinary."

The room disliked them for confirming it.

The representative looked at the clerk.

"Who can check the owner's claim without seeing the customer name?"

The clerk considered that.

"Someone the parties accept."

"Guild?"

"Could be."

"Town?"

"Could be."

"Both?"

"Could be."

The worker representative nodded.

That was enough.

No new institution required.

The safety inspector said:

"I can check hours and stoppages."

"Public?"

"To the shops in this dispute."

One owner said:

"That I can agree to."

The small-shop woman asked:

"And the amount of protected work?"

The guild craft officer said:

"Aggregate share. Not customer."

"By shop?"

"If the owners permit."

The owners looked at one another.

One said:

"For this review."

The representative heard the boundary.

Good.

---

The owners had a real answer.

A workshop owner put production summaries on the table.

Not contracts.

"Ordinary orders are down."

The small-shop woman said:

"Because you move them."

"Some. Not all."

The owner continued.

"External buyers are also delaying ordinary purchases because routes and prices are uncertain. The protected contracts are not merely taking capacity. They are also the orders still arriving on time."

A priority-line worker said:

"If those contracts leave, some of our overtime disappears."

The small-shop woman said:

"Good."

"My wages disappear too."

"Not all of them."

"Enough."

The worker representative let that sit.

Then asked:

"What category is protected?"

The guild clerk answered carefully.

"Several. Major repair. Infrastructure. High-grade retained work. Some security-related contracts."

The last category changed the room.

A priority-line worker asked:

"Security for who?"

"I cannot give customer names."

"Can you tell us how much?"

"Aggregate share, if the owners agree to the review."

That answer was better than no.

Not good.

Better.

---

The compromise came together slowly.

By the time lamps were lit, the parties had four broad terms on the board.

### 1. Safety / hours

For the next short review period:

- owners provide hours / stoppage information to the safety inspector and shop representatives.

### 2. Protected-share verification

A small set of people accepted by:

- owners;
- workers;
- guild / town participants

can see enough underlying records to verify:

- approximate protected share;
- current committed period.

They do not publish customer identity.

### 3. Ordinary repair space

During the review period, shops preserve some scheduling room for urgent civilian repair instead of allowing every ordinary order to move indefinitely.

Exact cases remain shop / local negotiation.

### 4. Reconvene

The parties meet again after the short review period with:

- actual delay;
- safety;
- workload;
- capacity

figures.

No permanent system.

No final settlement.

Enough to avoid immediate stoppage.

Workers would take the package back to their own shop meetings / representatives.

Owners would keep protected work running while those meetings happened.

Nobody called that peace.

It was something.

---

The production supervisor found the worker representative near the door.

"You think they accept it?"

"Some."

"Enough?"

"Maybe."

"Useful."

"Do not start."

The supervisor looked back at the hall.

"I cannot give you the customer names."

"I know."

"If that kills it—"

"It might."

"Then what is the point?"

The representative looked at the board.

"To see whether secrecy about names has to mean secrecy about everything."

The supervisor did not answer.

That was close enough to agreement to be dangerous.

---

The next morning:

- protected work continued;
- ordinary lines continued where they had work;
- the bridge hardware entered inspection;
- the delayed mine-haulage repair remained delayed.

Shop meetings began.

Some workers wanted the package.

Some wanted more.

Some priority-line workers wanted no disruption at all.

Some small-shop workers thought the review came too late.

The worker representative spent the day carrying the same four terms from room to room.

Near the end of the shift, a younger priority-line worker asked:

"If we accept this, do we find out who the security work is for?"

"No."

"Then what do we get?"

"We find out how much of the place it is taking and for how long."

"Same thing?"

"No."

"Feels like it."

The representative understood.

That was why the package might fail even without anything else happening.

"Read it," they said.

"Then decide what you can live with."

The worker took the paper.

The representative watched them go.

By evening, enough shops were still considering the package that another meeting had been scheduled.

Not victory.

Not collapse.

Close enough that new information could matter.
