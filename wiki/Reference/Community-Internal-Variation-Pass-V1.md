# Community Internal Variation Pass — V1

## Status

**Provisional cross-community stress test — not canon.**

This pass operates on the P2-ready community types in:

- [Community-to-Analogue Matrix — V1](Community-to-Analogue-Matrix-V1.md);
- [Community Profiles — P1 Wave 1](Community-Profiles-P1-Wave-1.md);
- [Community Spiderweb Test — P1 Wave 1](Community-Spiderweb-Test-P1-Wave-1.md);
- [Community Hold Resolutions — V1](Community-Hold-Resolutions-V1.md);
- [Mobile Circuits — Working Profiles V1](Mobile-Circuits-Working-Profiles-V1.md).

The purpose is to prevent a successful **community profile** from becoming a smaller version of the old failure:

> one region = one personality.

A community must also vary by:

- generation;
- household position;
- wealth / security;
- work status;
- migration history;
- faith / non-faith affiliation;
- gender where locally relevant;
- age / youth culture;
- institutional membership.

## Evidence rule

This page distinguishes:

- **derived consequence** — follows from current community systems;
- **open variable** — current sources do not justify a specific answer.

Do not silently import real-world gender, marriage, inheritance, or religious arrangements merely because a comparative source supplied another mechanism.

---

# 1. General rule — culture is argued over by the people living it

Every P2 community should support statements like:

> "That's how my grandparents did it."

> "That's how the guild wants it done."

> "That's only true in the old quarter."

> "People with land say that."

> "You can afford to call it tradition."

Those disagreements are evidence of functioning culture.

# 2. SH-1 — industrial mine / civic district

## Generational split — derived

### Older worker / craft generation

May place greater value on:

- mine-specific reputation;
- inherited work custom;
- mutual-aid halls;
- old safety practices;
- long employer / guild memory.

### Younger workers / apprentices

May be more willing to:

- change mines for wages;
- pursue formal credentials;
- follow recruiters;
- question inherited guild hierarchy.

This follows from:

- current strategic demand;
- apprenticeship;
- labor mobility.

It does **not** imply that young = radical or old = conservative.

## Stable vs precarious household — derived

Stable skilled household:

- tools;
- guild ties;
- credit;
- apprentice relationships;
- mutual-aid standing.

Casual / boarding worker:

- less storage;
- greater rent exposure;
- more dependence on daily / short contracts;
- weaker formal voice.

The same wage increase can therefore mean:

- opportunity to one;
- destabilization / rent inflation to another.

## Faith / service layer — derived but plural

Possible local institutions include:

- Eternal Forge apprenticeship / repair institutions;
- Radiant Path charity / moral testimony;
- Harmonious mediation;
- Cyclic Order burial;
- Illuminated technical / educational networks.

No dominant-faith assignment is justified.

## Youth culture — provisional

Likely youth social spaces should arise from:

- apprentice halls;
- work yards;
- cheap food / drink places;
- music / games;
- skill display;
- rumor about better jobs elsewhere.

Need future scene test.

## Gender / inheritance — OPEN

Current research does not justify:

- male mine labor as universal;
- patrilineal tool inheritance;
- gendered guild exclusion.

These must be built from local legal / household history, not imported from Welsh / American mining comparisons.

# 3. NC-1 — outer-island seasonal fishing community

## Generational split — derived

Older residents may remember:

- stronger fish runs;
- different visiting fleets;
- older rescue / access practices.

Younger people may see:

- seasonal work elsewhere;
- larger harbors;
- Port;
- convoy work

as normal parts of island life.

Fish decline can create a real argument over:

> staying, adapting, or leaving.

## Resident vs seasonal identity — derived

A person can be:

- island-born resident;
- recurring seasonal fisher with decades of ties;
- first-time seasonal worker;
- person who married into the island;
- resident who does not fish.

Those categories should matter differently depending on:

- fishery rights;
- rescue obligations;
- lodging;
- local politics.

## Faith layer — OPEN / plural

Coastal Great Weaver interpretations are plausible for some households.

They are **not** the default island religion.

Other faith networks can supply:

- burial;
- traveler lodging;
- charity;
- schooling.

## Youth culture — derived candidate

Likely pressure points:

- first season at sea;
- crew reputation;
- learning weather / route lore;
- boredom in closed-weather periods;
- attraction of major harbor / Port culture.

A young islander can simultaneously:

- mock Great Mouth stories;
- know exactly which version is "wrong."

## Gender / inheritance — OPEN

Need later work on:

- boat / fishery-right inheritance;
- shore-processing ownership;
- widow / survivor claims.

Do not default to historical North Atlantic gender patterns.

# 4. NC-3 — major compact harbor

## Generational split — derived

### Older harbor families

May defend:

- local quay rights;
- older harbor autonomy;
- rescue / pilot traditions.

### Younger merchant / clerk / crew population

May find:

- centralized records;
- convoy standards;
- reciprocal credentials

more normal / useful.

But young localists and old centralizers should also exist.

## Class / occupation variation — very strong

At minimum distinguish:

- merchant / warehouse households;
- shipowners;
- skilled repair workers;
- crews;
- casual / seasonal labor;
- pilots / harbor officials.

The "North Coast harbor position" on any issue is therefore suspect.

## Diaspora / outsider layer — derived

Long-distance trade supports:

- merchant houses with outside origins;
- visiting crews;
- people settled for generations whose grandparents were foreigners.

This is a candidate for later diaspora profiles.

## Myth variation

Great Mouth changes by class:

- crews → occupational / ecological memory;
- merchants → market / risk story;
- naturalists / clerks → recorded object;
- children → spectacle.

Same harbor, different legend.

## Household / gender rules — OPEN

Need later legal history.

# 5. LR-1 — cooperative irrigation village

## Generational split — derived

Older users may remember:

- old channels;
- former allocation rules;
- famine / drought responses.

Younger households may press for:

- new crops;
- new markets;
- changed maintenance burden;
- different use of surplus.

The conflict is not tradition vs progress by default.

It can be:

> which inherited rule still solves the actual water problem?

## Household-position split — derived

A household with:

- many working-age members;
- large fields;
- animals;

experiences communal maintenance differently from:

- widow / single-adult household;
- elderly household;
- tenant household;
- labor-poor household.

Therefore equal labor obligation can be **formally equal but materially unequal**.

## Wealth / user-status split — derived

Questions:

- does land size determine water share?
- does tenancy carry direct voice?
- who represents rented land?
- can an estate dominate a water group through many plots?

These remain local variables.

## Faith layer

Water maintenance can coexist with religious observance, but no water cult should be inferred from the comparative sources.

Faith institutions may provide:

- meals;
- mediation;
- calendar coordination.

## Youth culture — provisional

Potential:

- maintenance work as first adult responsibility;
- swimming / river risk;
- market-town attraction;
- arguments about leaving farming.

Needs later scene test.

## Gender / inheritance — OPEN

Especially important because:

- water right;
- field right;
- household labor

may transfer differently.

Do not infer from Balinese / Andean comparison.

# 6. LR-4 — lower-G1 floodplain community

## Generational split — derived

Older residents may possess strong memory of:

- old channels;
- high-water years;
- embankment failures;
- places where land used to be water.

Younger residents may be more willing to favor:

- drainage;
- road stability;
- stronger embankments;
- Port-facing market intensification.

Or the reverse.

The important distinction is exposure, not age stereotype.

## Livelihood split — strong

People who benefit from seasonal water:

- fishers;
- wetland-product workers;
- flood-fertility farmers

can oppose people who benefit from:

- dry fields;
- permanent roads;
- drainage.

This is a genuine intra-community political axis.

## Myth variation

Marsh Lure changes by age / work:

- children → intentional luring being;
- boat / flood workers → practical warning language;
- older storytellers → named old sightings;
- young skeptics → natural light explanation while still following route rules.

## Faith / household / gender — OPEN

Need local population history before specifying.

# 7. LW-1 — western Longwood compact community

## Generational split — derived

Older local-right households may remember:

- weaker compact authority;
- smaller roads;
- fewer external contracts.

Younger people may have grown up with:

- wage logging;
- market work;
- migrant coworkers;
- compact bureaucracy.

This can produce disagreement over what is "traditional Longwood."

## Local vs migrant split — derived

Migrant worker is not automatically:

- outsider enemy;
- anti-forest;
- politically voiceless.

Questions:

- when does repeated residence create use rights?
- can a logging worker's household join the local user community?
- what about children born there?

This is now an important membership issue.

## Commercial split — strong

At least:

- local forest enterprise;
- wage workers;
- outside concession;
- town merchants;
- rights-holding rural communities.

All can support commerce while disagreeing about authority.

## Myth variation

Forest Owner can become:

- rights rhetoric;
- worker joke;
- spiritual belief;
- children's warning;
- outsider caricature.

A local timber cooperative can invoke it just as sincerely as a conservation faction.

## Faith / gender / inheritance — OPEN

Great Weaver influence is plausible but not universal.

Need later legal / kin pass.

# 8. LW-2 — interior use-right community

## Generational split — derived

Potential disputes:

- younger people wanting market income from rights;
- older users defending restricted / seasonal practice;
- older reformers and younger traditionalists also possible.

Do not map age directly to ideology.

## Membership variation — central

Potential statuses:

- full resident user;
- seasonal user;
- married-out kin;
- married-in newcomer;
- fostered / adopted household member;
- recent resident;
- specialist with recognized nonresident use;
- traveler with passage only.

This is one of the richest current community variables.

## Household power — derived question

Because rights may attach to:

- household;
- person;
- community membership;
- use category,

the setting must eventually decide:

> who speaks for a household's right?

Do not assume household head = oldest male / landowner.

## Myth variation

Forest Owner stories can expose social position.

Someone denied membership may tell a version in which:

> the Forest Owner recognizes people the council does not.

That is an important possibility.

## Faith — plural / open

Great Weaver gives one interpretive language.

Other faiths may coexist and reinterpret the same rights.

# 9. OC-1 — upstream civic-water community

## Generational split — derived

Older technical / civic elites may defend:

- precedent;
- existing shares;
- old maintenance rules.

Younger reformers may demand:

- more accessible records;
- altered drought priorities.

But younger estate heirs may defend privilege more strongly than older technical officials.

Avoid simple age politics.

## Technical-status split — strong

Distinguish:

- water engineers / measurers;
- clerks / recordkeepers;
- estate holders;
- neighborhood users;
- political officeholders.

Technical authority can clash with elected / inherited political authority.

## Class split

Private storage / gardens create different lived water politics from:

- public basin dependence;
- rented rooms;
- craft water needs.

## Myth variation

Charter Beast:

- jurist → symbol;
- child → creature;
- household → protective mark;
- reformer → reminder that records bind rulers too.

## Faith / gender — OPEN

No Old Cities-wide answer is justified.

# 10. OC-2 — downstream canal-neighborhood culture

## Generational split — derived

People with living memory of:

- severe shortages;
- gate disputes

may react differently from people raised during stable cooperation.

This is **event-generation**, not simple age.

A drought cohort can become politically distinctive.

## Neighborhood split — strong

Head-of-channel vs tail-end neighborhood experience can differ dramatically.

Even inside downstream OC-D:

- one neighborhood receives reliable flow;
- another gets whatever remains.

This creates local politics below city-state rivalry.

## Work / class split

Water-intensive crafts may have interests different from:

- household users;
- market gardeners;
- estates.

## Myth variation

Charter Beast becomes sharper at the tail end of systems.

A wealthy central neighborhood may treat it as quaint legal art.

A vulnerable edge neighborhood may tell active stolen-water stories.

## Faith / gender / inheritance — OPEN

Need later city-specific work.

# 11. P-1 — old Port harbor-worker neighborhood

## Generational split — exceptionally strong

### Older Port labor households

May remember:

- earlier hiring practices;
- major fires;
- older guild boundaries;
- prior migration wave;
- waterfront before one rebuild.

### Younger workers

May see:

- reciprocal credentials;
- mixed households;
- new food / music;
- permanent crowding

as simply Port.

This can create different definitions of:

> real Port culture.

## Stable vs casual work — strong

Stable crew / credentialed worker:

- more predictable income;
- institutional voice;
- injury support.

Casual worker:

- hiring uncertainty;
- weak storage / housing;
- greater vulnerability to formal credentialing.

Therefore "labor reform" can split labor.

## Migrant generation — central

Distinguish:

- newly arrived;
- Port-raised child of migrants;
- grandparent who migrated;
- old family with mixed ancestry.

A Port-born person may defend a food / song / custom whose origin their grandparent still calls foreign.

## Faith / service layer

Port's plural faith networks can overlap around:

- burial;
- food relief;
- lodging;
- mediation;
- schools.

Mixed households may use different institutions for different needs.

No "Port religion."

## Youth culture — very important

Likely develops fastest around:

- docks;
- apprenticeships;
- cheap food;
- music;
- festival work;
- borrowed slang;
- clothing alteration.

Port should probably become the first place where a youth culture pass is later made concrete.

## Myth variation

Estuary Captive:

- child → festival figure;
- old harbor family → moral memory;
- legal worker → category problem;
- naturalist → zoological puzzle;
- activist → political analogy;
- skeptic → embarrassing old story.

All within Port.

## Gender / inheritance — OPEN

Need explicit Port law / household study.

- route memory;

Older carriers may rely heavily on:

- remembered route relationships;
- personal trust.

Younger operators may benefit more from:

Possible:

- major animal / wagon owners;

# 15. Cross-community conclusions

## A. Generation should follow historical experience, not age stereotype

Better:

> people who remember the Blight  
> people raised after harbor reform  
> first generation under guild reciprocity  
> flood cohort  
> migration generation

than:

> old = traditional, young = progressive.

## B. Household position is often more important than gender in current evidence

Current sources strongly support:

- apprentice;
- boarder;
- tenant;
- casual worker;
- user;
- migrant;
- widow / survivor;
- settled / traveling member.

They do **not yet support** region-wide gender rules.

Therefore gender should be built later through:

- property;
- inheritance;
- work;
- religion;
- law;

not pasted in as cultural color.

## C. Faith should enter through institutions before demographic labels

Ask:

- who buries?
- who teaches?
- who feeds?
- who mediates?
- who hosts travelers?

Then derive the local religious ecology.

Do not assign:

> "this is a Great Weaver village"

unless history earns it.

## D. Youth culture is the largest remaining everyday-life gap

The culture work now has:

- households;
- labor;
- institutions;
- food;
- architecture;
- festivals;
- myths.

But young people still too often appear only as:

- children;
- apprentices;
- future adults.

A later pass should specifically build:

- games;
- slang;
- courtship;
- music;
- risky behavior;
- clothing modification;
- first-work status;
- peer groups;
- mobility.

## E. Myth varies inside cultures as much as between them

This should become a permanent creature-design rule.

For any major legend, test:

- child version;
- worker version;
- scholar / clerk version;
- religious version;
- skeptical version;
- elite / commercial version;
- migrant version.

"One culture's version" is still too broad.
