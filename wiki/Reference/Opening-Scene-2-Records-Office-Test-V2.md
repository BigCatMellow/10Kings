# Prose test

The shared records office was not where Wurdren remembered anything being.

That was becoming a theme.

The grain agent led them through the warehouse belt, across a lane where carts were being unloaded directly into temporary sheds, and into a stone building with three open doors and twice as many people trying to use them.

A board beside the entrance listed services.

Wurdren read the first four lines and stopped.

"Which one are we?"

"Warehouse transfer."

"That sounds promising."

She pointed lower.

"Recognized warehouse transfer."

"Less promising."

She did not answer. She was already looking at the queue.

Inside, the room was long, bright, and loud in the way only a place devoted to paperwork could be. Not shouting. Questions. The same questions, mostly, repeated by people who seemed convinced volume might alter the answer.

Clerks worked behind a waist-high counter. Shelves behind them held bound books, rolled documents, trays of seals, and wooden markers cut with symbols Wurdren did not recognize. A conversion table had been painted directly onto one wall. Someone had pasted a newer strip of figures over part of it.

The grain agent joined a queue and opened her folio.

She checked the warehouse refusal they did not yet have.

Then she remembered that and shut the folio again.

Across the lane, under another awning, several guild notices had been pinned to a broad board.

Wurdren could not read them from here.

One had a red corner.

He found himself wondering what red meant now.

When he had last spent any time in Port, a red corner had meant dangerous work or urgent work, depending on who posted it.

Sometimes both.

"You're leaning," the grain agent said.

"I'm standing."

"Toward the door."

"I have always had a directional quality."

She looked back at her folio.

"You can leave."

He glanced at her.

"You brought the cargo here," she said. "That was the contract."

It was not accusation.

That made it worse.

"I know."

The line moved.

He stayed.

When their turn came, the worker behind the counter finished the line they were writing before looking up.

"Documents."

The grain agent handed over the warehouse contract.

The worker read it.

"Original."

Another sheet.

"Reservation."

Another.

"Buyer's terms."

Another.

The worker looked at the small stack.

"Release from the named warehouse."

"They refused us."

"I need the refusal."

"They told us to come here."

"I need the warehouse to release the reservation under its mark."

The grain agent stared at them.

"They have no room."

"Then 'full' is sufficient."

"Why isn't me standing here telling you they have no room sufficient?"

"Because you are asking me to alter the custody record. The warehouse has to say it is no longer accepting the custody."

The agent looked down at the papers.

"So I go back for one word."

"One word, their mark, and the date."

"Meanwhile the boat is paying to sit there."

The worker nodded once.

"Yes."

There was no apology in it and no pleasure either.

Wurdren leaned an elbow on the counter.

"What does that paper buy us when I bring it back?"

The worker looked at him.

"It lets me check whether the alternate warehouse is recognized for this cargo under the reciprocal schedule."

"And if it is?"

"I can certify the warehouse change."

"Then we're done."

"No."

The grain agent closed her eyes.

Wurdren took his elbow off the counter.

"What remains?"

The worker turned the buyer's terms toward them and put one finger beside a line.

"The guarantee names a custody chain. You are changing the chain."

"The grain doesn't change," the agent said.

"The promise does."

She looked up.

"By how much?"

"The amount is not the issue."

"Everything becomes an amount eventually."

For the first time the worker's expression changed.

Not amusement. Recognition.

"The guarantor decides whether the new custody is close enough to what they agreed to cover."

"So we have two problems."

"I can reduce one."

Wurdren nodded toward the door.

"What exactly do I ask the warehouse for?"

"A dated release under their mark. 'Full' is enough. Do not let them give you an unsigned note from a runner."

"Specific."

"It saves return trips."

The grain agent looked at Wurdren.

"I'm coming."

"No. Keep the line."

"If you come back without the date, every minute after that costs me."

"I have recently become aware of dates."

"Today would be a good one to remember."

That was better. It sounded like her.

The worker had already reached for the next person's papers.

"Use the left door when you return," they said.

"Why?" the grain agent asked.

"I've opened the transfer record."

"We haven't transferred anything."

"Correct."

The next person stepped forward.

Wurdren left before the distinction multiplied.

Outside, the red-cornered notice across the lane caught his eye again.

He could read part of it now.

Escort.

North—

A cart rolled between him and the board.

By the time it passed, he was already walking toward the quay.

The warehouse runner was exactly where they had left him and looked disappointed to see Wurdren return.

"What now?"

"I need you to write down the thing you told us."

"No room."

"That thing."

"I told you."

"And I believed you. The records office has higher standards."

The runner looked toward the waiting boats.

"I don't have time."

"Neither does the woman paying for that boat to remain tied up."

"That isn't my fault."

"No."

The runner had been prepared for the argument.

Agreement left him with nowhere obvious to put his annoyance.

Wurdren held out the paper.

"It's not yours. It's not hers. Write 'full.' Put your mark on it. Then I become somebody else's problem."

The runner took the sheet.

He wrote one word and pressed the warehouse mark beside it.

Wurdren looked at the page.

The runner followed his eyes.

"No."

"Date."

"You people—"

"I am becoming one of them by exposure."

The date was added.

Wurdren took the paper.

"Thank you."

"I didn't do it for you."

"That helps."

He went.

When he returned to the records office, the grain agent was no longer at the main counter. A clerk at the entrance pointed him through the left door.

The Records Worker sat at a narrower desk with the original papers spread in front of them.

Wurdren put the release beside the reservation.

They checked:

- mark;
- date;
- cargo;
- warehouse.

Then they opened a bound schedule and ran one finger down a column.

"South Transfer is recognized for grain weights and storage records under the current reciprocal schedule."

The grain agent exhaled.

"So that part is done."

"That part is done."

It was the closest thing to celebration the Records Worker seemed prepared to offer.

They copied the new warehouse mark onto a transfer sheet.

The grain agent watched every stroke.

"Can the guarantor still refuse?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

The worker looked back at the buyer's terms.

"Because other people act on their promise. If somebody else can rewrite the promise for them, it stops being much of a promise."

That answer did not make the agent happier.

It did stop the next question.

Wurdren looked at the shelves behind the worker.

Every book on them represented people trusting marks made by people they had never met.

He had spent much of his life thinking paperwork was the thing that happened after real work.

That belief had never survived much contact with clerks.

"Who do we need?" he asked.

The worker named a commercial house with an office near the inner transfer yards.

The grain agent's hand stopped over the folio.

"Their fees."

"You know them," Wurdren said.

"I know what they cost."

The worker arranged the documents into two sets.

"One copy to South Transfer. One to the guarantor. Keep your originals."

A second clerk leaned through the doorway.

"Your sister sent again."

The Records Worker looked up.

"Tell her not to wait."

"She said you'd say that."

A pause.

"Then she has the message."

The second clerk disappeared.

The Records Worker pushed the copies across the desk.

"Do not let either office keep your only copy."

The grain agent took them.

"Thank you."

"Not yet."

They had already reached for the next ledger.

Outside, the hiring board remained across the lane.

The red-cornered notice was visible again.

Wurdren read enough this time to understand that somebody wanted armed escort work north of Port.

Better pay than grain.

Probably more interesting.

The grain agent was beside him, fitting the new copies into the folio.

"If the guarantor says no, I pay another move and another day," she said. "If they say yes before South Transfer closes, I can still make settlement."

She looked toward the inner yards.

"Which way?"

Wurdren looked once at the red corner.

Then toward the guarantor's office.

"That way."

They went.

# V2 assessment

## Voice separation

### Wurdren

More clearly:

- social;
- self-deflective;
- willing to use humor to move people through irritation.

### Grain Agent

More clearly:

- cost-sensitive;
- deadline-sensitive;
- practical.

Her strongest line is not a joke:

> "Everything becomes an amount eventually."

### Records Worker

More clearly:

- categorical;
- procedural;
- minimally performative.

Their competence now carries more of the character than dry banter.

## Causality

Unchanged from V1.

**PASS.**

## Information budget

Still limited to:

- warehouse release;
- reciprocal recognition;
- guarantor authority.

**PASS.**
