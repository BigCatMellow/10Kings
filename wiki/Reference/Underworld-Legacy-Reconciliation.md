# Underworld Legacy Reconciliation

## Status

**Active reference / preservation layer — named factions are provisional unless promoted elsewhere.**

Source:

- [Volume 08 — Power: Council, Guilds & Underworld](../../legacy-notes/2026-09-22/consolidated-package/08_Power_The_Council_Guilds_Underworld.md)

Current owner:

- [Crime and the Underworld](../Politics/Crime-and-Underworld.md)

The legacy underworld material contains many named gangs and syndicates. Most should not be promoted literally as written, because they were designed around obsolete regional stereotypes, stronger magic, and game-like faction symmetry.

The useful material is:

- scale;
- cell structure;
- ordinary fronts;
- specialist criminal markets;
- illicit logistics;
- document fraud;
- information brokerage;
- racketeering;
- local adaptation;
- faction growth / fragmentation;
- criminal groups providing some services while harming victims;
- recurring networks whose local branches behave differently.

---

# 1. Strong structural findings

## Criminal geography follows opportunity

Crime should grow around:

- customs gaps;
- disputed jurisdiction;
- valuable bottlenecks;
- scarcity;
- high taxes;
- illegal demand;
- labor coercion;
- weak enforcement;
- corruption;
- mobile populations;
- trade routes.

A gang should not exist merely because a region "needs a gang."

## Networks can be cellular

The legacy **Black Tapestry** and later mini-faction models both use a useful structure:

```text
shared name / reputation
+ independent local cells
+ coded or personal links
+ occasional cooperation
→ network without perfect central control
```

This is stronger than one continent-wide criminal corporation.

Cells can:

- disagree;
- defect;
- impersonate one another;
- use the name without permission;
- share fences / brokers but not leadership.

## Ordinary businesses make good fronts

The **Tallow Runners** are a particularly useful legacy example.

Their core idea is not the old drug / alchemical candle details.

It is:

> a mundane mobile trade can provide legitimate reasons to move goods, enter households and institutions, cross borders, and maintain storage.

Other criminal fronts could arise from:

- carts;
- repair;
- food;
- livestock;
- freight;
- secondhand goods;
- lodging;
- entertainment;
- messenger work.

The front must make economic sense even without crime.

---

# 2. Criminal organizations need an economy

A group should have identifiable:

- revenue;
- expenses;
- suppliers;
- customers;
- storage;
- transport;
- bribery costs;
- enforcement;
- laundering / reinvestment;
- information sources.

If a syndicate has dozens of members, ask how they are paid.

If it controls territory, ask what revenue makes that worthwhile.

If it survives arrests, ask what replaces lost people and money.

---

# 3. Smuggling is logistics

Useful legacy smuggling mechanisms include:

- false manifests;
- forged seals;
- hidden cargo;
- corrupt inspectors;
- legitimate mixed cargo;
- transshipment;
- small loads through repeated routes;
- warehouse substitution;
- cross-border handoffs;
- exploiting Underpass or road knowledge.

Smuggling should depend on the same physical world as legal trade.

It needs:

- carriers;
- storage;
- trusted contacts;
- timing;
- market demand.

---

# 4. Information can be criminal merchandise

Legacy factions repeatedly trade in:

- blackmail;
- forged records;
- secrets;
- false credentials;
- coded communication;
- stolen guild knowledge;
- political intelligence.

This is worth retaining.

Information crime can be especially important because current Council / guild systems rely heavily on:

- contracts;
- records;
- certification;
- route knowledge;
- credit reputation.

A forged document can sometimes matter more than a stolen sword.

---

# 5. Crime can imitate legitimate institutions

Some underworld groups may provide:

- loans;
- protection;
- arbitration;
- work;
- transport;
- document help;
- refugee movement;
- access to markets;
- dispute enforcement.

This does not make them benevolent.

It explains why communities may tolerate them.

A group can simultaneously:

- help one household;
- exploit another;
- punish witnesses;
- provide jobs;
- extort businesses.

Keep victims visible.

---

# 6. Conflict brokerage

The legacy **Ebony Doves** contain a strong mechanism:

> intermediaries claim to solve disputes while quietly benefiting when disputes continue.

This can take the form of:

- information brokers;
- unofficial mediators;
- fixers;
- security providers;
- lenders;
- smugglers.

They need not literally manufacture every conflict.

The interesting version is that their livelihood creates an incentive **not to solve problems permanently**.

This is compatible with the wider world's interest in intermediaries and dependency.

---

# 7. Forgery and specialized crime

The **Painted Moon** has a useful non-magical core:

- dyes;
- pigments;
- high-quality inks;
- official marks;
- copied seals;
- art forgery;
- coded images.

That creates a believable specialist criminal network tied to:

- artists;
- scribes;
- merchants;
- customs;
- guild certification.

The old region-by-region magical pigment variants should remain demoted.

---

# 8. Relic and artifact trafficking

The **Broken Sigil** and **Obsidian League** both contain versions of this idea.

The useful form is:

- stolen religious objects;
- forged relics;
- fake provenance;
- illegal excavation;
- private collectors;
- disputed sacred ownership.

This now connects well to [Religious Artifacts Framework](Religious-Artifacts-Framework.md).

Do not promote:

- storm-control scrolls;
- magical-creature trafficking;
- powerful cursed weapons

unless later magic rules establish them.

Forgery may be more common than genuine magic.

---

# 9. Ideological crime

The **Indigo Vow** is worth preserving as a seed because it is not motivated only by profit.

Its useful mechanism:

- members justify sabotage / coercion as morally necessary;
- local cells select targets differently;
- outsiders disagree whether they are criminals, vigilantes, terrorists, reformers or cultists.

Do not establish the "Indigo Dark" theology yet.

The concept should be reconciled with:

- religion;
- political movements;
- audience movements;
- local law.

# 10. Named faction seed bank

The following legacy names remain available as **provisional quarry**.

## Stronger seeds

### Black Tapestry

Useful core:

- loose cells;
- smuggling;
- document fraud;
- information;
- Port / cross-route logistics.

Do not assume one mastermind or total Underpass control.

### Painted Moon

Useful core:

- pigments / inks;
- art forgery;
- copied marks;
- coded visual communication.

### Tallow Runners

Useful core:

- mobile ordinary trade used as smuggling infrastructure;
- loose traveling crews;
- inns / estates / religious houses as recurring customers or contacts.

### Ebony Doves

Useful core:

- information brokerage;
- mediation-for-profit;
- conflict dependency.

### Harbor Rats

Useful core:

- small Port waterfront youth / neighborhood gang;
- petty theft;
- low-level smuggling.

The name is somewhat generic and should pass later naming review.

## Conditional seeds

### Broken Sigil

Useful if relic / magic black markets are later established.

### Obsidian League

Useful as:

- illicit antiquities / relic trafficking;
- forgery;
- specialist appraisal.

The high-magic creature material should not survive automatically.

### Indigo Vow

Useful as a clandestine ideological / vigilante network, but overlaps with political / religious movement design.

### Ash Fang Brotherhood

Useful core:

- arms diversion;
- illicit mercenary work;
- enforcement for monopolies.

The militarized fantasy-brotherhood presentation is weaker.

### Velvet Blade

Mostly a generic elite-assassin network.

Do not promote without a much more specific institutional reason to exist.

# 11. Legacy faction material to demote

The weakest patterns in Volume 08 are:

- one themed criminal faction per region;
- gangs that perfectly embody regional stereotypes;
- names based on frost / ember / verdant / shadow merely to signal geography;
- a single global syndicate controlling all crime;
- professional assassin guilds as normal legal institutions;
- magical contraband everywhere;
- criminals possessing unexplained technology;
- every gang having a costume, symbol, named boss and ritual because the template demands it.

A real criminal ecology should contain many unnamed, temporary and boring operations.

Not every smuggling ring needs branding.

---

# 12. Local branches should mutate

The legacy mini-faction idea is useful when detached from regional caricature.

A shared network can develop branches that differ because of:

- local contraband;
- law;
- language;
- available routes;
- relationships with officials;
- recruitment;
- competing groups.

Over time two branches with the same name may have almost nothing in common.

This fits [Culture Phase 7 — Cross-Regional Cultural Transmission](Culture-Phase-7-Cross-Regional-Transmission.md).

Criminal culture also travels and mutates.

---

# 13. Relationship to the Council

The Council may interact with criminal networks through:

- deniable transport;
- information purchases;
- informal credit;
- stolen documents;
- intermediaries;
- quiet toleration.

But this should create risk.

Criminal partners can:

- blackmail Council clients;
- sell information twice;
- steal diverted goods;
- expose operations;
- pursue their own politics.

The Council should not secretly control the whole underworld.

---

# 14. Relationship to guilds

Crime often exists at guild boundaries.

Possible pressure points:

- stolen member marks;
- fake certification;
- diverted inventory;
- unlicensed labor;
- protection rackets;
- counterfeit products;
- secret subcontracting;
- guild leaders accepting bribes.

Guild enforcement can also create black markets by excluding people who still have skills or demand.

This creates a useful legitimate / illegitimate continuum.

---

# 15. Underworld scene test

For a criminal group, answer:

1. What does it sell?
2. Who buys?
3. Why is the legal alternative unavailable / expensive / forbidden?
4. What legitimate infrastructure does it depend on?
5. How does money move?
6. Who stores the goods?
7. Who is harmed?
8. Who receives a real benefit?
9. Which official is bribed, fooled or overwhelmed?
10. What happens when a member is arrested?
11. What prevents a rival from copying the business?
12. Does the group need a name?
13. Does it need central leadership?
14. Which route or jurisdiction gives it an advantage?
15. What would make the criminal market disappear?

If the answer is only "they are the local gang," redesign it.
