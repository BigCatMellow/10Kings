# Prose test

By the time Port came into view, Wurdren had spent three days guarding grain and trying not to think that this was what his return to adventure had amounted to.

The sacks had ridden under tarred cloth in the belly of the riverboat while he watched the banks, counted the crew, checked the lashings, and kept an eye on the leather folio the grain agent carried everywhere with her. Nothing had happened.

Nothing happening was, professionally, the point.

It did not feel like much of a beginning.

The river widened into the estuary. Warehouses appeared first—long roofs along the quays, timber stages over the water, lifting frames above them. Then came ferries, road traffic, repair yards, and the crowded rise of the city beyond. Masts stood behind the nearer roofs in numbers Wurdren did not remember.

Port had grown.

Or perhaps he had shrunk.

He stood.

His left knee had stiffened during the morning. He waited for it to unlock before taking his full weight, turning the pause into a look toward the harbor. A young deckhand noticed and shifted as if to help.

Wurdren gave him a look.

The boy found something urgent to do with a rope.

That was better.

Three Moons had not properly begun, but Port was already straining under it. Boats waited off the river quays. Temporary awnings covered stretches of open ground. Workers carried benches in one direction while another crew carried nearly identical benches in the other. Someone on a roof was still hanging festival cloth. Someone below was explaining, at considerable volume, that it was crooked.

At the quay ahead, a board divided incoming cargo among several receiving lanes. One direction had been painted over and replaced.

The boatmaster read it and swore.

"Festival," he said.

Wurdren had heard the word used to explain missing beds, higher meals, delayed departures, and a stolen harness since entering the lower river.

It was proving useful.

The grain agent came up beside him with her folio tucked under one arm.

"Same warehouse," she said.

Wurdren nodded.

"You remember where it is?"

"I remember where it was."

She looked at the new sheds along the water.

"That's not the same answer."

"No."

They watched the city for another moment.

The job had been respectable enough when he took it at the upper-river market. A small seller had grain bound for Port and wanted another experienced hand for the final leg. Three Moons traffic meant more strangers around cargo, more confusion at transfer points, and more opportunities for papers or goods to disappear.

Wurdren wanted money.

More than that, he wanted the road again.

The first part he could explain easily.

The second had been harder.

Three days later, after guarding sacks and papers while the river carried them steadily toward Port, he had begun to suspect the road did not care why he had come back to it.

The grain agent opened the folio and checked a page she had already checked twice that morning.

"How many sacks?" Wurdren asked.

She looked up.

"You signed the count."

"I know."

"You made us count them twice."

"That was prudence."

"And now?"

"I was checking your memory."

Her eyes narrowed.

He smiled.

"Approximately is a dangerous word around grain," she said.

That one he allowed her.

They reached the quay shortly after midday.

First they waited for room to tie.

Then they waited for two carts to clear the landing.

A quay worker took one look at their warehouse mark and sent them to a different receiving lane.

The grain agent showed him the paper.

He studied it again.

"The mark's right," he said.

"So this is the right lane."

"No. That changed this morning."

"What changed?"

He gestured toward the boats behind them.

They untied, moved scarcely the length of a house, and tied again.

Wurdren remembered when the river side of Port had been easier to understand.

He also remembered that people had complained about it then.

That made him feel somewhat better.

At the next landing, a warehouse runner took the top sheet from the grain agent's folio. He examined the seal, glanced at the boat, and gave the paper back.

"No room."

"We reserved room."

"You did."

"Then we have room."

"You reserved it there."

He tapped the warehouse mark.

"Yes."

"There's none there now."

The grain agent breathed through her nose.

"We were confirmed yesterday."

"Yesterday had room."

Wurdren looked away before either of them could see him smile.

The agent pulled out another page.

"Then use overflow."

"Not under these terms."

"Why?"

The runner pointed to a line.

"Your buyer's guarantee expects recognized storage and receipt. Yard overflow won't give you that. South transfer might have space, but their measures and records aren't covered by this as written."

"They're a warehouse."

"I didn't say they weren't."

"They store grain."

"I expect they do."

"Then what exactly is the problem?"

"The part where the people paying you need to trust a different warehouse as much as the one named here."

That stopped her.

Behind Wurdren, laborers had already begun preparing to lift the first sack.

The runner saw them.

"Don't unload."

The sack settled back onto the boat.

The grain agent stared at the papers.

"Who can change it?"

"Shared records office can tell you whether South transfer's recognized under the new reciprocal rules. If it is, you'll still need the buyer or guarantor to accept the change."

"Where?"

The runner gave directions.

The agent looked at Wurdren.

His part of the job was, by any narrow reading, nearly finished.

He had brought her to Port.

The cargo was intact.

The papers were intact.

The warehouse problem belonged to warehouses.

Somewhere inland were the hiring rooms and contract boards he had been thinking about since he took this job. Three Moons would have drawn work from half the continent. There would be something better than this. Something that did not end with a receipt.

The thought embarrassed him as soon as he had it.

The grain agent was doing figures in the margin of one page.

"If we wait here?" Wurdren asked.

"Berth fee."

"If you move?"

"Another fee."

"If the grain doesn't go into storage today?"

She stopped writing.

"I sell badly, pay badly, or carry it back upriver."

"Can you afford that?"

"No."

There was no hesitation.

The answer made the question seem foolish.

Behind them, someone on another boat began shouting that they had been waiting longer. A quay worker shouted back that everyone had been waiting longer.

Wurdren looked toward the streets beyond the warehouse belt.

He had come back to the road because he was tired of feeling that the years behind him contained nothing he could point to.

That remained true whether or not this particular problem was interesting.

"Where did you say that office was?" he asked the runner.

The runner repeated the directions.

"Far?"

"Depends on the queue."

Wurdren stepped onto the quay and held out a hand for the grain agent's folio.

She did not give it to him.

"Good," he said. "You are learning."

"Learning what?"

"Not to hand important things to old men who forget sack counts."

For the first time since the warehouse runner said no, she smiled.

Then they headed inland.

# V2 assessment

## Stronger than V1

- Wurdren's problem arrives before the humor.
- dry humor remains but no longer dominates every exchange.
- Port still feels busy / lived.
- the warehouse problem is clearer:
  - physical capacity;
  - recognized storage;
  - trusted measures / records;
  - guarantor acceptance.
- Wurdren's decision to stay reads as character rather than plot obligation.

## Remaining risk

The line:

> "He had come back to the road because he was tired of feeling that the years behind him contained nothing he could point to."

is the most explicit statement of his internal problem.

Possible later revision:

- keep it if early thematic clarity is desired;
- reduce it if later chapters establish the regret through memory / comparison more gradually.

## Current recommendation

**Use V2 as the working prose baseline for Scene 2.**

Do not treat wording as final manuscript text.
