# Current-Event Stakeholder Reaction Matrix — V1

## Status

**Active reference / pressure-reaction test.**

This page restores a useful legacy principle that had become too faint in the current model:

> **No important current event should have one social meaning.**

Legacy notes repeatedly treated guilds, piracy, smuggling, industrial change, Council regulation, strikes, and trade disruption as things that created:

- beneficiaries;
- losers;
- supporters;
- opponents;
- neutral / pragmatic users;
- internal faction splits.

The old named guilds / regions are not automatically canon.

The **reaction logic** is retained.

# 1. Reaction rule

For every major current event, ask:

1. Who is materially harmed?
2. Who materially benefits?
3. Who supports the response because of principle?
4. Who supports it because of profit / leverage?
5. Who opposes it despite benefiting economically?
6. Which institutions split internally?
7. Which group prefers the problem to continue because the response benefits them?
8. Which group dislikes the problem but dislikes the proposed solution even more?
9. Which actors can plausibly switch sides as conditions change?

If every group agrees that an event is simply "bad," the event is probably underbuilt.

# 2. Piracy / maritime insecurity

## Strong opponents

- crews / families exposed to violence;
- small shippers unable to absorb losses;
- merchants needing predictable delivery;
- lenders / risk underwriters exposed to loss;
- Port businesses dependent on reliable arrivals;
- NC-M / harbor authorities whose legitimacy depends on safe water.

## Conditional supporters / beneficiaries

Not everyone who benefits from piracy is "pro-pirate."

Possible beneficiaries include:

- convoy / escort providers;
- shipyards receiving security / repair work;
- larger merchants capable of absorbing higher risk while small competitors fail;
- smugglers whose alternate routes become more valuable;
- black-market buyers receiving diverted / stolen cargo;
- political factions favoring stronger maritime coordination;
- rival merchants who benefit when a competitor's route becomes unreliable.

## Possible sympathetic constituencies

Depending on the exact pirate / raider:

- coastal communities linked by kin / patronage;
- people who see some raiders as resisting unfair tolls / fishing rules;
- marginalized sailors excluded from legitimate shipping;
- actors who distinguish between "piracy" and locally legitimate seizure / reprisal.

Do not canonize universal popular support.

The useful rule is:

> **the label "pirate" may hide several different political / economic relationships.**

## People who oppose the solution

A fisher or island authority may hate piracy but still oppose:

- permanent NC-M convoy control;
- new levies;
- harbor inspection;
- centralized command.

This is particularly useful.

It prevents:

~~~text
piracy bad
→ everyone supports stronger state control
~~~

Instead:

~~~text
piracy bad
+ fear of permanent centralization
→ divided anti-piracy coalition
~~~

# 3. Convoy / patrol expansion

## Supporters

- merchants prioritizing reliability;
- families of sailors;
- security contractors;
- political factions favoring stronger NC-M coordination;
- lenders who prefer standardized risk.

## Opponents

- independent captains constrained by schedules;
- smaller operators unable to pay;
- NC-I / harbor actors fearing loss of autonomy;
- smugglers;
- merchants whose business depends on flexibility / unusual routes.

## Split groups

A shipping guild may divide between:

- large operators who can benefit from standardized convoy schedules;
- small captains who experience the same system as exclusion.

# 4. Low Rivers abundance / price collapse

## Harmed

- indebted producers;
- storage-poor farmers;
- small merchants holding inventory;
- lenders if default becomes widespread.

## Beneficiaries

- consumers buying cheap food;
- merchants with storage;
- livestock / processing businesses able to buy cheap inputs;
- land / debt buyers;
- Port relief networks able to purchase staples inexpensively.

## Political split

Producers may disagree over:

- emergency price support;
- export promotion;
- storage subsidies;
- restrictions on distressed acquisitions.

Consumers can oppose policies that raise food prices even while sympathizing with farmers.

This makes "save the farmers" politically non-simple.

---

# 5. Anti-consolidation / purchasing restrictions

## Supporters

- small producers;
- local reformers;
- some cooperative networks;
- politicians fearing permanent outside control of productive land / debt.

## Opponents

- outside merchants;
- lenders;
- local owners wanting access to buyers;
- consumers / officials who fear restrictions will reduce future investment.

## Opportunistic supporters

Established local elites may support "protective" rules because those rules also suppress outside competition.

A policy can be genuinely protective and self-serving at once.

# 6. SH-M labor dispute

## Worker side is not monolithic

Possible positions include:

- hard strike;
- negotiated settlement;
- safety-first faction;
- wage-first faction;
- apprenticeship / guild reform faction;
- workers afraid a prolonged strike will destroy orders permanently.

## Owner / guild side is not monolithic

Possible positions include:

- negotiate quickly;
- resist precedent;
- seek government protection;
- redirect production;
- use strategic-contract secrecy to avoid disclosure.

## Outside beneficiaries

- competing workshops;
- substitute suppliers;
- political factions wanting export control;
- buyers with pre-existing priority contracts.

# 7. Strategic-metal controls

## Supporters

- security planners;
- industries considered essential;
- workers who believe controls preserve domestic employment;
- politicians drawing on old ore-conflict memory.

## Opponents

- ordinary tool / repair users;
- foreign buyers;
- merchants;
- workshops excluded from priority status.

## Internal contradictions

A Port reformer may support Port retaining more strategic material while opposing Stone Hills using the same logic against Port.

That kind of hypocrisy / conflict is useful.

# 8. High Roads inspections / route security

## Supporters

- authorities responsible for safe passage;
- merchants carrying high-value cargo;
- insurers / lenders;
- legitimate carriers tired of fraud / banditry.

## Opponents

- merchants hurt by delay;
- jurisdictions fearing another polity's control of movement;
- smugglers;
- local economies dependent on flexible traffic.

## Beneficiaries from insecurity

- alternate-route towns;
- guides;
- security providers;
- rival corridors.

A town can sincerely condemn attacks while quietly benefiting from rerouted traffic.

# 9. Longwood emergency route / extraction project

## Supporters

- merchants needing redundancy;
- communities expecting jobs;
- local authority receiving fees / investment;
- people who see better road access as useful;
- outside governments concerned about supply.

## Opponents

- rights-holders whose authority was bypassed;
- ecological / specialist authorities;
- communities expecting extraction pressure;
- local traders who benefit from existing routes.

## Split communities

Do not assume "Longwood opposes road."

A household may want:

- easier market access;

while opposing:

- permanent timber concessions.

Another may oppose the road completely.

Another may support it because it weakens a rival local authority.

# 10. Old Cities water cooperation

## Supporters

- engineers / water officials;
- merchants needing predictability;
- farmers / urban consumers;
- political moderates.

## Skeptics / opponents

- rivals fearing a permanent bloc;
- officials protecting sovereignty;
- merchants who profit from separate systems;
- factions that fear shared technical institutions will accumulate political authority.

## Useful contradiction

A city can support water cooperation while opposing:

- shared food purchasing;
- joint security;
- permanent administration.

Cooperation is not one binary choice.

# 11. Port guild centralization

See [Guilds](../Politics/Guilds.md).

The current move toward stronger guild presence / cross-guild coordination in Port should create several camps.

## Port centralizers

Support:

- permanent representatives;
- shared standards;
- reciprocal credentials;
- common arbitration;
- cross-guild hiring / contracting.

Reasons:

- cheaper coordination;
- more work;
- easier continental reach;
- Port political strength.

Likely constituency:

- guild members whose work already crosses borders;
- Port branches;
- people frustrated by incompatible regional rules;
- some younger / expansion-minded professionals;
- guild leaders seeking continental influence.

## Federal coordinators

Support communication but resist central authority.

Prefer:

- rotating meetings;
- reciprocal agreements;
- strong branch autonomy;
- limited Port secretariat / clerical services.

This position is directly supported by legacy mechanisms:

- partial regional autonomy;
- rotating / multi-city guild meetings;
- fragile Guild Accord logic.

## Regional autonomists

Fear:

- Port leadership becoming de facto guild government;
- local rules being overwritten by distant standards;
- membership dues / contracts flowing toward Port;
- local governments losing influence;
- Port culture / commercial priorities dominating professions.

They may support guild cooperation while opposing **centralization**.

## Small-member / anti-conglomerate faction

Legacy notes repeatedly include:

- smaller merchants resenting dominant shipping conglomerates;
- members frustrated by guild bureaucracy;
- freelancers / rogue factions leaving central discipline.

Current-world version:

> some ordinary members may see Port centralization as making large guild houses even stronger.

They can object on class / scale grounds rather than regional identity.

## Traditional / professional-localist faction

Legacy guild material repeatedly uses:

- traditionalists vs innovators;
- local chapters with distinct practice;
- resistance to standardized central policy.

Current-world version:

some crafts / professions may argue that continental standardization destroys locally adapted practice.

## Splinter / independent associations

If centralization overreaches:

- a branch can refuse a decision;
- members can form an independent association;
- local chapters can stop recognizing Port decisions;
- professionals can operate outside the guild where law allows.

These should be real costs, not empty threats.

# 12. Three Moons Festival

## Enthusiastic supporters

- vendors;
- inns / boarding houses;
- performers;
- guild representatives;
- merchants seeking contracts;
- job seekers;
- Port civic boosters.

## Resentful participants

- workers facing extreme workload;
- residents facing rent / food increases;
- neighborhoods losing public space;
- local businesses displaced by larger festival operators.

## Political supporters

People who see Three Moons as proof:

> Port is the natural continental meeting point.

## Political skeptics

People who see the same event as proof:

> Port is accumulating too much continental influence.

Guild-centralization opponents may use Three Moons as evidence that temporary coordination is becoming permanent institutional relocation.

# 13. Smuggling / black market

Legacy notes strongly support mixed public perception.

## Opponents

- legal merchants;
- authorities;
- people exposed to dangerous / fraudulent goods;
- guilds whose standards are bypassed.

## Beneficiaries

- people avoiding onerous tolls;
- communities cut off from official supply;
- small merchants excluded from dominant channels;
- criminals;
- corrupt officials;
- consumers seeking restricted goods.

## Moral ambiguity

A smuggler can carry:

- weapons one day;
- medicine the next.

This is useful for the Villain's alternate network.

Do not equate:

> illegal = socially rejected.

# 14. Council intervention

## Supporters

- actors receiving stabilization;
- merchants who value predictable credit / routes;
- rulers fearing collapse;
- people whose food / medicine arrives because intervention worked.

## Opponents

- actors made to absorb the cost;
- people excluded from aid;
- political groups opposed to private hidden authority;
- guilds constrained by Council-linked financing.

## Ambivalent constituency

A person can believe:

> the Council saved us

and:

> the Council should not have this power

at the same time.

That is central to the setting.

# 15. Port autonomy / neutrality reform

## Stronger-Port supporters

May include:

- residents frustrated by delayed outside aid;
- local businesses wanting retained investment;
- guild centralizers;
- workers who believe stronger local institutions would improve services;
- reformers angered by conditional foreign help.

## Neutrality-first skeptics

May include:

- merchants dependent on broad foreign confidence;
- legal / arbitration interests;
- established harbor families;
- residents who fear becoming the battlefield over Port's sovereignty.

## Labor / neighborhood critics

Can agree with the grievance while asking:

> stronger power for whom?

They may oppose:

- foreign control;
- Council influence;
- large guild control;
- elite Port centralization

at the same time.

## Foreign beneficiaries of the status quo

Some outside merchants / powers sincerely value neutral access.

They can oppose Port political expansion without wanting to conquer Port.

## Design rule

The Villain's popularity should be measured against these competing positions.

Port support cannot be treated as one block.

# 17. Legacy material recovered

Useful mechanisms recovered from legacy notes include:

- Warriors-type institutions split between alignment and neutrality;
- Founders / industrial guild splinters breaking with official leadership;
- smaller merchants rebelling against dominant conglomerates;
- younger / older or traditional / reform factions inside professional institutions;
- open-knowledge factions challenging information monopolies;
- freelancers resisting growing guild bureaucracy;
- rogue local factions threatening central guild unity;
- a fragile inter-guild accord using shifting / neutral meeting places;
- guild chapters possessing meaningful partial autonomy;
- smuggling / black markets filling gaps left by official systems;
- crises generating supporters and beneficiaries as well as victims.

The old exact guild names and RPG-like guild roster remain legacy unless independently promoted.

The **factional mechanisms** are the part promoted here.
