# Mythic Creature Research Method — V1

## Status

**Active research method — not canon and not a creature list.**

This page defines how real-world mythological creatures, legendary animals, monster traditions, and uncertain historical beings should be researched and transformed for 10Kings.

Use with:

- [Mythic Creatures and Legendary Beasts — Framework V1](Mythic-Creatures-and-Legendary-Beasts-Framework-V1.md);
- [Magic Baseline](../Magic-Baseline.md);
- [Folk Belief, Taboo, and Superstition](../Culture/Folk-Belief-Taboo-and-Superstition.md);
- [Contested Historical Memory](../History/Contested-Memory.md).

---

# 1. Core method

Do **not** build creatures by visual collage.

Bad method:

~~~text
antlers from legend A
+
wings from legend B
+
eyes from legend C
=
fantasy chimera
~~~

Preferred method:

~~~text
real-world mythic pattern
+
ecological plausibility
+
historical distortion
+
local cultural meaning
=
in-world legendary creature
~~~

The unit of borrowing is primarily a **mechanism**, not an appearance.

---

# 2. What to extract from each source tradition

For every real-world comparator, record:

## A. Ecological role

Examples:

- cave / mine danger;
- sea / shoal danger;
- forest boundary;
- river / marsh hazard;
- highland pass;
- livestock threat;
- scavenger / carrion association.

## B. Narrative function

Examples:

- keeps children away from danger;
- warns workers about unsafe conditions;
- explains disappearance;
- encodes taboo;
- punishes greed / oathbreaking;
- marks sacred / restricted ground;
- preserves memory of catastrophe;
- gives shape to environmental uncertainty.

## C. Evidence pattern

Examples:

- tracks;
- bones;
- hair;
- old drawings;
- sailor testimony;
- family story;
- place names;
- ritual objects;
- recurring sightings;
- disputed remains.

## D. Social function

Examples:

- worker solidarity;
- local identity;
- outsider boundary;
- religious teaching;
- explanation of accidents;
- political scapegoat;
- moral warning;
- prestige / tourism / storytelling.

## E. Truth possibilities

Examples:

- ordinary animal;
- extinct animal;
- rare relict population;
- composite of several animals;
- natural phenomenon;
- historical event personified;
- fraud;
- spiritual interpretation;
- genuinely unresolved supernatural possibility.

---

# 3. What not to copy

For every comparator, explicitly state what should **not** be imported.

Possible non-borrows:

- sacred names;
- living religious claims;
- ceremonial practices;
- ethnicity;
- cosmology;
- exact visual design;
- language;
- ritual ownership;
- colonial stereotypes;
- one-to-one moral meaning.

This is especially important for traditions belonging to living Indigenous / religious communities.

Use the source as comparative research, not as a costume rack.

---

# 4. Function-first synthesis

When several traditions share a useful function, merge at the level of **why the story exists**.

Example:

~~~text
Tradition A:
worker spirit warns miners

Tradition B:
creature blamed for disappearances

Tradition C:
strange underground animal leaves physical traces

Local world history:
mine owners concealed unsafe collapses

In-world result:
a legendary underground being whose story contains:
- real hazard knowledge;
- labor memory;
- possible extinct fauna;
- later supernatural interpretation.
~~~

The final creature should not be identifiable as:

> "A + B + C with the serial numbers filed off."

It should arise naturally from 10Kings history.

---

# 5. Similarity test

Two real-world creatures belong in the same research pool only if they share at least one substantive dimension:

- ecological problem;
- social function;
- evidence pattern;
- moral role;
- historical distortion;
- occupational context.

Visual resemblance alone is insufficient.

---

# 6. Cultural transformation test

After extracting mechanisms, ask:

1. Which in-world community first tells this story?
2. What actual local hazard / event could have produced it?
3. Which neighboring communities changed it?
4. What does a scholar think?
5. What does a believer / worker / sailor think?
6. What practical behavior does the story preserve?
7. What evidence remains?
8. What cannot be explained cleanly?

Only then design:

- name;
- appearance;
- behavior;
- historical variants.

---

# 7. Truth-in-the-middle rule

The best creature legends should survive both extreme explanations.

If:

> "it was all fake"

makes the legend boring, it is too dependent on supernatural truth.

If:

> "it was definitely real"

settles every question, it is too much like a bestiary entry.

Preferred result:

> **something real happened, but the surviving legend is neither a literal record nor a pure invention.**

---

# 8. Research-slot method

Research begins by ecological / cultural slot.

Initial slots:

1. Spine / Underpass — subterranean / miner traditions;
2. North Coast / islands — maritime traditions;
3. Low Rivers / wetlands — river / marsh traditions;
4. Longwood — forest / boundary traditions;
5. High Roads / mountains — pass / cliff / weather traditions;
6. Old Cities / drylands — canal / ruin / desert traditions;
7. Port / estuary — harbor / fog / drowned-city / threshold traditions;
8. mobile circuits — road / camp / route-carried legends;
9. one or two continent-wide primordial traditions.

Each slot should gather **3–5 real-world comparators** before synthesis.

---

# 9. Research-note template

For each comparator:

~~~text
## [Tradition / creature]

Source culture / geography:
Historical / living tradition status:
Primary source(s):

### Source-derived features
- ...

### Likely function(s)
- ...

### Possible natural / historical roots
- ...

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms
- ...

### Do not borrow
- ...

### Potential slot(s)
- ...
~~~

Then create a synthesis page only after enough comparators exist.

---

# 10. Research priority

Begin with:

1. **Spine / Underpass**
   - strongest occupational / hazard fit;
   - already central to world geography;
   - naturally supports extinct / relict / miner-memory possibilities.

2. **North Coast / maritime**
   - strong oral / sailor tradition possibilities;
   - many natural misidentification pathways;
   - strong relation to trade and route risk.

3. **Longwood / forest boundary**
   - strong fit with layered access rights and ecological memory;
   - particularly useful for stories that preserve boundary / resource taboos.

Then expand outward.

---

# 11. Design safeguard

Do not ask:

> "What monster should this region have?"

Ask:

> **"What uncertainty, hazard, memory, or moral problem would this community have turned into a creature story?"**

That question should control the entire creature-design project.
