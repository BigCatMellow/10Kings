The question here is not simply:

> Where is the road?

It is:

> **What can actually move along it, how far, and where must cargo / people change mode?**

That determines where settlements, markets, warehouses, tolls, ferries, repair trades, inns, smugglers and political leverage naturally accumulate.

---

# 1. Core finding — mode change creates chokepoints

A narrow pass is not the only kind of chokepoint.

A place can become strategically important because traffic must switch from:

- river boat → cart;
- cart → pack animal;
- river barge → seagoing vessel;
- road → ferry;
- legal road → Underpass bypass;
- shallow craft → deepwater ship.

This produces:

```text
mode limit
→ unloading / waiting
→ storage
→ labor
→ inspection
→ lodging
→ finance
→ market
→ institution
→ political constituency
```

This is one of the strongest consequences of adding capacity to the map.

# 2. G1 — Low Rivers trunk river

## Strong working geometry

G1 should have **three transport zones**.

### G1-A — tidal / lower river

From Port upstream through the lower estuary / floodplain:

- deep or reliable enough for substantial cargo craft;
- strongly affected by tides near Port;
- lined with quays, storage and market settlements;
- capable of moving bulk grain and other heavy goods cheaply.

This is Port's main inland artery.

### G1-B — middle Low Rivers navigation

Farther upstream:

- regular barges / river craft remain viable;
- draft becomes shallower;
- crossings become more important;
- mills, bridges and ferry rights create friction.

Bulk movement remains normal.

### G1-C — upper river / head-of-navigation transition

Near the rising ground toward High Roads / The Spine:

- channel becomes shallower, faster or broken by falls / rapids / seasonal low water;
- large bulk craft stop being dependable;
- smaller boats may continue;
- cargo transfers to carts / pack trains.

This creates a **head-of-navigation market zone**.

Exact settlement name remains open.

## Strong consequence

The head-of-navigation market should become one of the most important unnamed inland nodes in the setting.

It naturally supports:

- warehouses;
- weighing;
- caravan yards;
- boat repair;
- cart repair;
- brokers;
- inns;
- labor hiring;
- toll / customs offices;
- translators;
- credit.

It does **not** need to be a capital.

---

# 3. G2 — Longwood tributary system

## Strong working geometry

G2 should be navigable in layers.

### Lower G2

From the G1 confluence toward western / lowland Longwood:

- shallow barges;
- rafts;
- timber movement;
- fish / forest goods;
- passenger boats.

### Middle G2

Farther into Longwood:

- smaller craft;
- seasonal navigation;
- local knowledge increasingly important;
- wetlands / shifting channels / debris may limit outsiders.

### Upper forest waters

Beyond reliable navigation:

- canoes / small boats where locally appropriate;
- pack paths;
- timber tracks;
- foot travel.

## Consequence

Longwood is connected to bulk markets without turning every forest settlement into a river port.

A few confluences and transfer settlements matter far more than the total length of the water system.

---

# 4. S1 — Old Cities river

## Strong working geometry

S1 is not a Low Rivers-style bulk superhighway.

Its lower reaches can support:

- local / regional boats;
- city-market traffic;
- some coastal connection.

But upstream navigation is constrained by:

- variable flow;
- irrigation diversions;
- seasonal shallows;
- gates / weirs / managed channels;
- competing water claims.

## Consequence

S1's most politically important points may be:

- diversion gates;
- reservoirs / cistern systems;
- bridge / ford towns;
- confluences;
- headworks;

rather than one uninterrupted navigable corridor.

This fits Old Cities politics better than a second G1.

---

# 5. W1 / W2 — Stone Hills drainage

## Strong working geometry

Western Stone Hills rivers are:

- shorter;
- steeper;
- useful for mills;
- useful for local floating / hauling in lower reaches;
- only partly navigable for bulk craft.

Some lower valleys may support:
- barges;
- rafts;
- small ports.

But Stone Hills should not gain an easy inland water network that makes Western Gate irrelevant.

## Consequence

Ore / metal often needs:

- cart;
- pack;
- short river transfer;
- coastal transfer;

rather than one continuous water route.

That increases the value of:
- roads;
- valley markets;
- secondary western ports.

# 6. Road capacity classes

The V1 surface roads should not all be equivalent.

## Class A — heavy commercial trunk

Supports carts through most of its length in good season.

Requires:

- bridges / ferries;
- road repair;
- drainage;
- grades manageable for draft animals;
- inns / yards;
- political agreements.

### R1 — Western Gate

**Class A with one difficult highland bottleneck.**

Heavy carts can cross end-to-end in ordinary good conditions.

At the hardest section:

- teams may be doubled;
- loads may be split;
- traffic queues;
- repair crews matter.

This is why R1 can carry meaningful bulk trade but remain vulnerable.

### R3 — River Road

**Class A.**

Runs beside / between navigable G1 sections and the HR ↔ LR ↔ Port corridor.

Bulk often uses water.

Road carries:

- passengers;
- livestock;
- high-value goods;
- time-sensitive cargo;
- traffic when river conditions fail.

### R5 — Southern Market Road

**Mostly Class A.**

Connects LR / Longwood transition zones toward Old Cities market systems.

It should include:

- major bridges / ferries;
- dry-season dust;
- wet-season trouble in some districts;
- several competing branches rather than one perfectly engineered highway.

---

## Class B — caravan / pack trunk

Reliable for animals and people.

Heavy carts may not pass end-to-end.

### R2 — North Shoulder

**Class B with Class A approaches.**

This is an important decision.

From NC and HR low approaches:

- wagons / carts can reach transfer towns.

At the high shoulder / principal pass:

- heavy carts become inefficient or impossible;
- cargo transfers to pack animals / narrower vehicles;
- some goods are warehoused until weather improves.

This keeps the sea route structurally superior for North Coast bulk freight.

It also naturally creates transfer settlements on both sides of the pass.

---

## Mixed Class A/B — Forest Edge Road

### R4

R4 should not be one uninterrupted cart highway.

It contains:

- good market-road segments;
- forest stretches;
- wet-ground sections;
- local bridges;
- ferry crossings;
- narrower roads where land / stewardship politics prevented widening.

Carts can travel meaningful portions.

Some through cargo must:
- transfer;
- detour;
- wait seasonally.

This helps Longwood remain connected without turning forest-edge infrastructure into effortless extraction.

# 7. North Shoulder transfer system

The R2 design creates **two linked chokepoints**, not one gate.

## NC-side roadhead

Functions:

- carts arrive;
- loads are sorted;
- animals are changed;
- weather reports gathered;
- pack trains assembled;
- winter storage matters.

## HR-side roadhead

Functions:

- pack loads become cart / caravan loads;
- contracts / tolls / guides matter;
- delayed cargo enters wider High Roads networks.

## Political implication

Control of the pass itself is only part of the power.

The roadhead institutions can control:

- scheduling;
- storage;
- animals;
- labor;
- information;
- insurance-like arrangements.

A ruler holding the ridge but not the transfer economy may have less leverage than expected.

# 8. Port local-geometry options

Three physical forms were tested conceptually.

## Option A — mainland peninsula

Advantages:

- easiest inland road connection;
- fresh water / expansion easier;
- simple administration.

Weakness:

- one adjacent territorial power can threaten it too directly;
- neutrality becomes harder to explain;
- fewer natural reasons outside powers need a special arrangement.

## Option B — single offshore island

Advantages:

- defensible;
- strong separate identity;
- difficult for one land power to absorb.

Weakness:

- weaker direct river integration;
- fresh-water / food dependence becomes extreme;
- bridge / ferry dependence may become too total;
- large urban expansion becomes awkward.

## Option C — estuary island / channel complex

A principal urban island or defensible low ridge inside / beside the estuary, with:

- deepwater channels;
- mainland river quays;
- ferry / causeway connections;
- roadheads on adjacent shore;
- room for satellite docks / yards.

Advantages:

- strongest river ↔ sea transfer;
- separate civic geography;
- multiple approaches;
- one shore does not automatically equal control of the whole harbor;
- physical reason for treaty access;
- enough land connection for food / water / expansion.

Weaknesses:

- flood / silt management;
- bridge / ferry politics;
- more complicated jurisdiction;
- some outer facilities may sit on mainland territory / disputed land.

## Result

**Option C is the strongest working Port geometry.**

Promote only the pattern:

> Port is centered on an estuary / channel complex with a defensible urban core separated enough from surrounding mainland power to sustain distinct local government, while remaining tightly connected to river and road infrastructure.

Do **not** yet fix:

- number of islands;
- exact channel shape;
- causeway locations;
- municipal boundary.

# 9. Port's internal transport zones

The geography implies at least four functional harbor zones.

These are functional zones, not named districts.

## Deepwater / seagoing berths

Where larger coastal / maritime vessels unload.

## River quays

Where G1 craft transfer:
- grain;
- timber;
- passengers;
- inland goods.

## Warehousing / transfer belt

Needs:
- storage;
- weighing;
- brokers;
- carts;
- labor;
- customs / inspection.

## Mainland roadheads

Where R3 and other surface routes meet ferry / bridge / quay systems.

## Consequence

Port's political conflicts can be spatial.

Different constituencies may depend on different pieces of the transfer system.

# 10. Underpass entrances

Do not create one giant formal Underpass gate.

Use multiple surface-interface types.

## U1 — Western crossing

### Stone Hills side

A major known entrance should lie near:

- mining / quarry / mountain settlement;
- cart road;
- storage;
- pack-animal yards.

Heavy carts may reach the entrance.

Most loads transfer before deeper tunnel travel.

### Eastern side

A major exit should emerge near:

- High Roads / upper Low Rivers foothill routes;
- not directly inside Port;
- a surface market capable of distributing small high-value cargo.

## U2 — northern branch

Entrances should lie near:

- the North Shoulder transfer system;
- HR-side highland settlements.

Its strategic value rises when R2 is closed.

## U3 — forest branch

Do **not** give U3 one dominant public gateway.

Use several:

- locally known entrances;
- old mine / cave connections;
- community-controlled branches;
- hidden / disputed routes.

That supports:

- smugglers;
- refugees;
- local trade;
- guide dependence.

# 11. Chokepoint tiers

## Tier I — world-system chokepoints

Failure changes several regions.

### Port estuary / transfer complex

Why:
- sea;
- river;
- roads;
- finance;
- storage;
- law;
- information

meet here.

### Western Gate

Why:
- highest-capacity direct SH ↔ eastern land route.

### High Roads major switching junction

Why:
- multiple surface routes converge;
- brokerage / redistribution;
- passenger / information movement.

---

## Tier II — major interregional chokepoints

Failure strongly alters two or three regional systems.

### G1 head of navigation

Because cargo must change river ↔ road.

### North Shoulder pass + roadheads

Because NC bulk / land travel changes mode.

### G2 confluence / main transfer zone

Because Longwood bulk goods enter LR / G1 systems.

### major R5 river crossing / ferry complex

Because LR ↔ OC inland movement concentrates there.

### major S1 water-control complexes

Because water distribution can affect several Old Cities polities.

---

## Tier III — contingency / local chokepoints

Important under specific conditions.

Examples:

- U1 / U2 tunnel junctions;
- minor ferries;
- seasonal forest bridges;
- secondary western ports;
- local Longwood boardwalk / causeway systems.

A Tier III point can temporarily become Tier II when the normal route fails.

---

# 12. Dynamic chokepoints

A major result:

> **chokepoint rank changes with conditions.**

Example:

```text
R1 open
→ U1 is secondary

R1 closes
→ U1 becomes strategically important

R1 reopens
→ U1 loses bulk leverage but retains secrecy / contingency value
```

Likewise:

- storm season raises North Shoulder value;
- winter closure raises U2 value;
- low G1 water raises River Road value;
- bridge loss raises ferries;
- drought raises S1 gate politics.

The world therefore has **contingent geography**, not one permanent map of importance.

# 13. Settlement consequences

The physical network now implies several settlement types that should exist even before they are named.

## Head-of-navigation market

At G1 upper navigation limit.

## Pass roadhead towns

On both sides of R2's pack-transfer segment.

## Western Gate service town(s)

At the difficult R1 section.

## G2 confluence town

Where Longwood river traffic enters the LR trunk system.

## Southern Market crossing town

At a major R5 bridge / ferry / junction.

## Underpass interface settlements

Where surface transport meets major known tunnel entrances.

These places do not need to be capitals.

Their importance comes from **mode conversion**.

---

# 14. Economic consequence

Mode conversion creates transaction cost.

Every transfer can require:

- labor;
- storage;
- weighing;
- damage risk;
- fees;
- paperwork;
- animals;
- waiting.

Therefore:

```text
shorter route
≠
cheaper route
```

A longer continuous river journey may beat a shorter trip requiring three unloads.

This should matter when writing trade and Council strategy.

---

# 15. Information consequence

Mode-conversion towns are also information hubs.

Why:

- travelers wait;
- cargo records accumulate;
- workers hear multiple routes;
- merchants compare prices;
- couriers change animals;
- interpreters / brokers operate.

Therefore:

- G1 head-of-navigation;
- R2 roadheads;
- Western Gate transfer yards;
- Port;

should know route news disproportionately early.

This extends [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md).

---

# 16. Political consequence

Control of a chokepoint can come from several different assets.

A political actor may control:

- land;
- bridge;
- warehouse;
- ferry;
- draft animals;
- river pilots;
- legal inspection;
- credit;
- local labor;
- tunnel guides.

Therefore "who owns the road" is not enough.

A ruler can formally own territory while:
- guilds own warehouses;
- local families control ferries;
- merchants finance repairs;
- guides control practical passage.

That is middle-worldbuilding leverage.

# 17. Tests against current crises

## Western Gate closure

Passes strongly.

The R1 bottleneck is:
- high-capacity enough to matter;
- replaceable enough that closure does not end all trade.

## North Coast shipping crisis

Passes strongly.

R2 cannot absorb all maritime bulk because of pack transshipment.

This preserves maritime leverage.

## Port labor stoppage

Becomes more consequential.

A dock strike can affect:
- sea unloading;
- river transfer;
- warehousing;
- cart dispatch;

without closing the physical harbor itself.

## Longwood road dispute

Passes.

R4 can be politically blocked while G2 still carries some goods, creating substitution rather than total isolation.

## Old Cities water conflict

Passes.

S1's political leverage comes from water-control infrastructure rather than only river transport.

---

# 18. Strong working promotions

This pass supports promoting the following **structural statements**:

1. G1 has a reliable bulk-navigation zone ending before the High Roads uplands.
2. A major head-of-navigation transfer market exists somewhere in upper / central Low Rivers.
3. R1 and R3 are principal cart-capable trunks.
4. R2 requires meaningful cart ↔ pack transshipment at its highland bottleneck.
5. R4 is not continuously heavy-cart capable.
6. Port is best modeled as an estuary / channel complex rather than a simple mainland port.
7. Underpass branches interface with surface transport through several different entrance types.
8. The most important chokepoints are often **mode-conversion points**, not merely narrow terrain.

Exact names / borders remain open.

# 20. Disposition

**Strong pass.**

The physical network is now specific enough to begin generating:

- transfer towns;
- warehouse politics;
- ferry rights;
- river pilots;
- pass labor;
- cart / pack industries;
- road-maintenance constituencies;
- tunnel-interface economies.

The next useful geography step is **not another continent sketch**.

It is to choose one or two of these implied nodes and build them as actual middle-scale places, beginning with either:

1. the G1 head-of-navigation market; or
2. the North Shoulder paired roadhead system.

Those will test whether the network produces believable towns rather than only good arrows on a map.
