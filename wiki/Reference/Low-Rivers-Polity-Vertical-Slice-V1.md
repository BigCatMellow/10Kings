# Low Rivers Polity Vertical Slice — V1

## Status

**Provisional polity test — not final canon.**

This page tests LR-A / LR-M from:

- [Working Political Geography — V1](Working-Political-Geography-V1.md)
- [Low Rivers Functional Governance Test](Low-Rivers-Functional-Governance-Test.md)
- [G1 Head-of-Navigation Market](G1-Head-of-Navigation-Market-Test.md)
- [Political Border History Test — V1](Political-Border-History-Test-V1.md)

It does not establish:

- polity name;
- ruler title;
- capital;
- dynasty;
- exact constitution;
- exact district boundaries;
- exact tax rates;
- exact reserve system;
- exact land-tenure law;
- exact succession rule.

The purpose is to answer:

> **Can LR-A remain one substantial territorial polity without flattening the villages, estates, river towns, cooperatives, water institutions, and markets that actually make the Low Rivers function?**

**Naming status:** G1, LR-A, and LR-M local lexical forms remain unresolved. **Last Quay** remains a grounded commercial exonym for LR-M. # 1. Starting constraint

Low Rivers requires meaningful large-scale authority for:

- taxation;
- military force;
- major works;
- emergency intervention;
- diplomacy;
- higher enforcement.

But its ordinary life depends on institutions the center cannot efficiently replace:

- villages;
- estates;
- market towns;
- cooperatives;
- seed systems;
- local storage;
- water associations;
- watershed / navigation institutions.

The strongest V1 solution is therefore:

> **LR-A as a basin-centered administrative territorial polity.**

Its state capacity follows:

- land;
- records;
- river crossings;
- tax collection;
- higher courts;
- reserve / emergency policy;
- major transport.

Its operational limits are especially visible in:

- water;
- storage;
- planting;
- local tenure;
- village rights.

---

# 2. Historical formation model

## SUPPORTED

Pre-Convergence Low Rivers contained:

- estate rulers;
- village leagues;
- river towns;
- grain confederacies;
- irrigation associations.

Crop failure repeatedly destabilized politics.

The Seven-year Blight or remembered equivalent strengthened:

- storage;
- crop diversity;
- communal aid;
- suspicion of concentrated grain / seed control.

## PROVISIONAL V1 sequence

~~~
many lowland authorities
→ crop crisis and raiding expose coordination failures
→ river towns / estates / village systems develop stronger storage and relief practices
→ Convergence stabilizes territorial claims and long-distance exchange
→ one territorial authority gradually consolidates much of G1 basin
→ consolidation works because it can coordinate:
   - tax;
   - defense;
   - crossings;
   - major roads;
   - reserve policy;
   - higher dispute resolution
→ local agricultural institutions survive because replacing them would reduce practical capacity
→ LR-A becomes a large polity with strong administration but distributed implementation
~~~

This makes LR-A different from SH-A.

SH-A grows around incorporation of powerful productive jurisdictions.

LR-A grows around **basin-scale administration and coordination of many local agrarian systems**.

---

# 3. State geography follows the basin

V1 places LR-A across much of the central G1 lowlands.

This gives the polity a practical administrative spine:

- river towns;
- crossings;
- market roads;
- grain movement;
- tax collection;
- information.

The boundary does not need to match all Low Rivers culture.

Some Low Rivers cultural communities can live:

- across SH-A frontier;
- in Port;
- near Old Cities;
- in High Roads valleys.

LR-A is a polity inside the Low Rivers cultural core, not the political definition of the culture.

---

# 4. Central authority

LR-A needs one recognized central authority capable of:

- external diplomacy;
- military mobilization;
- broad taxation;
- major public works;
- higher courts;
- land-title recognition at large scale;
- emergency reserve policy;
- major crossing / river-road investment;
- Convergence obligations.

V1 does not require a specific ruler type.

The state architecture can support:

- monarchy;
- mixed hereditary / council rule;
- another territorial executive system.

Unlike SH-A, the current evidence does **not** force a succession model yet.

That difference should be preserved.

---

# 5. Administrative districts

LR-A should not divide itself by cultural subregion.

More plausible administrative units can follow practical systems such as:

- tributary basin;
- market catchment;
- old estate district;
- river crossing;
- town hinterland;
- historical jurisdiction.

Different districts can retain different:

- tenancy;
- commons;
- village rights;
- irrigation practice.

The center needs records precise enough to tax and adjudicate.

It does not need one uniform land code.

---

# 6. Land tenure

V1 deliberately supports plural tenure.

A district can contain:

- smallholders;
- tenant farms;
- large estates;
- cooperative holdings;
- village commons;
- religious / charitable land;
- town-owned land.

### Central state role

LR-A needs to recognize and enforce some land claims.

It does not need to own all land or make every tenure category identical.

### Political consequence

Land consolidation can therefore become a current crisis without requiring the law itself to be new.

Existing legal forms can be used aggressively during debt distress.

---

# 7. Estate authority

Estates can possess real local power over:

- leases;
- rents;
- labor;
- private roads;
- private storage;
- credit.

But estates remain under LR-A political sovereignty.

They should not normally possess independent:

- diplomacy;
- major criminal law;
- military policy.

### Important limit

An estate can become locally dominant enough that tenants experience it as government.

That does not make it legally sovereign.

This gap between lived power and formal authority is politically useful.

---

# 8. Village / cooperative rights

The Seven-year Blight memory supports durable local institutions around:

- seed;
- storage;
- mutual aid;
- local water;
- labor.

V1 gives villages and cooperatives real recognized competence over some of these functions.

This can be based on:

- custom;
- charter;
- district law;
- long-standing practice.

### Why this matters

If every cooperative exists only by temporary central permission, the history of communal survival loses institutional weight.

If every village is sovereign, LR-A stops functioning as a large polity.

Recognized local rights fit between those extremes.

---

# 9. Water authority

This is the strongest difference from Stone Hills.

LR-A territorial officials should **not** automatically control all water allocation.

The polity needs watershed / river institutions capable of cutting across:

- villages;
- estates;
- towns;
- administrative districts.

Their authority can cover:

- flow;
- navigation;
- shared works;
- flood warning;
- allocation;
- dispute procedure.

### V1 model

The central state recognizes and enforces these institutions without turning them into ordinary ministries.

Some may predate LR-A.

Some may have received later charter / legal recognition.

This preserves functional jurisdiction.

---

# 10. LR-M — G1 transfer-market jurisdiction

LR-M is inside LR-A's wider political sphere.

It is not merely a market square.

It needs local authority over:

- quays;
- roads;
- warehouses;
- scales;
- markets;
- crossing;
- fire;
- sanitation;
- local watch;
- commercial disputes.

## Why the center tolerates strong local authority

LR-M's value comes from:

- speed;
- trust;
- records;
- expertise;
- commercial reputation.

Overcentralizing routine operations could reduce those advantages.

## Why LR-M remains inside LR-A

The central polity needs:

- tax;
- route access;
- strategic food movement;
- law;
- military control;
- diplomacy.

LR-M is powerful because it is a chokepoint.

It does not need sovereign foreign policy.

---

# 11. LR-M political status

The best V1 model is:

> **a privileged market / river city inside LR-A with protected local commercial and civic competence but no independent sovereignty.**

Its privilege can arise from:

- old town rights;
- river crossing importance;
- post-Blight storage role;
- Convergence-era trade settlement;
- later charter.

Exact source remains open.

This resembles SH-M only superficially.

### SH-M power

Comes heavily from concentrated industrial production.

### LR-M power

Comes heavily from:

- transfer;
- storage;
- records;
- market coordination;
- crossing;
- river / road conversion.

The institutional politics should therefore feel different.

---

# 12. Fiscal base

LR-A's strongest recurring revenue should come from the agrarian / commercial landscape.

Possible sources include:

## Land assessment

Regular revenue tied to:

- acreage;
- household;
- productivity category;
- tenure.

Exact system remains open.

## Market / customs revenue

Major towns and crossings generate:

- fees;
- tolls;
- customs.

## Estate obligations

Large estates may owe:

- cash;
- grain;
- military support;
- negotiated obligations.

## River / crossing revenue

Major ferries, bridges, or commercial points may contribute.

## Extraordinary levy

Crisis can create:

- war levy;
- flood levy;
- famine / reserve levy.

### Difference from SH-A

SH-A can lean unusually heavily on extraction and industrial wealth.

LR-A's revenue is broader and more geographically dispersed.

That can make it administratively stronger in ordinary rural records but less able to control each productive unit directly.

---

# 13. Reserve policy after the Blight

The Seven-year Blight memory creates a strong Writing-the-Sequel opportunity.

V1 tests:

> **LR-A maintains some public / mandated reserve capacity, but not one monopolistic central grain system.**

Possible mixed structure:

- central reserve stores;
- town stores;
- village / cooperative stores;
- estate stores;
- religious / charitable stores.

The state may require:

- minimum reserve;
- emergency reporting;
- temporary release;
- purchase.

### Why distributed reserves fit

They preserve the cultural suspicion of concentrated grain control.

They also make emergency capacity real.

---

# 14. Seed politics

The state should not normally own seed.

LR-A may regulate:

- fraud;
- quarantine;
- emergency distribution;
- debt claims;
- sale of new strains.

But seed systems can remain distributed among:

- households;
- cooperatives;
- estates;
- merchants;
- specialists.

This makes current suspicion around new high-yield strains plausible.

---

# 15. Higher courts / appeals

Local disputes can begin with:

- village forum;
- estate authority;
- town court;
- water association;
- market arbitration.

LR-A needs higher forums for:

- major land title;
- estate / tenant conflict;
- inter-district dispute;
- serious crime;
- state tax claims.

Exact court hierarchy remains open.

### Design rule

A tenant should have some path beyond the estate.

A village should have some path beyond the neighboring town.

But distant justice can be expensive and class-biased.

---

# 16. Military capacity

LR-A requires real territorial force.

Possible layers:

## Central force

Handles:

- strategic defense;
- serious rebellion;
- major road / river security;
- executive enforcement.

## District levies / obligations

Agrarian districts may owe:

- soldiers;
- supplies;
- animals;
- money.

## Town forces

River towns maintain:

- watch;
- local guards;
- crossing security.

## Emergency labor

Flood / bridge / road crisis may mobilize labor outside military structure.

### Important difference

LR-A military strength should not make the polity look like an agrarian war machine.

The state exists largely to make ordinary production / circulation governable.

---

# 17. Ordinary legitimacy

LR-A can be legitimate because it provides:

- recognized land claims;
- higher courts;
- defense;
- major crossings;
- flood / disaster coordination;
- market order;
- famine response;
- Convergence standing.

A smallholder can resent taxes while still valuing:

- the bridge;
- predictable title;
- protection from estate seizure;
- emergency grain.

An estate owner can resent regulation while valuing:

- contract enforcement;
- roads;
- military protection.

That creates cross-class reasons for the polity to survive.

# 19. Test 1 — abundance / collapsing-price crisis

The current harvest produces too much grain for immediate demand / storage.

## LR-A

Can:

- purchase reserves;
- defer tax;
- finance transport;
- alter export rules;
- regulate distressed sale.

## LR-M

Can:

- manage queues;
- warehouse access;
- market records;
- transfer.

## Villages / cooperatives

Can:

- pool loads;
- store;
- delay sale where possible.

## Estates

Can often wait longer.

### Result

No one actor can solve the crisis alone.

**STRONG PASS.**

LR-A matters without erasing market mechanics.

---

# 20. Test 2 — flood

Local communities respond first.

Water institutions coordinate:

- warning;
- gates / embankments;
- river condition.

LR-A supplies:

- money;
- labor;
- military logistics;
- higher enforcement.

**PASS.**

This confirms a basin administrative state rather than a centralized water ministry.

---

# 21. Test 3 — drought / upstream conflict

An upstream district takes too much water.

Local political officials favor their own taxpayers.

The watershed body rules against them.

## Key test

Will LR-A enforce a functional institution against one of its own districts?

If not, water law is decorative.

If yes automatically in every case, local politics are too weak.

### V1 model

The center generally recognizes water rulings because basin-wide predictability benefits the state, but can:

- delay;
- interpret;
- negotiate emergency exceptions.

**PASS.**

---

# 22. Test 4 — estate / tenant conflict

A powerful estate attempts debt-based eviction after the abundance crash.

The estate cites contract.

Tenant community cites:

- custom;
- disaster relief rule;
- false measure;
- local rights.

The case can move:

local / estate forum  
→ district / town  
→ higher LR-A court

**PASS.**

LR-A is strong enough to limit estate sovereignty without administering every tenancy.

---

# 23. Test 5 — LR-M resists central export policy

LR-A imposes a temporary export restriction.

LR-M merchants argue it will:

- clog warehouses;
- collapse prices further;
- violate contracts.

The city cannot simply ignore the state.

The state cannot implement policy without the city.

Negotiation may produce:

- quota;
- exception;
- compensation;
- timed release.

**STRONG PASS.**

This gives LR-M genuine political weight without sovereignty.

---

# 24. Test 6 — major crossing failure

A G1 crossing fails.

LR-M or another local authority manages emergency traffic.

LR-A can:

- finance repair;
- mobilize labor;
- coordinate alternate routes;
- negotiate with other districts.

**PASS.**

---

# 25. Test 7 — emergency reserve seizure

A bad harvest follows the abundance year.

LR-A orders release or purchase of grain.

Some estate / merchant stores resist.

## Design test

The state needs real emergency power.

But wholesale uncompensated seizure would intensify historical fear of concentrated grain control.

### V1 rule

Emergency reserve policy works best through:

- preexisting obligations;
- purchase;
- requisition with recognized compensation;
- release rules established before crisis.

**PASS.**

---

# 26. Test 8 — Convergence / cross-border food dispute

A neighboring polity claims LR-A is using export policy to violate protected exchange.

LR-A answers that domestic food security requires restriction.

Evidence comes from:

- LR-M records;
- warehouse data;
- river conditions;
- reserve reports.

The dispute reaches diplomatic / Convergence forums.

**PASS.**

LR-A has external standing while economic evidence remains distributed.

# 27. Comparison with SH-A

## SH-A

Core state problem:

> how does a central ruler govern a highly concentrated industrial / mining economy whose productive institutions have their own power?

Best model:

> **post-Convergence composite territorial polity.**

## LR-A

Core state problem:

> how does a large territorial state coordinate a dispersed agrarian / river basin without destroying the local institutions that know how land, water, storage, and planting actually work?

Best model:

> **basin-centered administrative territorial polity.**

### Shared features

Both have:

- central diplomacy;
- force;
- taxation;
- local privilege;
- inherited institutional diversity.

### Important differences

SH-A politics clusters strongly around:

- concentrated mines;
- guild cities;
- strategic metal;
- industrial credit.

LR-A politics spreads across:

- land;
- river towns;
- estates;
- villages;
- water systems;
- storage.

Therefore:

> **similar state size does not imply similar political structure.**

This passes the anti-symmetry test.

---

# 28. Strong V1 polity conclusion

LR-A works as:

> **a basin-centered territorial polity with meaningful central administrative, fiscal, military, legal, and emergency capacity whose implementation remains distributed across river towns, estates, villages, cooperatives, markets, and watershed institutions.**

LR-M works as:

> **a privileged transfer-market city inside LR-A whose power comes from storage, records, crossing, and mode conversion rather than sovereign independence.**

The strongest distinctive institutional principle is:

> **LR-A can govern the basin without owning the basin's water institutions or food system outright.**

# 30. Promotion gate

Alternative models:

## A. Fully centralized agrarian state

Fails because it makes water, villages, estates, and local storage too administratively subordinate.

## B. Loose river league

Fails because current world needs stronger large-scale taxation, military capacity, emergency intervention, and diplomacy.

## C. Estate oligarchy

Fails because towns, smallholders, cooperatives, and public institutions matter too much.

## D. Basin-centered administrative territorial polity

Fits:

- one large V1 state;
- distributed agrarian competence;
- watershed authority;
- LR-M;
- abundance crisis;
- flood / drought;
- Convergence diplomacy.

**V1 winner: basin-centered administrative territorial polity as the working design model.**

This remains a reference conclusion, not a named constitutional canon.
