# Convergence Enforcement and Violation Tests

## Status

**Working enforcement model — not a fixed treaty procedure.**

Owners:

- [The Convergence](../History/The-Convergence.md)
- [Convergence Layered Settlement Model](Convergence-Layered-Settlement-Model.md)
- [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md)
- [Port](../Places/Port.md)

This page tests how the Convergence can matter without possessing a central sovereign.

The goal is to preserve a meaningful middle range between:

- rules nobody can enforce;
- automatic war.

---

# 1. Core rule

The Convergence works through **graduated consequences**.

A useful default sequence is:

notice / complaint  
→ local remedy  
→ negotiation  
→ recognized arbitration  
→ commercial / guild pressure  
→ reciprocal restriction  
→ coalition pressure  
→ targeted suspension of privilege  
→ limited coordinated enforcement  
→ open conflict if the settlement fails

No case must follow every step.

Severity depends on:

- what obligation was violated;
- whether the act was private or official;
- whether it was isolated or systematic;
- how many actors are harmed;
- whether the violator accepts review;
- whether the act threatens the wider settlement itself.

---

# 2. The first distinction — breach versus challenge to the system

Not every violation is equally political.

## Ordinary breach

Examples:

- one merchant fails to pay;
- one caravan damages cargo;
- one guild member uses a false mark;
- one local official overcharges a traveler.

Usually handled through:

- local law;
- contract;
- guild discipline;
- arbitration.

## Systemic obstruction

Examples:

- a polity systematically blocks protected caravans;
- officials repeatedly seize foreign goods despite recognized access;
- one authority knowingly shelters attacks on a protected route;
- Port access is denied selectively for political leverage.

These can become Convergence issues.

## Direct challenge

Examples:

- seizure of recognized territory;
- unilateral occupation of Port infrastructure;
- deliberate closure of a major protected route to coerce another polity;
- sustained repudiation of recognized arbitration / access obligations.

These threaten the settlement itself.

---

# 3. Private actor versus public authority

A private actor can violate law without creating a treaty crisis.

A ruler's response matters.

Example:

bandits attack a caravan.

Possible outcomes:

- local authority pursues them → ordinary security problem;
- local authority cannot stop them but cooperates → capacity failure;
- local authority ignores repeated attacks → political dispute;
- local authority secretly sponsors them → possible treaty violation.

The same physical event can occupy very different legal layers.

---

# 4. Good-faith incapacity versus deliberate obstruction

This distinction should matter.

A road may be unusable because:

- flood;
- landslide;
- war damage;
- lack of repair funds;
- deliberate blockade.

The result for merchants may look similar.

The political meaning is not.

Good-faith incapacity can produce:

- requests for aid;
- temporary exceptions;
- shared repair;
- delayed obligations.

Deliberate obstruction can produce:

- adverse arbitration;
- reciprocal restriction;
- political pressure;
- suspension of privileges.

---

# 5. Notice and cure

A believable settlement should allow some violations to be corrected before escalation.

Possible pattern:

1. complaint is sent;
2. alleged violator is given a chance to explain / cure;
3. disputed facts are gathered;
4. a forum is chosen if needed;
5. consequences escalate only if remedy fails.

This helps separate:

- administrative mistake;
- local corruption;
- genuine policy;
- deliberate repudiation.

Exact deadlines remain open.

---

# 6. Commercial enforcement often precedes political enforcement

The world already supports strong network consequences.

Before rulers mobilize force, merchants and institutions may react through:

- refusing credit;
- raising risk premiums;
- rerouting;
- suspending contracts;
- denying reciprocal market access;
- guild warnings;
- Port restrictions;
- warehouse refusal.

This is especially plausible because the Convergence protects exchange.

A polity can feel the cost of noncompliance before soldiers move.

---

# 7. Arbitration does not need coercive supremacy

An arbitration decision can matter because:

- merchants rely on it;
- guilds recognize it;
- lenders price against it;
- Port enforces it locally;
- other polities treat refusal as evidence of bad faith.

A losing party can still refuse.

Refusal simply changes the political / commercial cost.

This preserves fragmented sovereignty.

---

# 8. Reciprocal restrictions should be targeted

A strong settlement should avoid turning every dispute into total embargo.

Possible targeted responses:

- suspend one route privilege;
- deny one class of goods;
- refuse one guild credential;
- increase inspection;
- deny one harbor service;
- withhold reciprocal toll preference;
- suspend one bilateral annex.

This makes partial breakdown possible.

It also creates bargaining space.

---

# 9. Coalition pressure

A serious violation becomes dangerous when several actors independently decide that allowing it would weaken rules they rely on.

Coalition pressure can take the form of:

- joint diplomatic demand;
- coordinated commercial restriction;
- shared arbitration support;
- route cooperation with the injured party;
- refusal to recognize the violator's preferred interpretation.

This is the Convergence's closest substitute for central enforcement.

---

# 10. Limited coordinated enforcement

Current canon supports joint action against threats to shared circulation more strongly than broad military alliance.

Likely candidates include:

- piracy;
- bandit control;
- reopening a protected route;
- clearing a seized shared crossing;
- escorting protected traffic.

This should remain:

- narrow;
- mission-specific;
- politically contentious.

It should not become a permanent continental army.

---

# 11. Case test — ordinary contract breach

A High Roads merchant fails to pay a Low Rivers supplier.

Likely path:

contract forum  
→ local / guild remedy  
→ arbitration if cross-border  
→ judgment  
→ credit / market consequences

Not a Convergence crisis unless:

- a government blocks enforcement;
- a recognized forum is systematically repudiated;
- the dispute becomes a test of protected exchange.

**Result: pass.**

The treaty framework remains background infrastructure.

---

# 12. Case test — discriminatory toll increase

A polity raises tolls sharply on one neighboring polity's caravans while charging others normally.

Questions:

- Is the route covered by protected-passage rules?
- Is differential tolling prohibited by an attached agreement?
- Is there a legitimate security / maintenance reason?
- Is the measure temporary?
- Is it retaliation for another breach?

Possible escalation:

complaint  
→ negotiation  
→ arbitration  
→ reciprocal toll action  
→ broader commercial pressure

**Result: pass.**

This can become serious without becoming war.

---

# 13. Case test — tolerated banditry

A major protected road suffers repeated attacks.

Local authority claims it lacks resources.

Three versions:

## Good-faith weakness

Authority requests help and cooperates.

Likely response:
- shared patrol;
- merchant-funded security;
- route aid;
- temporary diversion.

## Negligence

Authority does little despite repeated warnings.

Likely response:
- arbitration;
- commercial rerouting;
- pressure;
- demand for specific security action.

## Sponsorship

Authority benefits from or supports the attacks.

Likely response:
- serious treaty accusation;
- coalition pressure;
- reciprocal restriction;
- possible limited joint enforcement.

**Result: strong pass.**

Intent and response matter as much as the attack.

---

# 14. Case test — bridge closure

A major cross-border bridge is closed.

## Safety closure

Bridge is damaged.

Likely:
- temporary restriction;
- alternate ferry;
- repair coordination.

## Fiscal dispute

Owner closes it to force higher tolls.

Likely:
- contract / attached-agreement dispute;
- arbitration;
- commercial pressure.

## Political coercion

A ruler closes it to starve a rival of trade despite protected passage.

Likely:
- wider Convergence issue.

**Result: strong pass.**

The same infrastructure can move through different legal layers.

---

# 15. Case test — territorial seizure

One polity occupies a disputed frontier settlement recognized in practice as belonging to another.

This is more serious because the Convergence strongly supports recognized claims / non-aggression.

Likely sequence:

- factual dispute over recognition / boundary;
- emergency negotiation;
- arbitration / mediation if possible;
- coalition pressure;
- commercial penalties;
- demand for withdrawal.

If occupation continues, wider military confrontation becomes plausible.

**Result: pass.**

This is one of the cases where the settlement's credibility is directly at stake.

---

# 16. Case test — Port warehouse seizure by foreign troops

A foreign power lands armed personnel and seizes a warehouse during a dispute.

This triggers several layers:

- local Port law;
- foreign commercial rights;
- armed-presence limits;
- neutrality;
- rival powers' fear of precedent.

Even if the warehouse dispute itself is legitimate, unilateral armed seizure changes the category.

Likely sequence:

- Port demands withdrawal;
- other guarantor / interested powers pressure the actor;
- commercial access consequences;
- emergency arbitration / negotiation;
- coordinated resistance if occupation expands.

**Result: very strong pass.**

The rule "right to use Port ≠ right to rule Port" becomes enforceable through coalition interest.

---

# 17. Case test — Port labor strike

Dockworkers stop unloading one class of ships.

This is **not automatically** a Convergence violation.

Likely first layer:

- Port labor / local law;
- guild / employer dispute;
- local government.

It becomes an external-access question only if:

- Port government selectively suppresses one foreign power's access;
- an outside power claims strike disruption justifies military intervention;
- the strike is manipulated into discriminatory denial of treaty rights.

**Result: strong pass.**

Neutrality does not erase Port's internal politics.

---

# 18. Case test — false route-closure report

A roadhead falsely reports a major pass closed.

If caused by:

- honest error → local / commercial dispute;
- negligent verification → professional consequences;
- merchant manipulation → fraud;
- official political strategy → possible protected-passage issue.

**Result: pass.**

Information itself can become the object of enforcement.

---

# 19. Violation classes

A useful provisional classification:

## Class I — private / local breach

Handled locally unless enforcement fails across borders.

## Class II — cross-border dispute

Requires recognition, negotiation, or arbitration.

## Class III — systematic interference

Repeated behavior undermines protected exchange or attached agreements.

## Class IV — core-settlement challenge

Threatens recognized claims, Port neutrality, major protected routes, or the legitimacy of the settlement itself.

These are **design classes**, not established in-world legal names.

---

# 20. Remedies should match the violated interest

If the problem is:

- blocked passage → restore passage;
- unpaid debt → enforce or compensate;
- unsafe route → repair / secure;
- discriminatory access → remove discrimination;
- unlawful occupation → withdraw;
- false inspection → correct / compensate;
- ignored arbitration → impose recognition costs.

Avoid punishment disconnected from the actual problem unless politics pushes it there.

---

# 21. Overreaction is itself a threat

A polity can exploit a minor violation to justify:

- territorial seizure;
- embargo;
- troop deployment;
- political humiliation.

Other actors may oppose the overreaction even if they agree the original breach occurred.

This is important because:

> maintaining the settlement can require restraining both violators and self-appointed enforcers.

---

# 22. Selective compliance

Real actors will comply strategically.

A polity may:

- obey trade rulings;
- resist family-status recognition;
- honor Port neutrality;
- exploit ambiguity in toll rules;
- cooperate on anti-piracy;
- reject one guild privilege.

The settlement can therefore remain functional despite chronic disagreement.

Perfect consistency is unnecessary.

---

# 23. Enforcement creates precedent

Every major enforcement crisis can change later expectations.

A successful response may establish that:

- one kind of closure is unacceptable;
- one Port action counts as occupation;
- one route authority can certify closure;
- one arbitration forum is trusted.

Thus:

violation  
→ response  
→ precedent  
→ changed future behavior

This is Writing the Sequel applied to law.

---

# 24. Political legitimacy

Because the Convergence stabilizes recognized claims and access, rulers can gain legitimacy by presenting themselves as:

- reliable treaty actors;
- defenders of passage;
- protectors of neutral access;
- responsible custodians of shared routes.

They can also lose legitimacy through visible bad-faith violations.

This does not mean ordinary people care equally about every clause.

Legitimacy varies by constituency.

---

# 25. Council relevance

The Council does not need to formally enforce Convergence law.

It can influence escalation by:

- financing repair;
- extending or withdrawing credit;
- encouraging settlement;
- funding arbitration;
- moving supply;
- signaling commercial risk;
- pressuring client institutions.

This fits current Council canon.

A crisis should not become "the Council decides."

---

# 26. Villain relevance

The Villain can exploit the enforcement system by:

- provoking ambiguous incidents;
- making private acts look official;
- circulating false evidence;
- encouraging overreaction;
- targeting a rule whose enforcement splits allies;
- creating a breach that benefits from delayed verification.

This is stronger than simply attacking the treaty.

---

# 27. Wurdren relevance

Wurdren-level stories can sit below grand diplomacy.

Examples:

- escorting witnesses;
- discovering that a closure report is false;
- protecting a caravan while authorities argue over responsibility;
- helping people trapped by retaliatory restrictions;
- carrying evidence that changes how an incident is classified.

His local actions can matter because the enforcement system depends on credible facts.

---

# 28. Strong conclusions

The enforcement model passes if:

1. minor violations remain minor;
2. systematic obstruction can become politically expensive;
3. serious violations can trigger coalition response without a central sovereign;
4. arbitration matters without being omnipotent;
5. targeted restrictions exist below embargo / war;
6. intent, repetition, and official sponsorship change the category;
7. overreaction can itself threaten the settlement;
8. enforcement crises create precedent;
9. Port neutrality can be defended through overlapping interests rather than automatic military guarantees.

All nine pass under the current world model.

---

# 29. Still open

- exact formal complaint procedure;
- whether any permanent treaty secretariat exists;
- exact arbitration panels;
- who can formally declare a serious violation;
- exact suspension powers;
- exact accession / withdrawal mechanics;
- whether sanctions are coordinated formally or ad hoc;
- how quickly emergency consultations occur;
- whether any historical crisis created a famous enforcement precedent.

Those should be solved when a concrete historical or story crisis requires them.
