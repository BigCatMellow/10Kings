# Culture Phase 7 — Cross-Regional Cultural Transmission

## Status

**Reference / synthesis tests — not canon.**

This phase tests whether the culture system built in Phases 1–6 actually behaves like a connected world.

It does **not** establish the exact dish, song, garment, word, place-name history, game or ritual used below.

Each trace is a mechanism test.

The required question is not:

> Can this cultural thing appear in several regions?

It is:

> **Who carries it, along which established network, what changes at each stop, why does anyone keep it, and where does transmission fail?**

# 1. Transmission model

A cultural form moves only when several things line up.

```text
portable form
+ human carrier
+ repeated route
+ receiving use / desire
+ available materials or substitutes
+ social permission
+ repetition
→ local adoption
```

Then adoption can produce:

```text
local adoption
+ different audience
+ different material conditions
+ different institutions
+ new generation
→ transformed local form
```

This means geography alone is insufficient.

Two neighboring towns may exchange little if people rarely cross between them.

Two distant cities may share slang, fashion or games because sailors, guild members or caravan workers move constantly between them.

# 2. Carrier matters

Different people carry different kinds of culture.

## Sailors and harbor workers

Especially good carriers of:

- portable food;
- slang;
- songs;
- jokes;
- games;
- small objects;
- work clothing;
- religious practices connected to travel.

## Caravan workers and High Roads merchants

Especially good carriers of:

- trade vocabulary;
- portable games;
- travel food;
- clothing adaptations;
- route rituals;
- prices;
- stories;
- practical tools.

## Migrant laborers

Especially good carriers of:

- food techniques;
- work songs;
- repair methods;
- household customs;
- clothing;
- names;
- labor politics.

## Guild networks

Especially good carriers of:

- technical vocabulary;
- tool forms;
- certification customs;
- apprenticeship practice;
- status fashion;
- institutional ritual.

## Pilgrims and religious travelers

Especially good carriers of:

- stories;
- hospitality customs;
- ritual;
- songs;
- calendars;
- moral vocabulary;
- information about distant communities.

## Children

Especially good carriers of:

- games;
- rhymes;
- insults;
- dares;
- slang;
- fashions;
- simplified rituals.

## Itinerant households

Especially good carriers of:

- repair methods;
- songs;
- news;
- marriage links;
- route knowledge;
- portable crafts;
- stories;
- local variants accumulated across circuits.

No carrier transmits everything equally well.

# 3. Transmission cost

Cultural forms have different practical costs.

A useful working hierarchy is:

## Very low material cost

- words;
- jokes;
- simple songs;
- game rules;
- gestures;
- nicknames.

These can move after a single encounter, although language and social barriers still matter.

## Low to moderate material cost

- dance;
- clothing cuts;
- repair techniques;
- recipes using broadly available ingredients;
- household etiquette.

These require practice, materials or repeated contact.

## Moderate to high material cost

- cuisine dependent on specific ingredients;
- musical forms requiring specialized instruments;
- sports requiring space / equipment;
- architectural methods;
- elaborate public ritual.

These need supply chains, venues, institutions or sustained communities.

## Administrative transmission

Names, legal terms, measures and dates can spread rapidly when institutions require them even if ordinary people dislike or ignore the official form.

This creates a very different diffusion mechanism from fashion or food.

# 4. Trace A — food

## Provisional form

A workday preparation combining:

- preserved salted fish from a North Coast tradition;
- fresh or lightly preserved vegetables from Low Rivers market agriculture;
- a local souring / seasoning component;
- an inexpensive starch or bread-form available in the contact settlement.

The exact recipe and name remain unset.

## Origin condition

The form emerges in a long-lived mixed river / market settlement.

Necessary conditions:

- North Coast-descended households or workers have reliable preserved-fish knowledge;
- Low Rivers growers supply vegetables cheaply;
- mixed households or neighboring food sellers repeat the combination;
- the meal works as inexpensive labor food.

This is not created because someone wants "fusion."

It survives because it is practical.

## First movement — mixed market → Port

**Carrier:** traders, migrant workers, sailors with family connections.

In Port:

- preserved fish is easy to obtain through shipping;
- vegetables vary with season and import price;
- crowded kitchens favor food that can be assembled or cooked quickly;
- street vendors can sell it in portions.

The Port version may become:

- sharper;
- cheaper;
- more portable;
- served with a different starch;
- more condiment-heavy because small amounts of imported condiments are available.

Port-born children may know only the Port version.

## Second movement — Port → High Roads

**Carrier:** caravan provisioning and workers moving inland.

Problem:

fresh vegetables travel poorly.

Adaptation:

- more pickled / dried vegetable components;
- smaller amount of fish if maritime supply is expensive;
- local dairy, grain or legume component may take over some of the meal's substance;
- the dish becomes more portable.

At this point the High Roads version may no longer resemble the original closely in texture.

## Stop condition

The form weakens where:

- preserved fish is too expensive;
- local religious practice rejects the preparation;
- the necessary souring / preservation method is unfamiliar;
- another cheap worker meal already occupies the same niche.

The **idea** of combining preserved protein with sharp preserved vegetables may continue after the specific dish disappears.

## Return loop

A High Roads traveler later encounters the original contact-zone version and complains that it is "missing" the grain / dairy element normal in the route version.

That argument is evidence the borrowed form has become local culture.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This directly supports the cultural-contact and diaspora models.

# 5. Trace B — garment

## Provisional form

A durable work overshirt / jacket with:

- reinforced high-wear areas;
- replaceable patches or panels;
- practical fastening;
- tool attachment or pocketing appropriate to workshop labor.

Exact cut, fiber and name remain open.

## Origin condition — Stone Hills industrial district

The garment exists because:

- clothing is damaged by work;
- replacement is expensive;
- repair skill is common;
- some tools need to be carried;
- workshops and secondhand sellers can standardize useful modifications informally.

This is workwear, not regional costume.

## First movement — Stone Hills → Port

**Carrier:** migrant laborers and secondhand clothing.

Port's secondhand market separates the garment from its original occupation.

Tailors and repairers notice:

- the reinforced cut lasts;
- some pocket / fastening features are useful to dockworkers;
- lighter imported cloth can reproduce the shape without the original heavy material.

The Port version becomes recognizable without being identical.

## Second movement — Port → High Roads

**Carrier:** warehouse workers, caravan hands, merchants buying durable secondhand garments.

High Roads adaptation may favor:

- different layering;
- easier riding / walking movement;
- pockets or closures suited to documents, route tokens or small tools;
- repair using locally available cloth.

The garment has now moved from workshop wear into travel wear.

## Prestige movement — Port / High Roads → Old Cities

A merchant or young elite encounters the practical garment and commissions a cleaner, expensive version.

Prestige adaptation may:

- retain the silhouette;
- remove ugly reinforcement;
- use expensive cloth;
- exaggerate one closure or pocket;
- make a work feature decorative.

This produces an authenticity argument.

Workers may regard the fashionable version as absurd because the expensive version removed the thing that made the garment useful.

## Return loop

Cheap manufacturers or tailors then copy the prestigious version back into Port.

Port now contains:

- old practical work versions;
- modified dock versions;
- route versions;
- fashionable imitation versions.

No one can honestly point to one "authentic" form anymore.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This gives [Material Culture Supply Chains](Material-Culture-Supply-Chains.md) a complete circulation loop rather than a one-direction fashion model.

# 6. Trace C — word

## Provisional semantic item

A High Roads trade-register term meaning roughly:

> a person or institution that stands behind a stranger's promise / obligation.

The actual word is deliberately **not coined** because language families remain unresolved.

## Origin condition — High Roads

Multilingual trade and arbitration create a practical need to distinguish:

- the person making a promise;
- the person reporting it;
- the person guaranteeing it;
- the conditions under which responsibility transfers.

A compact technical term becomes useful.

## First movement — High Roads → Port

**Carrier:** merchants, clerks, arbitrators, caravan guilds.

Port adopts the term into commercial / legal speech because the same problem exists there.

Local clerks may adjust pronunciation or spelling.

## Semantic broadening — Port

Outside contracts, Port workers begin using the word informally for:

- someone who introduces you to an employer;
- someone who vouches for a boarder;
- a person who gets you into a guild hall;
- a neighborhood contact who says you can be trusted.

The technical term becomes slang.

## Second movement — Port → maritime / guild networks

**Carrier:** sailors, dockworkers, traveling guild members.

In another city, people may learn only the slang sense.

They may not realize it began as a precise High Roads legal term.

## Return loop

Young High Roads workers hear the Port slang version and start using it jokingly.

Older clerks complain that the word is being used incorrectly.

## Stop condition

The term may fail to enter communities where:

- a local equivalent already has high prestige;
- the concept is handled through kinship rather than contract;
- Port slang carries low status;
- pronunciation makes the borrowed form unstable.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This demonstrates how technical vocabulary can become ordinary identity language.

# 7. Trace D — name

## Provisional form

Use **Port** itself as the mechanism test.

The current naming owner already treats "Port" as a plausible plain international shorthand.

This trace does **not** establish Port's lost original name.

## Layer 1 — older local geography

Before Port becomes an international institution, the harbor / settlement plausibly has one or more local names.

Those names remain UNKNOWN.

## Layer 2 — commercial shorthand

Merchants repeatedly refer to the strategically important place by function:

> the port

In multilingual commerce, a translated or standardized common form is useful.

Repeated international use can turn description into proper noun.

## Layer 3 — legal stabilization

Treaties, contracts, guild records and maps increasingly use a standardized equivalent.

The functional exonym can become more internationally stable than older local forms.

## Layer 4 — local response

Port-born people may:

- use Port proudly;
- use an older district / local name in intimate speech;
- distinguish "Port" the legal city from a neighborhood identity;
- mock outsiders for calling every part of the urban area simply Port.

## Layer 5 — export

Elsewhere, phrases derived from Port can develop meanings such as:

- Port-style contract;
- Port cut;
- Port speech;
- Port price;
- Port-born.

The place-name becomes a cultural adjective.

## Stop condition

Older local names can survive where:

- families retain them;
- religious sites preserve them;
- neighborhood identity resists commercial standardization;
- political movements revive them.

**Result: STRONG PASS.**

The test works without inventing a false ancient etymology and gives the simple name historical depth.

# 8. Trace E — song

## Provisional form

A humorous caravan / work song about a corrupt or incompetent toll official.

No exact lyrics, melody, title or historical official is canon.

## Origin condition — High Roads

The form works because:

- caravan crews wait at gates;
- toll disputes are repetitive;
- workers from many places understand the frustration;
- verse can be added or replaced easily.

## First movement — High Roads route

Different crews alter verses to name:

- another gate;
- another official;
- another town;
- another type of petty corruption.

The refrain survives longer than the details.

## Second movement — Port

**Carrier:** overland merchants and caravan workers.

Port performers adapt the target:

- toll official becomes dock inspector;
- road delay becomes warehouse delay;
- route jokes become harbor jokes.

The audience may never have visited the original High Roads gate.

## Third movement — Old Cities performance culture

A performer or patron turns the crude song into a staged satire.

Changes may include:

- polished language;
- multiple characters;
- fashionable instrumentation;
- removal of obscure route references.

The prestige version becomes famous.

## Return loop

High Roads crews hear the respectable version and parody it by restoring vulgar or local verses.

The "original" now exists in several incompatible forms.

## Stop condition

Lyrics fail where language does not travel well.

But:

- melody;
- rhythm;
- comic structure;
- refrain pattern

may continue without the original words.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This supports the distinction between cultural incubation, circulation and prestige.

# 9. Trace F — game

## Provisional form

A compact counting / placement game using small interchangeable pieces.

Exact board, rules and name remain open.

## Origin condition — caravan network

The game is useful because:

- it is portable;
- it fills waiting time;
- pieces can be improvised;
- two people can learn quickly.

## High Roads yard

Workers develop one standard-ish version because strangers need mutually understandable rules.

Wagering may attach in some yards.

## Port

Dockworkers alter:

- scoring;
- pace;
- wagering;
- slang.

Cheap vendors may sell simple boards.

## North Coast

Sailors carry the Port version into harbors.

A shipboard version changes rules because:

- space is limited;
- pieces must not slide easily;
- games may need to pause abruptly for work.

## Low Rivers

River traders or returning workers introduce it to market towns.

Children simplify the rules and play with stones, seeds, scraps or other locally available counters.

Adults later insist the children's version is "wrong."

## Prestige branch

A wealthy household commissions a decorative board.

The elite version can look materially different while using essentially the same rule family.

## Stop condition

The game has unusually low transmission cost.

It is more likely to transform than disappear.

What may stop instead is:

- wagering;
- mixed-gender play;
- public play;
- child participation;

depending on local rules.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This strongly supports low-infrastructure popular culture as a fast trans-regional layer.

# 10. Trace G — ritual

## Provisional form

A departure practice associated with long-distance travel.

Possible minimal structure:

- shared food or drink before departure;
- explicit statement of destination / intended route;
- named person or institution expected to receive the traveler;
- small gesture of safe-return expectation.

No exact prayer, object or wording is canon.

## Origin condition

The practice can grow where long-distance travel is dangerous and social networks matter.

The [Infinite Compass](../Politics/Religions.md) is a plausible religious carrier because pilgrimage, hospitality and travelers are already among its institutional interests.

But the practice need not begin there exclusively.

## First movement — pilgrimage / High Roads

Pilgrims and caravan households repeat the practice because it has practical and emotional value.

The route / host declaration helps people know:

- where someone intended to go;
- who expected them;
- where to send news if they disappear.

## Port

Mixed households adopt a shortened version for sailors, migrants and seasonal workers.

Some families keep the faith language.

Others keep only:

- meal;
- destination statement;
- farewell gesture.

The ritual becomes partly secular.

## Diaspora effect

A family living abroad may preserve an older elaborate version because departure is tied strongly to identity.

Meanwhile the home community simplifies it.

A century later the diaspora version may look "more traditional" than the homeland version despite having changed in its own ways.

## Crisis test

During route attacks or suspicious ship losses, the practice becomes more emotionally charged and administratively useful.

Guilds or faith institutions may begin recording departures formally.

That can turn private ritual into information infrastructure.

## Stop condition

The full ritual weakens where:

- travel becomes routine;
- households are too fragmented;
- authorities replace private route knowledge with reliable records;
- another faith or local custom occupies the same social role.

But a small farewell gesture may survive long after the original explanation is forgotten.

**Result: VERY STRONG PASS.**

This supports the Phase 6 finding that ritual can persist after its original meaning changes.

# 11. Network comparison

| Form | Primary carriers | Material dependence | Fastest likely network | Main transformation pressure | Likely stop condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food | migrants, vendors, sailors | medium / high | mixed markets, Port | ingredients, fuel, price | unavailable / expensive components |
| Garment | workers, secondhand sellers, tailors | medium | labor migration, Port resale, caravans | climate, work, class, fashion | impractical material / cut |
| Word | merchants, clerks, workers | very low | High Roads + Port + guilds | pronunciation, semantic broadening | strong local equivalent / low prestige |
| Name | clerks, merchants, law, maps | administrative | treaties / trade records | translation, shorthand, politics | local resistance / revived name |
| Song | workers, performers, travelers | low | caravan / harbor / itinerant networks | audience, language, satire | language barrier |
| Game | children, workers, travelers | very low | nearly any repeated travel | rule drift, wagering, equipment | social prohibition more than material |
| Ritual | households, faiths, guilds, migrants | medium / institutional | pilgrimage / migration networks | belief, law, family structure | competing ritual / loss of social function |

The comparison suggests that "culture spreads along trade routes" is too broad by itself.

The better question is:

> **Which carrier on that route can transport this particular cultural form?**

# 12. Contact zones do different work

## Border towns

Best at:

- long-term household synthesis;
- bilingualism;
- new food;
- marriage custom;
- local law;
- shared work practices.

They create culture through **continuous ordinary coexistence**.

## Port

Best at:

- dense encounter;
- secondhand circulation;
- scenes;
- slang;
- migrant adaptation;
- rapid copying;
- large audiences.

Port creates culture through **density and repeated recombination**.

## High Roads

Best at:

- transmission;
- translation;
- portable forms;
- trade vocabulary;
- games;
- route ritual.

The High Roads create culture through **movement and repeated recontextualization**.

## Itinerant networks

Best at:

- carrying variants between places that otherwise have weak direct contact;
- preserving stories;
- accumulating local additions;
- transmitting repair / craft practice;
- carrying kin and marriage connections.

They create culture through **circuits rather than hubs**.

## Pilgrimage

Best at:

- ritual;
- hospitality;
- moral vocabulary;
- story;
- calendar-linked movement.

Pilgrimage creates culture through **repeated purposeful travel**.

## Guild branches

Best at:

- technical vocabulary;
- certification;
- occupational dress;
- tools;
- apprenticeship;
- welfare custom.

Guilds create culture through **institutional replication**.

## Refugee / labor migration

Best at:

- household food;
- language;
- names;
- religious practice;
- repair;
- work customs.

Migration creates culture through **rebuilding ordinary life somewhere else**.

# 13. Where culture stops

A believable world needs failed transmission.

Cultural forms can stop because of:

- ecology;
- price;
- unavailable materials;
- language;
- law;
- religion;
- class stigma;
- gender rules where locally established;
- guild restriction;
- weak routes;
- dangerous travel;
- lack of venue;
- lack of free time;
- strong local alternatives;
- political hostility;
- deliberate rejection.

Rejection can still leave traces.

A city that bans a game may preserve its vocabulary.

A guild that rejects a garment may copy one fastening.

A religious community that refuses a ritual meal may preserve the date.

Cultural borders are therefore often **selective filters**, not walls.

# 14. Return loops

Phase 7 repeatedly produces a useful pattern:

```text
origin
→ migration / route
→ adaptation
→ wider circulation
→ prestige or institutional adoption
→ return to origin
→ authenticity dispute
```

Return influence matters because culture is not a one-way export.

A homeland can be changed by its diaspora.

Workers can copy elites who originally copied workers.

A technical term can return as slang.

A local song can return as prestigious theater.

This is likely one of the strongest ways to make cultural history feel alive.

# 15. Cross-phase findings

## Finding 1 — proximity is weaker than repeated contact

The cultural map should not be predicted from physical adjacency alone.

Repeated movement can make distant places culturally closer than neighboring settlements.

## Finding 2 — carrier-specific networks matter

Trade routes are social routes, but **the traveler matters as much as the road**.

A pilgrim, child, sailor, guild clerk and migrant laborer use overlapping geography while transmitting different things.

## Finding 3 — cultural forms have transmission costs

A joke can cross the world without a supply chain.

A cuisine cannot.

An architectural method needs labor and materials.

A legal name can spread because institutions impose it.

This creates different cultural geographies for different domains.

## Finding 4 — contact zones are producers, not just receivers

Border towns, Port, caravan settlements and migrant districts do not merely receive outside culture.

They generate forms that later travel outward.

## Finding 5 — return influence is normal

Diaspora and borrowing should sometimes alter the source culture.

A culture that only exports and never changes from contact feels artificially sealed.

## Finding 6 — authenticity arguments are historical evidence

Arguments over the "real" version often indicate:

- migration;
- class change;
- patronage;
- generational change;
- commercialization;
- religious divergence;
- return influence.

They should not be resolved by the narrator.

# 17. Promotion discipline

The traces above prove that the **system can generate cultural movement**.

They do not prove that:

- the fish dish exists;
- the work jacket exists;
- the unnamed High Roads guarantor term exists;
- Port definitely acquired its name in exactly this way;
- the toll song exists;
- the counting game exists;
- the departure ritual exists in the described form.

Those remain examples until a story, local history or later owner deliberately establishes them.

What can be promoted safely is the mechanism:

> Cultural diffusion depends on the form's transmission cost, the people carrying it, the route they actually use, and the receiving community's material and social conditions.

---

# 18. Phase 7 result

**STRONG PASS.**

The culture model does not require hard borders to remain legible.

Distinctiveness can emerge from:

- different network positions;
- different carriers;
- different material constraints;
- different institutions;
- different histories of borrowing;
- different return loops.

That is preferable to preserving cultural distinctiveness by isolating regions from one another.

The next phase should therefore be [Phase 8 — Breath](Culture-Deepening-Plan.md): convert the working systems into small scene-level details without canonizing an encyclopedia.
