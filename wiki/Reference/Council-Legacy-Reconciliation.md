# 1. What survived the legacy review

The strongest legacy Council material is:

- the Council as a **cross-border economic coordination system**, not a second government with better uniforms;
- families / interests whose wealth and institutional memory outlast individual rulers;
- control of **bottlenecks and dependencies**, not literal ownership of whole economies;
- indirect pressure through contracts, credit, routing, storage, standards, investment and access;
- front organizations, intermediaries and plausible deniability;
- emergency aid that creates long-term dependence;
- incremental policy pressure rather than constant spectacular sabotage;
- genuine belief that coordination prevented recurrence of pre-Convergence catastrophe;
- internal disagreement over how much coercion is justified;
- the danger that overreach causes kingdoms, guilds or rival financiers to build alternatives;
- secrecy itself becoming a strategic weakness;
- succession and generational drift;
- the Council needing information from ordinary institutions rather than possessing omniscience.

These mechanisms are compatible with current [World Rules](../World-Rules.md).

# 2. The Council as an institutional memory

Legacy profiles repeatedly return to one useful emotional idea:

> Some Council families remember, or inherit stories of, the chaos that preceded the present order and sincerely believe that their coordination prevents famine, war or economic collapse.

That does not make them correct.

It explains why members can experience:

- pride in preserving stability;
- guilt over people harmed by their interventions;
- fear of what happens if they stop;
- contempt for rulers they regard as short-sighted;
- anxiety that exposure will destroy useful systems along with illegitimate control.

The Council should contain people who disagree about whether the institution has:

- remained necessary;
- become self-serving;
- confused stability with stagnation;
- accumulated too much authority;
- failed to update assumptions inherited from earlier generations.

This is stronger than a uniformly cynical cabal.

# 3. Operational layers

The old notes propose several useful layers between a Council family and an outcome.

Possible layers include:

```text
Council family / domain
→ investment house / trading company / estate / consortium
→ contract or financing relationship
→ guild / merchant / ruler / local authority
→ ordinary market consequence
```

Other operations may involve:

- agents;
- accountants;
- factors;
- scribes;
- lawyers / contract specialists where locally supported;
- guild allies;
- warehouse operators;
- shipping brokers;
- lenders;
- patrons;
- informants.

The exact institutional names remain open.

The important rule is:

> Most people affected by a Council decision should not receive an order stamped "THE COUNCIL."

They experience:

- a rate increase;
- unavailable credit;
- a delayed repair;
- a warehouse refusing a contract;
- a preferred buyer;
- a new insurance condition;
- a suddenly attractive alternative route.

# 4. Aid with strings

One of the strongest legacy mechanisms is Council intervention during crisis.

A family or affiliated network can provide:

- grain;
- seed;
- transport;
- medicine;
- rebuilding finance;
- engineering;
- emergency credit.

The assistance may be genuinely lifesaving.

The problem comes later.

Conditions can create:

- exclusive contracts;
- debt;
- preferred access;
- political obligations;
- control of storage;
- dependency on replacement parts or specialist labor;
- influence over future policy.

This creates morally difficult situations because refusing Council assistance can also hurt ordinary people.

---

# 5. Gradual influence

Legacy stress testing correctly identifies that dramatic punishment exposes the system.

The Council is more plausible when pressure accumulates through:

- small tariff changes;
- altered credit terms;
- licensing;
- priority access;
- resource quotas;
- shipping schedules;
- purchasing agreements;
- delayed investment;
- differential risk pricing.

No one measure needs to be decisive.

The cumulative environment can make one option much easier than another.

This supports the current owner's idea of **choice architecture**.

---

# 6. Use real crises rather than creating every crisis

The legacy stress test contains a particularly useful correction:

The Council should often exploit or redirect **real events** rather than secretly causing everything.

Examples include:

- drought;
- crop disease;
- bridge damage;
- mine exhaustion;
- piracy;
- war scares;
- epidemic;
- succession uncertainty;
- labor unrest.

The Council's intervention may:

- mitigate;
- exploit;
- redirect;
- selectively relieve;
- worsen through miscalculation.

This preserves an independent world.

The Council is less believable if every bad harvest, pirate attack and strike was secretly engineered.

---

# 7. Information is a dependency, not omniscience

Older notes frequently overstate Council information control.

The salvageable mechanism is narrower.

Council-linked people may influence:

- copying;
- patronage;
- distribution;
- commercial records;
- market reports;
- technical licenses;
- maps;
- access to archives;
- informants.

They cannot guarantee:

- no unauthorized copying;
- no oral transmission;
- no independent scholarship;
- no leaks;
- no false reports;
- no local records;
- no rival intelligence.

Their information system should produce **blind spots and bad data** as well as advantage.

# 8. Internal structure

The source contains several incompatible Council structures.

The current six-domain model is stronger than the older region-coded "Dominus" system.

Useful legacy principles:

- domains specialize;
- major decisions require coordination;
- families can form temporary coalitions;
- one domain's preferred solution can damage another;
- member interests extend beyond the formal Council;
- heirs may disagree with founders;
- local branches / clients can act without complete central knowledge.

Exact:

- number of seats;
- titles;
- voting rules;
- succession;
- veto structure;
- meeting procedure

remain unresolved.

Do not restore old structures merely because they were detailed.

---

# 9. Succession is a major unresolved mechanism

Several legacy versions experiment with:

- hereditary family control;
- guild nomination;
- expertise-based appointment;
- replacement for negligence;
- internal election;
- wealth / ownership qualification.

None is cleanly established.

The useful question is:

> **What makes a person entitled to exercise a dependency domain after the previous holder dies or fails?**

Succession can expose contradictions between:

- expertise;
- property;
- family inheritance;
- guild legitimacy;
- Council continuity.

This should be developed deliberately rather than inherited from the old Dominus profiles.

# 10. How the Council fails

The legacy stress test is especially valuable here.

## Alternate supply chains

A kingdom, city, guild coalition or new merchant network can weaken Council leverage by developing:

- another source;
- another route;
- substitute materials;
- independent credit;
- local expertise;
- shared reserves.

This should be one of the most important limits on Council power.

## Coalition risk

The Council benefits when rivals interpret pressure separately.

Its position becomes much weaker if several actors compare notes.

Possible challengers:

- several kingdoms;
- guild coalitions;
- religious networks;
- Port institutions;
- rival financiers;
- merchant alliances;
- public investigators.

## Internal rivalry

Council domains can disagree over:

- short-term profit vs. stability;
- relief vs. debt collection;
- embargo vs. open trade;
- secrecy vs. selective transparency;
- innovation vs. protecting existing investments.

## Exposure

The institution's usefulness does not guarantee legitimacy.

Exposure can cause:

- public anger;
- contract repudiation;
- asset seizure;
- rulers building competing systems;
- guild leaders defecting;
- internal blame;
- panic if useful coordination suddenly stops.

## Overreach

Every overt coercive act creates evidence that apparently unrelated economic pressures are connected.

The Council therefore has a built-in reason **not** to use maximum leverage constantly.

# 11. The "Circle of Mirrors"

Legacy proposes a **Circle of Mirrors**: critics from different backgrounds whom the Council consults, sometimes indirectly, to expose blind spots.

The exact named characters are not promoted.

The underlying idea is good.

A large coordinating institution needs access to perspectives it does not naturally possess:

- historian;
- ordinary merchant / worker perspective;
- ethical critic;
- military/security critic;
- cultural interpreter;
- technical specialist;
- political dissident.

Possible current interpretations:

1. a deliberate internal red-team system;
2. informal advisors retained by individual families;
3. commissioned reports whose authors do not know the true client;
4. independent people whose work the Council quietly monitors;
5. a historical practice that has decayed.

This is worth preserving because it provides a mechanism for the Council to be sophisticated **without being omniscient**.

It can also fail when leaders ignore unwelcome feedback.

# 12. Legacy domain models

The source contains several older domain schemes:

- Flame / Frost / Stone / Sky / Life / Night;
- Iron & War;
- Commerce & Passage;
- Masonry & Expansion;
- Insight & Innovation;
- Harvest & Healing;
- Shadows & Secrets;
- later Trade / Finance / Infrastructure / Agriculture / Labor / Knowledge formulations.

These are design history.

Current owner domains:

- Food and Biological Supply;
- Materials and Production;
- Routes and Transport;
- Infrastructure;
- Finance and Exchange;
- Knowledge and Information

are currently stronger because they describe dependencies without claiming ownership of entire social sectors.

Useful names from the old domain schemes can remain as:

- nicknames;
- internal historical terminology;
- abandoned proposals;
- titles used in one era;
- external rumors.

They are not the current structural owner.

# 13. Legacy family-name seed bank

Older drafts propose family names including:

- **Veynor**
- **Luthain**
- **Torradon**

and other region-derived candidates.

Preserve these as **name quarry only**.

The old family draft was built around superseded regional/cultural naming assumptions and should not establish:

- origin;
- personality;
- clothing;
- speech;
- ethnicity;
- current Council seat ownership.

Any family name promoted later must pass the current [Naming](../Culture/Naming.md) framework.

# 14. Things to reject or heavily demote

Do not restore:

- "the Council decides who prospers, starves, rises and falls" as literal total control;
- monopoly over all innovation;
- perfect surveillance;
- hidden armies as a default solution;
- assassinations as routine administrative tools;
- magical R&D laboratories unless the magic baseline later supports them;
- steam factories or other technology beyond current World Rules;
- one Council family mechanically assigned to each old region;
- a Council response that always works;
- rulers who never identify dependencies;
- guilds that obey because the Council simply owns them.

The Council is strongest when it is dangerous because the system often works, **not because resistance is impossible**.

# 15. Council scene test

For a Council intervention, ask:

1. Which dependency is involved?
2. Which intermediary actually acts?
3. What legitimate business reason can they give?
4. Who benefits besides the Council?
5. Who loses?
6. What information might be wrong?
7. What substitute could reduce the pressure?
8. Which Council member would disagree?
9. What evidence does the intervention leave?
10. Why doesn't the Council simply use more force?
11. What happens if the target refuses?
12. How can the Council's intervention accidentally strengthen a rival?

If the answer is "the Council controls it," the mechanism is incomplete.
