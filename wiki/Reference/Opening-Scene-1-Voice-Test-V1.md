# Prose test

By the time Port came into view, Wurdren had decided that guarding grain was worse than guarding sheep.

Sheep at least had opinions.

The sacks had spent three days stacked under tarred cloth in the belly of the riverboat, saying nothing while Wurdren watched them, counted them, watched the banks, counted the crew, watched the banks again, and tried not to wonder whether this was what returning to adventure looked like.

The river widened slowly. Warehouses began appearing before the city did—long roofs along muddy quays, timber stages pushed over the water, cranes and hoists standing above them like thin-necked birds. Beyond those came the roadheads and ferry landings, then taller buildings crowded together across channels and low banks. Masts rose behind them in numbers that made the riverboats around Wurdren look like toys someone had forgotten in a washbasin.

Port had grown.

Or perhaps he had shrunk.

He disliked the thought enough to stand.

His left knee had stiffened during the morning. He let it straighten before putting weight on it, carefully enough that nobody nearby could call the movement careful. The youngest deckhand noticed anyway and reached toward his elbow.

Wurdren looked at the hand.

The boy withdrew it.

"Wasn't offering," the boy said.

"Good."

"I was pointing."

"At my elbow?"

"At Port."

Wurdren looked where the boy pointed.

"Seen it."

The boy grinned. "Not like this."

That, unfortunately, was true.

Three Moons had not begun, not properly, but Port already looked as though half the continent had arrived early to complain that the other half was late. Boats sat two and three deep off some quays. Temporary awnings covered stretches of open ground that Wurdren remembered as cart yards. Men on one landing were carrying benches inland while men on the next were carrying benches toward the water. A crew on a roof was still hanging festival cloth while a woman below shouted that they had hung it crooked. Nobody on the roof appeared troubled by this information.

At the river quay ahead of them, a painted board directed arriving cargo to four different lanes.

Someone had crossed out two.

A second board pointed elsewhere.

Someone had crossed that out too.

The boatmaster spat over the side.

"Festival," he said.

Wurdren had heard the word used to explain higher prices, missing beds, late departures, a stolen harness, one fistfight, and a woman from the Low Rivers marrying a man she had known for four hours.

It was proving versatile.

The grain agent came up from below carrying the leather folio she had checked every morning since they left the upper river market.

"We're still expected at the same warehouse," she said.

Wurdren nodded.

"You don't know that."

"I know you asked me yesterday."

"I asked if you knew where it was."

"And I did."

"You said you remembered where it was."

"That is generally how knowing where something is works."

She looked toward the city. "Does it?"

He followed her gaze.

A new line of sheds had appeared where he remembered a tavern yard. One quay had been extended into the channel. A stone storehouse he could have sworn stood almost against the water now sat behind two newer buildings. Somewhere inland, bells were ringing, though whether for work, worship, warning, or festival preparation Wurdren could not tell.

"Mostly," he said.

The agent made a sound that meant she had won something.

Wurdren sat again.

The job had seemed respectable enough when he took it. A small Low Rivers seller had grain in Port, or would have grain in Port if someone could get it there intact and with the papers intact too. The agent needed another trustworthy hand for the last stretch because Three Moons traffic made theft, substitution, and simple disappearance easier than usual. Wurdren needed money and wanted the road.

It had sounded like a beginning.

Three days later it sounded like grain.

That was unfair to the grain, he supposed.

The sacks were feeding somebody.

The money mattered to the people who had grown what was inside them.

The agent had spent most of yesterday doing figures in the margin of a contract and had stopped smiling around noon.

All of that was true.

It was also true that no child had ever begged an old man to tell again the story of the time he had successfully accompanied one hundred and something sacks of milling grain downriver without incident.

Wurdren frowned.

He could not remember whether there were one hundred and something sacks or two hundred and something.

That seemed like information a legendary escort should probably know.

He leaned toward the hold.

"How many sacks?"

The grain agent stared at him.

"You signed the count."

"Yes."

"You watched us load them."

"Yes."

"You made us recount them."

"That was prudent."

"And now you don't know."

"I know approximately."

Her expression sharpened. "Approximately is a dangerous word around grain."

Wurdren smiled despite himself.

"There," she said. "Now I know you remember."

He did.

He had only wanted to check that she did.

That explanation was available to him if needed.

They reached the quay shortly after midday.

Nothing happened quickly after that.

First they waited for room to tie up.

Then they waited for two carts to move.

Then a quay man told them their warehouse receiving mark was wrong for the lane they had entered.

The boatmaster produced the mark.

The quay man examined it.

"Mark's right," he said.

The grain agent closed her eyes.

"But not here."

"Where?"

He pointed across the transfer yards.

"That lane."

"That board says river cargo comes here."

"It did this morning."

"What changed?"

"River cargo."

He had the weary confidence of a man who had spent several days answering questions whose answers had become false between breakfast and lunch.

They untied.

They moved perhaps the length of a house.

They tied again.

Wurdren began to understand why Port had needed more clerks.

By the time they reached the correct receiving place, the grain agent had checked her folio twice more.

A warehouse runner took the top paper, looked at the seal, looked at the boat, then looked back at the paper.

"No."

The agent blinked. "No what?"

"No room."

"We reserved room."

"You reserved room there."

He tapped the warehouse mark.

"Yes."

"There's no room there."

"We were told yesterday—"

"Yesterday had room."

"We have a reservation."

"You have a reservation."

"You just said there's no room."

"There isn't."

Wurdren watched the agent's jaw tighten.

The runner had the expression of a man who knew this conversation by heart and liked none of the endings.

The agent opened the folio and pulled another sheet free.

"Then put us in overflow."

"Overflow isn't covered."

"Covered by what?"

"Your guarantee."

She stared at him.

He pointed at a line on the document.

"Storage has to be recognized under the same terms. Yard overflow isn't. South transfer can take it, maybe, but they're under different measures and their receipt won't satisfy this unless somebody endorses the change."

"Somebody who?"

"Not me."

"I gathered that."

The runner handed back the paper.

Behind Wurdren, the first sack was already being lifted toward the quay.

The runner saw it.

"Don't."

The laborer stopped.

"Put that back."

The sack settled onto the boat again.

The grain agent looked at Wurdren.

Wurdren looked at the runner.

The runner looked toward the growing line of boats behind them.

"I can tell you where to ask," he said.

The grain agent laughed once, without humor.

"Of course you can."

Wurdren had been paid to get her and the shipment to Port.

They were in Port.

They were tied to the right quay.

The warehouse had seen the papers.

There were several perfectly reasonable versions of the contract in which his work was now finished.

He could collect his witness mark, find the guild hiring rooms, and spend the afternoon looking for something worth doing.

Something difficult.

Something dangerous.

Something that, when he remembered it years later—if he had years later—would feel less like a line in somebody else's account book.

The grain agent was still staring at the papers.

"If we sit here?" Wurdren asked.

"Berth fee."

"And if you move?"

"Another fee."

"And if you can't store it today?"

She looked at him.

"Then I sell badly, store badly, or carry it back upriver."

"Can you afford any of those?"

"No."

The answer was so immediate that he felt embarrassed for asking.

The boats behind them were beginning to shout.

Wurdren looked inland, toward the direction the runner had pointed.

"Where do we ask?"

The runner gave him the name of an office Wurdren did not recognize.

Of course he didn't.

Port had grown.

"Far?"

"Depends how many people are asking before you."

"That many?"

The runner looked past him at the harbor.

"Festival."

Wurdren sighed.

The grain agent began gathering her papers.

He could still leave.

Instead he stepped onto the quay.

"Come on," he said. "Before somebody decides the office has moved."

# Test notes

## What this version is trying to establish without explanation

- Wurdren has intentionally returned to the road.
- He is dissatisfied with ordinary usefulness.
- Age / mortality are present but not melodramatic.
- His actual character already contradicts his heroic scoreboard.
- Port feels physically busy before political exposition begins.
- Three Moons functions as ordinary logistical pressure.
- the first problem is capacity + paperwork + guarantee, not villainy.
- humor exists inside bureaucracy / travel.

## Deliberate omissions

- no Villain;
- no Council;
- no named recurring secondary character yet;
- no exposition on Port neutrality;
- no regional political summary;
- no explanation of Wurdren's whole past;
- no exact grain species;
- no exact warehouse legal terminology.

## Voice questions for later evaluation

1. Is Wurdren too quippy?
2. Is the prose too modern in cadence?
3. Does the significance-regret register clearly without being overexplained?
4. Does Port feel vivid enough before named districts exist?
5. Does the grain problem create enough forward pull for a first chapter?
6. Should the first scene end here, or continue through meeting the Records Worker?
