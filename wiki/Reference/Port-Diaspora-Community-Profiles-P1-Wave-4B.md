# 1. D-1 — North Coast-descended Port neighborhood

## Formation

This community develops through several migration waves rather than one founding event.

Plausible streams:

- sailors who stay ashore;
- widowed / displaced fishing households;
- seasonal workers who settle;
- ship / rope / repair workers;
- relatives joining established households.

The first settlement pattern likely follows:

- cheap harbor-adjacent housing;
- North Coast-linked employers;
- fish / provisioning markets;
- existing kin.

## First generation

Likely characteristics:

- strong harbor / island / town-of-origin identification;
- regular remittances / visits;
- familiar food and mourning customs;
- occupational concentration around maritime work;
- strong use of North Coast speech forms at home.

Some first-generation residents may still think:

> Port is where we work.

not:

> Port is home.

## Second generation

The transition becomes more interesting.

Port-born children may:

- speak Port street forms better than ancestral dialect;
- work outside maritime occupations;
- marry into other Port households;
- know North Coast customs through grandparents;
- visit a coast / island they have never lived in.

They may be treated as:

- North Coast in Port;
- Port people on the North Coast.

That tension is culturally productive.

## Household pattern

Likely mix:

- old migrant grandparent;
- Port-born adult children;
- unrelated boarder;
- sailor relative;
- mixed-background spouse.

This overlaps P-1 harbor-worker households but has a stronger ancestry / kin-route layer.

## Institutions

Possible:

- burial / sea-loss fund;
- mutual-aid society;
- faith house with North Coast roots;
- lodging contacts for new arrivals;
- fish / shipping business networks;
- social / song gathering.

Over time these institutions may serve people who no longer work at sea.

## Food transformation

The strongest pattern:

> North Coast food becomes Port food through urban constraint.

Possible changes:

- preservation foods become cheap dock meals;
- ingredients substitute through Port markets;
- dishes become more heavily seasoned / sauced through contact;
- street vendors sell simplified versions to non-North-Coast customers.

Third generation may insist the Port version is the normal one.

## Myth transformation

### Great Mouth

First generation:
- real / occupational North Coast story.

Second generation:
- family story + Port illustration.

Third generation:
- knows festival / children's version first.

Older relatives can complain:

> Port ruined the story.

But the Port version is now authentically Port culture.

### Road Hound

Can merge with:

- harbor-loss / sailor-return traditions;
- broader Port child version.

## Internal divisions

- recent arrival vs old Port family;
- maritime worker vs nonmaritime descendants;
- people preserving ancestral faith / language vs mixed households;
- wealthier shipping / merchant branch vs labor households.

Do not make "North Coast-descended" one class.

## Current-story role

This community can split over convoy / piracy politics.

Some may:

- strongly favor maritime protection;
- resent North Coast state interference;
- support Port autonomy precisely because their ancestors came from elsewhere.

That last point matters:

> diaspora identity can strengthen Port political identity rather than weaken it.

## P1 result

**STRONG PASS.**

# 2. D-2 — High Roads merchant / clerk network in Port

## Formation

This is less a single neighborhood at first and more a **circulating professional network**.

Migration / residence may include:

- merchant factors;
- clerks;
- interpreters;
- arbitration specialists;
- carrier brokers;
- family representatives;
- students / apprentices.

Some settle permanently.

Others rotate.

## First generation

Strong ties to:

- HR-C;
- one route polity;
- merchant family / partnership;
- caravan network.

Their most valuable resource is:

> trustworthy information.

They know:

- who shipped;
- what arrived;
- what failed to arrive;
- which route closed;
- which contract is disputed.

## Port institutions

Possible local institutions:

- letter / courier house;
- merchant lodging;
- trusted witness circle;
- remittance / credit office;
- family / faith charity;
- document-copying / translation services.

These need not all belong to one formal "High Roads quarter."

## Second generation

Port-born descendants may move into:

- court work;
- guild administration;
- shipping finance;
- warehouse records;
- municipal clerk roles.

Their inherited advantage may be:

- multilingualism;
- cross-route kin;
- contract literacy.

But new generations can also resent being expected to stay inside family commerce.

## Cultural transformation

High Roads practices become Port-specific.

Examples of mechanisms:

- route-contract phrasing enters Port commercial slang;
- High Roads lodging food becomes office / clerk food;
- family correspondence conventions influence Port recordkeeping;
- marriage networks widen into other merchant / professional groups.

## Internal divisions

- rotating merchant vs permanent Port household;
- rich family branch vs low-paid clerk;
- route-centered traditionalist vs Port civic careerist;
- local guild affiliation vs family-network loyalty.

## Information role

This group can know continental movement unusually early.

Important safeguard:

> **information advantage is not conspiracy.**

Their knowledge is:

- partial;
- commercially filtered;
- sometimes wrong;
- unevenly shared.

This makes them valuable to:

- Villain;
- Council;
- governments;
- journalists / rumor networks

without turning them into secret rulers.

## Myth transformation

### Night Road

Can become:

- clerk metaphor for missing caravan;
- family story;
- joking explanation for delayed paperwork.

### Weather Animal

Less central, but preserved in route-family speech.

A Port-born clerk might use an old weather-animal phrase without literal belief.

## Current-story role

During Three Moons:

- visiting guild / merchant concentration;
- increased correspondence;
- route reports

make this network unusually information-dense.

It helps explain why Port can know more without omniscience.

## P1 result

**VERY STRONG PASS.**

# 3. D-3 — Low Rivers-descended labor / food-market community

## Formation

Migration streams may include:

- seasonal market workers who stay;
- produce handlers;
- mill / bakery workers;
- warehouse labor;
- displaced tenant households;
- young adults leaving debt-heavy farms;
- family members joining existing migrants.

Important:

> Low Rivers migration can rise during **abundance**, not only famine.

The grower may be insolvent while food is plentiful.

## First-generation footholds

Likely sectors:

- produce market;
- grain / flour handling;
- baking;
- dairy / vegetable trade;
- cheap prepared food;
- boarding houses;
- warehouses.

These are footholds, not permanent ethnic occupations.

## Second generation

Port-born descendants may enter:

- dock work;
- guild trades;
- retail;
- clerical work;
- municipal services;
- performance / leisure economy.

Their identity can remain tied to:

- food;
- extended kin;
- rural visits;
- Blight stories

even after leaving food work.

## Port-born cuisine

This community should be one of the strongest engines of **new Port food**.

Mechanism:

~~~text
Low Rivers ingredients / household methods
+
Port imports / condiments
+
small urban kitchens
+
shift work
+
street selling
→ Port-born dish
~~~

Later:

~~~text
Port-born dish
→ carried upriver
→ treated in Low Rivers as "Port food"
~~~

This return loop is essential.

## Institutions

Possible:

- market mutual aid;
- informal hiring / kin placement;
- food-vendor association;
- faith-linked meals;
- boarding contacts;
- burial / emergency fund.

If wholesale merchants gain property, a more formal market association may emerge later.

## Internal divisions

- fresh migrant vs Port-born;
- produce wholesaler vs casual market labor;
- old tenant background vs cooperative background;
- household preserving rural kin ties vs fully urban branch;
- vendor benefiting from abundance vs grower relatives being ruined by it.

## Myth transformation

### Marsh Lure

In Port it may shift toward:

- drain / quay light;
- warehouse-flood story;
- children's warning around tidal mud / canals.

A grandparent may reject the urban version as nonsense.

That is useful.

## Current-story role

The Low Rivers abundance crisis becomes physically visible here:

- more cheap produce;
- more migrants;
- ruined relatives upriver;
- lower food cost but higher housing pressure.

This is exactly the kind of contradiction Port should contain.

## P1 result

**VERY STRONG PASS.**

# 4. D-4 — Stone Hills skilled-worker diaspora

## Formation

Migration reasons:

- better wages;
- contract work;
- ship / dock repair;
- guild transfer;
- mine downturn;
- strategic demand elsewhere;
- apprenticeship.

Unlike D-3, many migrants arrive with **portable recognized skill**.

## First-generation work

Possible sectors:

- ship / crane / hoist repair;
- metal fittings;
- toolmaking;
- warehouse mechanisms;
- inspection;
- structural metal / masonry support;
- precision repair.

Exact technology follows setting limits.

## Neighborhood pattern

This may not form one large enclave.

Skilled workers can settle near:

- repair yards;
- guild halls;
- industrial streets.

Kin / apprentice chains still produce clusters.

## Second generation

Children may enter:

- Port guild administration;
- inspection;
- shipbuilding;
- clerical / commercial metal trade;
- unrelated professions.

The most durable inheritance may be:

- skill vocabulary;
- quality expectations;
- guild contacts;
- mutual aid.

Not the original occupation.

## Institutional transfer

This group may introduce / strengthen:

- inspection habits;
- tool standards;
- apprentice practices;
- injury / burial funds;
- technical reading / training culture.

Port then changes those practices.

Returning workers can bring the Port version back to Stone Hills.

## Internal divisions

- guild-recognized migrant vs informal skilled worker;
- old Stone Hills craft family vs industrial worker;
- permanent settler vs temporary contract worker;
- local Port trainee vs migrant-trained specialist.

## Myth transformation

### Knocking Below

The strongest urban transformation is metaphorical / technical.

A ship / building repair worker may say:

> it is knocking

about:

- strained support;
- bad beam;
- dangerous vibration.

The phrase can survive after the underground creature story fades.

### Ore-Spoiler

Can become slang for:

- deceptive material;
- poor-quality metal;
- dishonest supplier.

This is cultural mutation through occupation.

## Current-story role

Guild centralization creates a mixed response:

Benefits:

- credential recognition;
- easier cross-city work;
- standard contracts.

Costs:

- stronger gatekeeping;
- local informal workers excluded;
- large guild offices gain power.

This community makes that tension visible.

## P1 result

**STRONG PASS.**

# 5. Second-generation test

## D-1

Still a North Coast enclave after two generations?

**NO.**

Port labor / mixed households / new occupations produce distinct Port identity.

## D-2

Still just a High Roads branch office?

**NO, if permanent households enter Port courts / guilds / civic life.**

Rotating merchants remain transregional; settled descendants become Port.

## D-3

Still just Low Rivers food sellers?

**NO.**

The community produces new Port food and occupations diversify.

## D-4

Still just Stone Hills craftspeople?

**NO.**

Technical culture survives while work roles expand.

All four pass the basic diaspora-development test.

# 6. Cross-diaspora Port interactions

These communities should overlap.

## D-1 × D-3

Dock / food / boarding-house overlap.

Potential result:

- North Coast preserved fish + Low Rivers vegetables + Port condiments becomes a local dish.

This is a concrete example of the user's cultural-bleed rule.

## D-2 × D-4

Clerks / guild / technical contracts overlap.

Potential result:

- High Roads contract language becomes embedded in Port craft guarantees.

## D-1 × D-2

Shipping / route information overlap.

Potential result:

- merchant information passes through sailors / harbor families before formal reports.

## D-3 × D-4

Food-market / industrial neighborhood overlap.

Potential result:

- worker canteens / cheap meals develop around repair districts.

These intersections matter more than preserving enclave purity.

# 7. Port identity implication

The strongest result is:

> **Port identity is partly made from people whose grandparents were "from somewhere else" but whose children no longer experience their lives as derivative.**

Therefore avoid dialogue such as:

> "My family is Low Rivers, so I am Low Rivers."

More realistic range:

- "My grandmother was."
- "We still cook that way."
- "I've never been there."
- "They say I sound Port."
- "When I visit, they call me Port."
- "The old people still call this a North Coast street. Half the block isn't."

This is the target texture.
