# Adaptive War Escalation Graph — V2

## Status

**Active author-side war architecture.**

Supersedes [War Escalation Causal Chain — V1](War-Escalation-Causal-Chain-V1.md) as the active planning model.

V1 remains valuable as the first no-leap proof. Its flaw was treating a plausible retrospective chain too much like a sequence the Villain could anticipate and manage prospectively.

V2 keeps the causal logic while replacing the fixed sequence with:

- pressure states;
- multiple routes;
- intervention windows;
- failure branches;
- adaptation.

See [Adaptive Domino Doctrine — V1](Adaptive-Domino-Doctrine-V1.md).

[Current Events Relative Chronology — V1](Current-Events-Relative-Chronology-V1.md) is the temporal application layer. It sequences prerequisites, Three Moons, containment, residue, and later security conversion without turning this graph back into a predetermined itinerary.

---

# 1. What remains established from V1

The world still requires intermediate stages between local problems and war.

A plausible broad progression is:

~~~text
independent pressures
→ commercial protection
→ scarcity / access anxiety
→ strategic controls
→ bloc dependence
→ security precautions
→ partial mobilization
→ security dilemma
→ armed incident / retaliation
→ guarantee activation
→ war
~~~

These are **state transitions**, not a predetermined itinerary.

There may be:

- reversals;
- skipped sub-branches;
- parallel routes;
- successful containment;
- substitutions.

No single local event is mandatory.

---

# 2. Strategic destination

The Villain wants the system to reach a condition where several significant actors simultaneously believe:

1. access to essential goods / routes is politically uncertain;
2. rivals are becoming more capable of denying that access;
3. allied guarantees matter to their security;
4. waiting may leave them strategically exposed;
5. normal arbitration may be slower than events.

When enough actors believe these things, defensive preparation becomes self-reinforcing.

That is the desired **war-permissive state**.

He does not need to know which incident starts the fighting.

---

# 3. Information accelerator — Port guild concentration + Three Moons

The adaptive graph assumes the Villain can observe several systems well enough to adapt.

That information advantage is now grounded in:

- Port's normal role as the largest commercial / maritime aggregation hub;
- increasing guild branch / representative presence in Port;
- cross-guild communication and contracting;
- the seven-year [Three Moons Festival](../Culture/Three-Moons-Festival.md).

Three Moons is **not a pressure node**.

It is an **observation accelerator**.

It temporarily improves his ability to detect:

- which branches are moving;
- which branches were contained;
- where unexpected bottlenecks formed;
- which actors are beginning to seek guarantees;
- where several sectors are competing for the same resource.

It also increases noise and false signals.

Therefore the festival can make him:

- faster;
- better informed;
- still wrong.

This is especially useful for the adaptive doctrine because his plan depends on updating beliefs rather than predicting a fixed sequence.

# 4. Pressure graph

## Node A — maritime insecurity

Inputs:

- fish shift;
- real piracy;
- overlapping sea-use rights.

Can feed:

- convoy concentration;
- Port risk pricing;
- armed maritime presence;
- access disputes.

Possible containment:

- successful NC-M / NC-I agreement;
- piracy decline;
- cheap shared escort solution.

Possible Villain response:

- usually wait;
- exploit only if a policy dispute is already close.

---

## Node B — Low Rivers financial stress

Inputs:

- abundance shock;
- storage pressure;
- weaker distant demand;
- debt.

Can feed:

- consolidation fears;
- purchasing restrictions;
- direct trade alternatives;
- food-security contracting elsewhere.

Possible containment:

- emergency cooperative purchasing;
- debt moratorium;
- new buyers;
- successful storage expansion.

Possible Villain response:

- support alternative buyers;
- use genuine abusive acquisitions as political evidence;
- abandon the restriction branch if reform solves it.

---

Inputs:

Possible containment:

- rapid route repair;
- trusted joint verification;
- arbitration;
- distributed rerouting with little political cost.

Possible Villain response:

- exploit whichever corridor actually becomes strained;

# 5. Convergence nodes

The Villain cares most about **convergence**, where independent branches begin producing the same pressure.

## C1 — reserve behavior

Can be fed by:

- food fear;
- metal uncertainty;
- convoy losses;
- route delays;
- water anxiety.

Effect:

> early purchasing makes shortage more real.

No Villain intervention may be necessary.

---

## C2 — strategic controls

Can be fed by:

- metal scarcity;
- route insecurity;
- food protection;
- harbor security.

Effect:

> governments / authorities gain legal tools to discriminate among users.

Historical memory makes these tools politically frightening.

---

## C3 — sphere sorting

Can be fed by:

- uncertain contracts;
- discriminatory controls;
- competing guarantees;
- arbitration distrust.

Effect:

> actors increasingly prefer partners who can guarantee access.

This converts economic networks into political ones.

---

## C4 — security conversion

Can be fed by:

- convoying;
- armed route escorts;
- guarded depots;
- repaired posts;
- reserve registration.

Effect:

> commercial precautions also become mobilization infrastructure.

This is the most important mid-game threshold.

---

## C5 — readiness fear

Can be fed by:

- visible reserves;
- escorts;
- strategic controls;
- guarantees;
- reserve personnel;
- troop / ship movements.

Effect:

> defensive preparations create evidence that rivals can act quickly.

This is where war can become self-sustaining.

# 6. Route redundancy matrix

No single path is required.

## Route R-A — maritime / credit

~~~text
piracy / maritime insecurity
→ convoying / risk repricing
→ Port credit tightening
→ commercial insecurity
→ reserve behavior / preferred counterparties
→ sphere sorting
~~~

Can fail if:

- piracy declines;
- shared convoy rules work;
- Port credit absorbs the losses cheaply.

If contained, other routes can still feed sphere sorting.

---

## Route R-B — food / water

~~~text
Low Rivers abundance stress
+ Old Cities drought anxiety
→ long-horizon purchasing
→ reserve behavior
→ access anxiety
→ food guarantees
→ sphere dependence
~~~

Can fail if:

- Low Rivers finds stable buyers;
- Old Cities water outlook improves;
- stock / contract data remains transparent.

If contained, metal / route pressures can still create strategic guarantees.

---

## Route R-C — roads / Longwood

~~~text
route damage / banditry
→ rerouting
→ bottlenecks / redundancy projects
→ rights disputes / inspections
→ armed escorts / chokepoint control
→ security conversion
~~~

Can fail if:

- route repair succeeds;
- Longwood rights negotiation works;
- trusted joint verification prevents escalation.

If contained, maritime escorts / strategic-material controls can still feed security conversion.

---

## Route R-D — metal / labor

~~~text
SH-M labor uncertainty
+ advance strategic purchasing
→ allocation anxiety
→ export licensing / priority rules
→ foreign reserve buying
→ access fear
→ sphere guarantees
~~~

Can fail if:

- labor settlement succeeds;
- allocation becomes transparent;
- reserves are released.

If contained, food / route / maritime pressures can still create access fear.

---

## Route R-E — Port itself

~~~text
congestion / repair / aid delay
→ Port reform pressure
→ rival fears of privileged influence
→ competing guarantees / access demands
→ neutrality stress
~~~

This route can intensify the political objective without independently producing war.

It becomes especially powerful once other routes have already created security competition.

---

## Redundancy rule

For any critical convergence node:

- reserve behavior;
- sphere sorting;
- security conversion;
- readiness fear;

there should be **at least two plausible feeder routes**.

If a final outline requires one named local event to occur exactly as planned, the graph is too brittle.

# 7. Constituency branching

A pressure node does not produce one response.

See [Current-Event Stakeholder Reaction Matrix — V1](Current-Event-Stakeholder-Reaction-Matrix-V1.md).

Every important node can branch because different groups experience the same event differently.

Example:

~~~text
piracy rises
   |
   +→ merchants demand convoys
   |
   +→ small captains oppose convoy costs
   |
   +→ island / harbor actors resist central command
   |
   +→ smugglers gain business
   |
   +→ security providers gain contracts
   |
   +→ centralizers cite the crisis as proof stronger coordination is needed
~~~

This factional structure is a major reason the Villain cannot predict exact outcomes.

He may know:

> piracy increases pressure for maritime policy.

He cannot know in advance:

> which faction wins the policy fight.

The adaptive process therefore watches:

- coalition formation;
- defections;
- splinters;
- who unexpectedly benefits;
- who changes position.

This also creates alternate paths.

A failed convoy-centralization branch can still produce:

- smuggling growth;
- Port credit changes;
- stronger harbor-autonomy politics.

# 8. Intervention windows

The Villain should not intervene continuously.

## Window W1 — policy tipping point

A real institution is divided.

Small input may matter:

- one report;
- one disclosure;
- one constituency receiving help.

Good intervention.

## Window W2 — expectation cascade

Actors are watching one another.

Small visible actions can alter beliefs:

- a reserve purchase;
- a guarantee;
- a route choice.

Good intervention.

## Window W3 — bottleneck formation

A route / warehouse / material / credit system is near capacity.

A small allocation change has disproportionate consequences.

Good intervention.

## Window W4 — attribution contest

Several explanations are plausible.

Selective truth can change political interpretation.

Good intervention.

## Window W5 — already self-sustaining

Actors are escalating on their own.

**Do not intervene unless necessary.**

Unnecessary action adds risk and evidence.

# 9. Adaptation examples

The relative chronology uses these as more than hypothetical tests: its working default includes a contained maritime political branch, a successful Longwood compromise, and imperfect Low Rivers stabilization so the Villain must visibly revise his board after Three Moons.


## Example A — maritime branch succeeds unexpectedly well

Expected:

convoy rules create constitutional conflict.

Actual:

NC-M and NC-I negotiate a workable arrangement quickly.

Villain response:

- do not sabotage the agreement merely to preserve the plan;
- maritime tension drops;
- shift attention to Port credit effects that already occurred, if any;
- rely more heavily on route / food / strategic-production pressures.

Lesson:

a branch can fail politically while still leaving useful economic residue.

---

## Example B — High Roads reroute goes the "wrong" way

Expected:

traffic shifts to corridor A.

Actual:

merchants choose corridor C.

Villain response:

- map corridor C's capacity and political ownership;
- ask whether C creates:
  - a different congestion point;
  - a Port approach problem;
  - a different rights dispute;
- abandon preparations tied specifically to A.

Do **not** claim corridor C was always the plan.

---

## Example C — Longwood compromise succeeds

Expected:

concession dispute delays route capacity.

Actual:

rights-holders negotiate a narrow agreement.

Villain response:

- let it stand;
- successful cooperation is a real setback;
- perhaps study what made it work;
- do not manufacture violence merely to save the branch.

This can later inform Wurdren's thematic counterexample.

---

## Example D — Council contains Low Rivers debt crisis

Expected:

protective rules create food-access fear.

Actual:

Council-linked credit / local policy stabilizes producers without severe restrictions.

Villain response:

- this route toward food insecurity weakens;
- preserve evidence of Council influence for later;
- seek another path into reserve behavior:
  - Old Cities drought anxiety;
  - maritime disruption;
  - route uncertainty.

The Council should sometimes win.

# 10. Failure matrix

Every active branch should be tagged:

- **GREEN** — moving toward desired pressure without help;
- **YELLOW** — useful but unstable / ambiguous;
- **RED** — contained, reversed, or no longer worth exposure risk;
- **BLACK** — dangerous to the Villain; could expose him or strengthen the old order.

A RED branch is not secretly GREEN.

A BLACK branch may require:

- retreat;
- cleanup;
- sacrifice of access;
- accepting that the opponent won.

---

# 11. War threshold

War becomes plausible when enough conditions overlap.

Working threshold — not mathematical canon:

- at least two major supply / route insecurities;
- meaningful sphere guarantees;
- armed escorts or equivalent security measures normalized;
- strategic reserves / controls visible;
- at least two actors with partial mobilization capability;
- diplomacy / arbitration perceived as slower than operational events;
- a local dispute where backing down threatens alliance credibility.

The exact combination can vary.

This allows multiple plausible final sparks.

---

# 12. Spark families

The Villain need not choose the exact final spark in advance.

Possible families include:

## Cargo / inspection

Strategic cargo is detained under a rule one side disputes.

## Convoy / harbor access

An armed escort claims access or exemption Port / another authority refuses.

## Maritime detention

Fishing / patrol / anti-piracy enforcement kills or detains someone tied to a guaranteed partner.

## Chokepoint crossing

Armed escorts and local guards dispute passage at a route / bridge / pass.

## Retaliatory seizure

One actor responds to an earlier commercial/security action with a "limited" seizure that the other treats as aggression.

By the time the graph reaches the war threshold, several of these can plausibly ignite conflict.

# 13. The Villain's actual control

He controls:

- where he places limited capital;
- what information he purchases;
- when selected information is released;
- which relationships he develops;
- which alternatives he helps build;
- whether he adds a push at a tipping point.

He does **not** control:

- weather;
- fish;
- markets as a whole;
- rulers;
- workers;
- the Council;
- every criminal group;
- which branch survives;
- the exact war spark.

His control is therefore:

> **selective leverage + adaptation.**

---

# 14. Author test

For every major story turn ask:

1. Which pressure node moved?
2. Was it natural, responsive, opportunistic, or Villain-touched?
3. If Villain-touched, why did he choose this moment?
4. What did he expect?
5. What actually happened?
6. Did he reinforce, abandon, or redirect?
7. What evidence did the touch leave?
8. What alternate route still exists if this branch dies?

If the answer to #5 is always "exactly what he expected," rewrite.
