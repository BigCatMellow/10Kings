# Linguistics Legacy Reconciliation

## Status

**Active reference / preservation layer — contact mechanisms retained, old region-coded language systems demoted.**

Source:

- [Volume 07 — Linguistics](../../legacy-notes/2026-09-22/consolidated-package/07_Linguistics.md)

Current owners:

- [Language and Thought](../Culture/Language-and-Thought.md)
- [Language Families, Contact, and Mutual Intelligibility](../Language-Families-and-Contact.md)
- [Naming](../Culture/Naming.md)
- [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md)

The legacy linguistics volume is extremely large and contains several incompatible generations of the language design.

Its strongest material concerns **contact, variation, code-switching and social use**.

Its weakest material assigns a single personality, accent, grammar and philosophy to each old region.

---

# 1. Main salvage result

Keep:

- local dialect pockets;
- isolation preserving older forms;
- migration creating speech enclaves;
- border continua;
- code-switching;
- stable mixed speech;
- generational language change;
- guild / professional registers;
- trade language;
- prestige language;
- class variation;
- language as evidence of migration and contact;
- bilingual children acting as brokers;
- differences between home language and formal / professional language.

Reject as default:

- one language per cultural region;
- one accent per cultural region;
- "industrial people speak directly";
- "forest people speak softly";
- "scholarly people have logical grammar";
- phonetic eye-dialect based on cultural stereotype;
- grammar determining moral or philosophical personality.

---

# 2. Micro-dialects are worth retaining

One of the better legacy observations is that broad language labels hide:

- valley speech;
- island speech;
- harbor speech;
- river speech;
- city / countryside differences;
- isolated religious communities;
- occupational speech;
- migrant neighborhoods.

A realistic language map therefore contains:

```text
language family
→ language / major variety
→ dialect continuum
→ local dialect
→ neighborhood / occupational register
→ individual speech
```

Not every level needs a proper name.

---

# 3. Isolation can preserve old forms

The legacy source correctly identifies:

- mountain valleys;
- islands;
- remote settlements;
- insular religious communities

as places where speech can retain older vocabulary, pronunciation or grammar.

Important correction:

Isolation does not make a community "archaic" in every way.

A community can preserve one linguistic feature while innovating rapidly in another.

---

# 4. Migration creates language pockets

Useful retained pattern:

A group can move far from an older homeland and preserve:

- older pronunciation;
- ritual vocabulary;
- kinship terms;
- food words;
- place-name forms.

Later generations may:

- shift toward the local majority language;
- retain only household vocabulary;
- become bilingual;
- create mixed speech;
- revive an older language for identity reasons.

This directly supports noncontiguous cultural geography.

---

# 5. Code-switching is social behavior

The border-town material repeatedly uses code-switching.

Retain the mechanism.

People may shift speech because of:

- audience;
- workplace;
- law;
- guild;
- family;
- religion;
- class;
- politeness;
- secrecy;
- intimidation;
- solidarity.

A person can speak one way:

- at home;
- with a guild client;
- in court;
- with grandparents;
- with friends;
- in Port.

That does not require separate personalities.

---

# 6. Stable contact speech

The old "code-fusion" idea should be translated into a more grounded current rule:

> repeated multilingual contact can create a stable local speech variety that younger speakers learn natively.

This can happen in:

- border towns;
- Port;
- caravan settlements;
- mixed guild neighborhoods;
- migrant districts.

The new variety should not remain permanently describable as "half language A + half language B."

After generations, it is local speech.

---

# 7. Trade and professional registers

Volume 07 repeatedly notices that merchants, guild members and specialists can share vocabulary across linguistic boundaries.

This is strongly worth retaining.

Possible registers include:

- trade;
- contracts;
- weights / measures;
- navigation;
- construction;
- medicine;
- scholarship;
- religion;
- military signaling.

A professional register can spread without becoming anyone's home language.

The newer language-family owner now captures this structurally.

---

# 8. Class and prestige

The legacy material usefully points toward differences between:

- rural / urban speech;
- guild / non-guild speech;
- elite / ordinary speech;
- formal / intimate speech.

Retain the mechanism but not the old value judgments.

Prestige language can create:

- job access;
- legal advantage;
- stigma;
- schooling pressure;
- accent suppression;
- deliberate revival of local speech.

A person may be fully competent but judged as provincial because of accent.

That judgment belongs to characters / institutions, not the narrator.

---

# 9. Linguistic relativity — preserve the caution, not the caricature

The legacy notes correctly include a caution that language does **not** rigidly determine thought.

That caveat should survive.

Useful linguistic design options include systems that mark or emphasize:

- information source / evidentiality;
- inclusive vs. exclusive "we";
- politeness / formality;
- grammatical aspect;
- spatial orientation;
- classifiers;
- motion;
- kinship;
- number distinctions.

These can affect what speakers must routinely make explicit.

They should **not** be assigned to a culture merely to prove that culture is:

- logical;
- communal;
- mystical;
- polite;
- practical.

The linguistic feature needs a historical language reason first.

---

# 10. Evidentiality is especially useful

The legacy research on evidentiality is worth keeping as an option.

A language or register might require speakers to distinguish:

- witnessed;
- heard;
- inferred;
- reported.

This could matter in:

- law;
- rumor;
- scholarship;
- religion;
- intelligence.

It is **not established** that any current language does this.

But it is a strong candidate because it creates practical consequences rather than decorative accent flavor.

---

# 11. Politeness systems are socially useful

Likewise, formal distinctions can encode:

- familiarity;
- age;
- status;
- office;
- respect;
- distance.

The source's old "Sunplains = polite language" assignment is rejected.

The mechanism remains useful for any specific language whose social history supports it.

---

# 12. Direction systems can follow environment

The legacy research notes languages that use:

- cardinal directions;
- uphill / downhill;
- environmental orientation

rather than only body-relative left / right.

This is a useful option for:

- mountain communities;
- river systems;
- sailors;
- route cultures.

Again, it is a **possible linguistic feature**, not a High Roads stereotype.

---

# 13. Trade tongues / simplified contact registers

The old notes repeatedly propose bazaar / Underpass / Port trade speech.

Retain the general mechanism.

Possible path:

```text
limited-purpose contact register
→ repeated use
→ expanded vocabulary
→ children learn it
→ native local language / contact variety
```

Not every trade register becomes a full language.

Some remain narrow professional tools for centuries.

---

# 14. Translation is labor

The newer language owner develops this more fully, but Volume 07 supports the need.

Translation / interpretation can require:

- bilingual merchants;
- clerks;
- legal interpreters;
- religious translators;
- caravan brokers;
- guides.

That means multilingual ability can create:

- employment;
- prestige;
- political access;
- opportunities for manipulation;
- misunderstandings with material consequences.

---

# 15. Writing dialogue

The old corpus contains many phonetic accent spellings.

Do not retain that as the default writing method.

Problems:

- difficult to read;
- easily caricatured;
- often maps social stereotype directly to spelling;
- can make one group appear less intelligent.

Better cues:

- syntax;
- vocabulary;
- register;
- idiom;
- what a speaker chooses to explain;
- code-switching;
- occasional pronunciation note in narration.

Use phonetic spelling sparingly, if at all.

---

# 16. Idioms and proverbs

The legacy volume contains large banks of region-themed idioms.

Most are too mechanically derived from "region = occupation."

The useful rule is:

> idioms emerge from repeated shared experience and can outlive the original occupation or environment.

A route proverb can spread into a city.

A maritime saying can become ordinary Port speech.

A guild phrase can become slang.

Do not give every culture ten mandatory themed metaphors.

---

# 18. Old language names / labels

Legacy examples use provisional language or dialect labels such as **Ironet** and region-derived language names.

Do not promote them simply because they recur in the old notes.

Future language names should follow:

- endonym / exonym history;
- political standardization;
- migration;
- scholarly classification;
- ordinary speaker usage.

# 20. Remaining useful questions

Still open:

- actual language families;
- actual mutual-intelligibility gradients;
- scripts;
- literacy distribution;
- standard orthographies;
- how many people read contracts;
- who teaches writing;
- which professional registers use standardized vocabulary;
- which faiths preserve older languages;
- where language revival is politically important;
- which speech varieties carry prestige;
- whether any evidentiality / directional / honorific systems deserve promotion.

These need dedicated development rather than automatic recovery from Volume 07.

---

# 21. Language design test

For any proposed linguistic feature, ask:

1. Which language / community uses it?
2. Why did it develop?
3. Is it grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation or register?
4. Is it actually widespread?
5. What neighboring varieties lack it?
6. What happens in bilingual speech?
7. Does class affect it?
8. Does writing preserve an older form?
9. Does the feature create a practical consequence?
10. Are we using it to stereotype a whole culture?

If the last answer is yes, redesign it.
