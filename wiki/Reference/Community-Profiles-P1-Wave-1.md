**Provisional community profiles — P1, not owner-page canon.**

They are constrained by:

- current regional owners;
- political vertical slices;
- Culture Phases 2–7;
- [Community-to-Analogue Matrix — V1](Community-to-Analogue-Matrix-V1.md);

# 1. SH-1 — Stone Hills industrial mine / civic district

## Formation

This community grew around several overlapping systems:

- major extraction;
- processing / workshops;
- heavy transport;
- worker housing;
- guild institutions;
- civic authority.

It is not simply a company settlement.

Its present form likely records several historical layers:

~~~text
older mine / craft households
→ mine expansion
→ migrant labor
→ dense worker housing
→ worker mutual-aid / learning institutions
→ stronger guild / owner concentration
→ current strategic-production pressure
~~~

## Livelihood

Households may depend on:

- extraction;
- haulage;
- sorting / processing;
- toolmaking;
- maintenance;
- accounting;
- food / lodging services;
- transport.

One household may contain people tied to **different layers of the same industrial economy**.

## Household pattern

Use the current Stone Hills mixed-workshop logic rather than inventing a mining barracks culture.

Possible household:

- mine worker;
- workshop / repair worker;
- older relative with technical knowledge;
- apprentice or boarder;
- partner managing market / household commerce;
- children doing age-appropriate errands / schooling.

Poorer casual workers may live in:

- rented rooms;
- boarding arrangements;
- crowded worker streets.

## Civic institutions

Plausible worker-created institutions include:

- injury / burial funds;
- training / reading room;
- meeting hall;
- common meal / relief system;
- music / recreation group;
- strike / unemployment relief.

These need not belong to one political movement.

A hall may host:

- technical lecture one night;
- funeral collection the next;
- labor meeting later;
- feast / music on another day.

## Internal divisions

Important lines include:

- skilled guild worker vs casual laborer;
- old local family vs newer migrant;
- worker vs owner;
- worker vs technical supervisor;
- guild reformer vs guild traditionalist;
- households dependent on strategic contracts vs households harmed by unsafe priority production.

Do not make "workers" one faction.

## Built environment

From the existing architecture pass:

- irregular streets grown around old mine roads / workshops;
- workshop-households;
- masonry fire walls near industrial work;
- slag / ash / dirty runoff;
- cleaner owner / professional districts uphill or away from production;
- shared wells / water conflict.

## Food / ordinary life

Likely ordinary food remains:

- grain;
- roots;
- legumes;
- dairy;
- bread;
- pickles;
- small preserved meat amounts.

Industrial wages do not mean rich eating.

During labor conflict:

- credit;
- relief kitchens;
- guild meals;
- recruiter food / lodging offers

become political.

## Mythic tradition

**The Knocking Below** is embedded in:

- worker songs;
- memorial books;
- accident testimony;
- safety custom.

The district's educated workers need not abandon the story.

A person can simultaneously believe:

> rock stress explains most knocking

and

> only a fool ignores the old pattern.

**Ore-Spoiler** survives as:

- technical slang;
- warning;
- moral story around rushing bad material.

## Cultural exports

This district exports:

- trained workers;
- safety lore;
- industrial songs;
- technical vocabulary;
- guild practices;
- labor politics.

Migrating miners carry the Knocking Below into:

- other Stone Hills workings;
- Underpass crews;
- Port scholarship.

## Current-event vulnerability

Strategic-priority production creates conflict over:

- safety;
- wage;
- secrecy;
- ordinary civilian orders;
- labor discipline.

The Villain's timed release of truthful allocation information can land here because the social infrastructure for collective interpretation already exists.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

The community is economically, socially, politically, and mythically distinct enough to move from R2 → **P1**.

# 2. NC-1 — outer-island seasonal fishing community

## Formation

This is a permanently inhabited island / skerry community whose annual rhythm is transformed by one or more major seasonal fisheries.

The key distinction:

> **the community exists all year; the crowd does not.**

History may show:

- older resident fishing / farming households;
- gradually formalized seasonal lodgings;
- landing / drying / repair infrastructure;
- recurring visiting crews.

## Livelihood

Resident households may combine:

- fishing;
- preservation;
- boat / gear repair;
- small livestock / hardy crops;
- lodging;
- guiding / pilot knowledge.

Visiting seasonal workers may:

- rent bunks;
- join crews;
- bring different food / speech / stories;
- leave after the run.

## Household pattern

A household can contain:

- person at sea;
- shore preservation / market worker;
- children;
- older relative;
- storm-displaced kin;
- seasonal boarder.

This builds directly on the current North Coast household slice.

## Institutions

Likely local institutions include:

- harbor / landing rules;
- fishery-use rights;
- rescue obligations;
- seasonal lodging customs;
- gear / drying-space allocation;
- burial / support expectations after sea loss.

Some rights may be:

- household-held;
- harbor-recognized;
- seasonal.

## Internal divisions

Not simply:

> islanders vs outsiders.

Possible fractures:

- resident crew owners vs visiting wage / share crew;
- older fishery-right households vs newcomers;
- households benefiting from seasonal lodging vs those burdened by crowding;
- people supporting convoy coordination vs people fearing outside command;
- fishers vs preservation / merchant interests.

## Built environment

Likely:

- shore-following settlement;
- gear storage;
- drying / smoking areas;
- seasonal bunk / lodging buildings;
- storm-protected work yards;
- fresh-water systems separated from salt contamination.

## Food / ordinary life

Fish remains central but not exclusive.

Households also use:

- hardy grains;
- roots;
- dairy where supported;
- berries;
- imported grain.

When stocks fall:

- premium fish is sold;
- household diet shifts toward cheaper grain / root foods;
- preservation spaces become underused or repurposed.

## Mythic tradition

This is the strongest literal **Great Mouth** community.

Residents often speak as though:

> something real lies behind the story.

But they may strongly reject Port's monstrous reconstruction.

Seasonal crews can carry different versions into the island and back out.

This allows variation **inside one seasonal economy**.

## Cultural exports

- fish preservation techniques;
- route / weather songs;
- crew stories;
- Great Mouth variants;
- rescue practices;
- seasonal labor customs.

## Current-event vulnerability

Fish decline and piracy / convoy pressure alter:

- household income;
- crew recruitment;
- marriage prospects;
- imported food dependency;
- fishery-right politics.

The maritime compromise later leaves durable convoy residue here.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 3. NC-3 — major compact harbor community

## Formation

A major harbor grew from multiple older rights:

- private / household quays;
- merchant storehouses;
- fishing facilities;
- repair yards;
- local landing rights.

As traffic increased, harbor-wide problems forced broader institutions around:

- navigation;
- fire;
- storage;
- quarantine / health where applicable;
- security;
- pilotage;
- convoy administration.

The harbor therefore contains a political memory that:

> **functional coordination can become permanent without abolishing older property / guild rights.**

## Livelihood

Major sectors:

- vessel ownership;
- crews;
- fish / cargo merchants;
- warehouses;
- preservation;
- ship / gear repair;
- pilots;
- market labor;
- finance / records;
- seasonal workers.

## Neighborhood layering

Possible historical districts:

- old harbor-family quarter;
- warehouse / merchant quarter;
- seasonal-fisher lodging district;
- repair yards;
- later migrant neighborhood;
- civic harbor-office zone.

Do not make these hard ethnic enclaves.

## Institutions

- harbor office;
- merchant associations;
- crew / rescue associations;
- warehouse / weighing institutions;
- guild branches;
- local civic authority.

No one institution controls everything.

## Internal divisions

- local merchants vs outside houses;
- shipowners vs crews;
- year-round residents vs seasonal labor;
- large harbor vs smaller surrounding harbors;
- central convoy command vs local captain autonomy.

## Cultural character

Compared with NC-1:

- more written records;
- more merchant influence;
- more class difference;
- more imported goods;
- more outsiders;
- more civic administration.

But it is still culturally North Coast only in the broadest sense.

## Mythic tradition

Great Mouth here becomes:

- sailor history;
- ecological memory;
- commercial illustration;
- tavern story.

The harbor may be the first place old island stories get:

- copied;
- standardized;
- named for outsiders.

That transformation later intensifies in Port.

## Current-event vulnerability

Convoy / piracy policy becomes a constitutional issue because:

- local harbor institutions already possess real capacity;
- outside / central coordination needs them;
- they do not want temporary security authority to become unlimited.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 4. LR-1 — Low Rivers cooperative irrigation village

## Formation

This community exists because several households depend on the same:

- channel;
- diversion;
- gate;
- tributary;
- drainage / irrigation system.

Its social geography may follow **water dependence more strongly than administrative boundaries**.

## Livelihood

Likely mix:

- grain;
- legumes;
- vegetables;
- orchard / garden crops;
- livestock;
- mill / market exchange.

Not every member farms the same crop.

## Water institution

A durable local water-user body can govern:

- schedule / turn;
- maintenance;
- emergency allocation;
- local repair;
- evidence / records.

Membership should be tied to:

- actual use;
- recognized right;
- household / field connection.

## Obligations

Water rights may carry:

- maintenance labor;
- material contribution;
- emergency repair duty.

This gives the institution a social calendar.

A canal-cleaning / repair period may also become:

- meal;
- gossip;
- courtship;
- dispute;
- political event.

Do not make it a happy communal festival by default.

## Internal divisions

- large user vs small user;
- upstream field vs downstream field;
- household with labor to spare vs labor-poor household;
- old recognized user vs new tenant;
- cooperative member vs estate-controlled water.

Questions of **who counts as a legitimate user** should exist here too.

## Household / food

Existing Low Rivers logic remains:

- stored food;
- mixed farm / market work;
- seasonal household labor;
- credit / access rights as wealth.

Abundance can coexist with debt.

## Mythic tradition

Marsh Lure is less central here than in LR-4.

Local version:

> lights follow forgotten / abandoned channels.

Meaning:

- old watercourses matter;
- maintenance memory matters.

Some people use the creature as a story about infrastructure neglect.

## Cultural exports

- water-management procedures;
- communal-maintenance practices;
- food / milling customs;
- river-market connections.

## Current-event vulnerability

During abundance / debt pressure:

- water institution may remain functional while household finances collapse;
- storage / buyer / credit politics become more important than production.

This is useful because:

> a competent community can still suffer from a system-level market crisis.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 5. LR-4 — Low Rivers floodplain community

## Formation

This community occupies land where seasonal water movement is expected.

Its culture therefore distinguishes between:

- permanent ground;
- seasonally wet ground;
- temporary channels;
- usable water;
- dangerous water.

Flooding is not only catastrophe.

It is part of ordinary land knowledge.

## Livelihood

Possible mix:

- flood-adapted cultivation;
- fish;
- livestock on higher ground;
- seasonal vegetables;
- reed / fiber / wetland products;
- boat / ferry work;
- seedlings / nursery production.

Exact floating-bed technology remains open until local flora / material review.

## Settlement form

Use multiple strategies:

- raised floors;
- higher storage;
- movable / sacrificial lower structures;
- seasonal paths;
- boats;
- flood marks;
- shared elevated facilities.

Do not make the whole village stilt houses.

## Household rhythm

Household work changes with water level.

One path may be:

- road in one month;
- boat route later;
- unusable during peak flood;
- productive wetland afterward.

Children learn the landscape as **seasonal categories** rather than fixed map.

## Institutions

Possible community roles around:

- flood warning;
- boat access;
- levee / embankment maintenance;
- high-ground refuge;
- communal storage.

These can overlap with LR-A authority without being replaced by it.

## Internal divisions

- higher-ground households vs low-ground households;
- fisher / wetland users vs farmers wanting drainage;
- people favoring stronger embankment vs people whose livelihood depends on seasonal inundation.

This prevents "flood protection" from being universally good.

## Mythic tradition

This is the strongest **Marsh Lure** community.

Important:

> the people telling the story are experts in water.

The story warns against:

- assuming yesterday's path still exists;
- trusting apparent distance in mist / darkness;
- following isolated lights.

A person can understand the natural explanations and still avoid a moving light.

## Historical ecology

If channels are:

- drained;
- embanked;
- redirected,

old Marsh Lure sightings may disappear.

New ones may arise elsewhere.

Folklore can become evidence of old hydrology.

## Current-event vulnerability

Abundance / storage crisis can hit differently here because:

- access routes change seasonally;
- storage / transport timing matters;
- debt may push households toward more intensive drainage / land conversion.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 6. LW-1 — western Longwood compact / market-edge community

## Formation

The community lies where:

- forest-use systems;
- town / market institutions;
- roads;
- Stone Hills / Low Rivers demand;
- timber / charcoal / other forest-product commerce

meet.

External extraction pressure encouraged multiple communities / towns to build a compact authority able to negotiate:

- roads;
- concessions;
- large contracts;
- enforcement.

The compact did not erase local rights.

That is its defining contradiction.

## Livelihood

Likely mix:

- timber / managed wood;
- charcoal where supported;
- river transport;
- agriculture in clearings;
- processing;
- market trade;
- wage labor;
- road / bridge services.

Locals can be commercially engaged without endorsing unlimited extraction.

## Political culture

People distinguish among:

- territorial sovereignty;
- compact authority;
- town authority;
- household / community use right;
- specialist / warden judgment.

A contract can be valid at one layer and still violate another.

This should feel normal to locals and maddening to outsiders.

## Internal divisions

- market town vs surrounding use-right communities;
- older residents vs recent logging / road workers;
- commercial forest cooperative vs large outside concession;
- people wanting more compact capacity vs people fearing it has become too permanent;
- community recognized as "user" vs newer settlement seeking recognition.

## Household / material life

Existing Longwood river-town logic applies:

- purchased grain / salt;
- seasonal forest foods;
- river / timber work;
- wet-weather clothing;
- covered work spaces;
- tool repair.

Expansion creates:

- bunkhouses;
- worker camps;
- bridges;
- sediment / drainage changes.

## Mythic tradition

Forest Owner becomes politically contested here.

Different claims:

> "It punishes cutting."

versus:

> "No. It punishes taking what wasn't yours."

versus:

> "It's a story people tell when they don't want to cite the actual use-right clause."

All three can coexist.

This is a very strong Wurdren-story community because the folklore itself mirrors the underlying legal distinction.

## Cultural exports

- concession / compact legal practice;
- processed forest goods;
- Forest Owner stories transformed by market contact;
- workers moving toward Stone Hills / Port.

## Current-event vulnerability

The current road / access dispute can originate from precisely this layered structure.

Wurdren's early Longwood counter-domino should involve:

- real commercial need;
- valid passage claim;
- invalid assumption that passage includes unlimited clearing.

No party has to oppose roads in principle.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 7. LW-2 — interior Longwood use-right community

## Formation

This community is part of the looser LW-I interior political order.

Its defining institution is not a centralized forest government.

It is a dense mesh of recognized rights / obligations tied to actual use.

## Rights structure

Different households / groups may possess rights to:

- residence;
- gathering;
- timber;
- hunting;
- fishing;
- grazing;
- water;
- passage.

These rights can overlap spatially.

No simple:

> this family owns the forest

model is required.

## Membership politics

The comparative research adds a crucial question:

> **who counts as a legitimate user?**

Possible disputes:

- resident household vs married-out kin;
- old user vs newcomer;
- seasonal user vs permanent resident;
- wealthy household vs labor-poor household;
- young generation wanting different use.

Representation should never be treated as naturally equitable.

## Rights and obligations

A use right may require:

- trail maintenance;
- firebreak labor;
- replanting / rotation;
- boundary marking;
- emergency response.

This gives "customary rights" material obligations.

## Political organization

For ordinary use:

- local community institutions dominate.

For cross-community problems:

- delegates receive limited mandates.

Do not make this a regional forest council.

## Household / material life

Compared with LW-1:

- less market dependence in some places;
- stronger direct connection to local rights;
- still uses imported:
  - grain;
  - salt;
  - tools.

Do not make the community self-sufficient.

## Mythic tradition

The Forest Owner here is least likely to have a stable humanoid body.

Possible understanding:

> the place turns people who act as though one permission means every permission.

Its cultural force lies in:

- layered rights;
- obligation;
- attention to who else uses the same place.

A child may hear a literal creature story.

An adult may treat the same story as:

- practical rule;
- spiritual truth;
- old metaphor.

## Current-event vulnerability

Outside concessions become dangerous when:

- a compact / polity recognizes one category of authority;
- contractors assume that recognition covers every use.

This community supplies the local-right side of Wurdren's Longwood dispute.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 8. OC-1 — upstream city civic / water culture

## Formation

This culture grows inside OC-U, where political leverage and civic responsibility both follow:

- upstream position;
- headworks;
- diversions;
- estates;
- inherited water systems.

The cultural self-image should not simply be:

> we control the water.

A stronger internal narrative is:

> **we keep the system working and therefore have both rights and burdens others underestimate.**

## Water institutions

People may interact with:

- technical officials;
- share / allocation records;
- neighborhood / agricultural user groups;
- maintenance crews;
- shared-water compact representatives.

Authority is narrow but respected.

## Civic culture

Possible values:

- precision in records;
- visible maintenance;
- continuity of precedent;
- technical prestige;
- skepticism toward downstream accusations framed without engineering knowledge.

These values can become arrogant.

## Internal divisions

- large estates vs neighborhoods;
- technical officials vs political rulers;
- upstream users near headworks vs peripheral users;
- people defending old allocation vs reformers;
- elites with private storage / gardens vs poorer public-water users.

## Ordinary life

Existing Old Cities patterns apply:

- courtyard / dense urban households;
- public / household water storage;
- early markets / heat-adapted work;
- bread / pulses / vegetables / oils / dairy;
- visible class differences in water access.

## Mythic tradition

Charter Beast here is most formalized.

Often seen as:

- old legal emblem;
- guardian of proper division;
- mark that allocation is legitimate.

Lawyers / clerks may deny literal belief.

Households still use the image.

## Current-event vulnerability

During drought / fear:

- maintaining flow can be presented as technical necessity;
- downstream actors may interpret the same act as coercion.

This community gives the water crisis a defensible upstream voice.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 9. OC-2 — downstream canal-city neighborhood culture

## Formation

This community lives inside OC-D's greater dependence on continued upstream flow.

Its political habits therefore emphasize:

- measurement;
- transparency;
- shared warning;
- maintenance;
- public evidence.

## Neighborhood water life

A dense district may organize around:

- local channels;
- public basins / fountains;
- cisterns;
- maintenance crews;
- irrigation / market schedules.

Water workers can be ordinary neighborhood figures rather than remote state officials.

## Civic institutions

A narrow water tribunal / forum can possess high legitimacy for:

- flow dispute;
- maintenance;
- gate scheduling;
- emergency allocation.

It does not become a city parliament.

## Internal divisions

- households vs large downstream estates;
- merchants wanting predictable supply vs farmers wanting crop priority;
- reformers wanting stronger shared compact authority vs sovereignty defenders;
- neighborhoods with reliable channels vs tail-end areas.

## Ordinary life

Water politics affect:

- cooking;
- washing;
- gardens;
- market vegetables;
- craft production;
- public fountains;
- hospitality.

Drought anxiety therefore changes etiquette before actual shortage.

## Mythic tradition

Charter Beast here is more animate.

It is said to:

- walk dry canal edges;
- notice stolen water;
- disturb false markers;
- remember where flow should have gone.

A skeptical canal worker may still use the phrase:

> the beast will find that gate

to mean:

> the records will expose it.

## Current-event vulnerability

The current Old Cities cooperation braid can become politically charged because this community has rational reasons to want:

- stronger shared measurements;
- stronger warning rules.

OC-U may interpret that as:

- expansion of downstream control over upstream sovereignty.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 10. P-1 — old Port harbor-worker neighborhood

## Formation

The neighborhood developed through repeated proximity among:

- docks;
- warehouse labor;
- small-craft movement;
- lodging;
- repair;
- casual hiring.

It has been rebuilt / subdivided repeatedly.

"Old" means:

> Port-rooted across generations,

not architecturally untouched.

## Occupational ecology

Do not use "dockworker" as one job.

Possible work identities:

- quay loading;
- warehouse handling;
- tally / weight work;
- lighter / small-craft transport;
- rigging / hoist work;
- repair;
- casual carrying;
- provisioning.

These groups have different:

- skills;
- injuries;
- schedules;
- guild relationships;
- bargaining power.

## Household form

Existing Port dock-household logic applies strongly:

- mixed-origin Port-born family;
- boarder;
- sailor / short-term relative;
- shift work;
- improvised childcare;
- limited privacy.

## Labor institutions

Possible neighborhood institutions:

- injury / burial fund;
- meal / relief society;
- hiring contacts;
- crew-based mutual aid;
- informal child supervision;
- faith-linked assistance;
- guild branch.

No single institution needs to organize everyone.

## Internal divisions

- stable crew vs casual labor;
- guild credential vs informal experience;
- old Port family vs recent migrant;
- warehouse workers vs ship-side labor;
- people benefiting from guild centralization vs people excluded by formal credentials.

This is crucial for final Port politics.

## Material / food culture

- crowded housing;
- small kitchens;
- prepared / street food;
- imported ingredients transformed into Port food;
- secondhand clothing;
- work gear;
- repeated fire / rebuilding.

## Mythic tradition

This is the primary living memory carrier for the **Estuary Captive** ethical version.

Old families care less about:

> what species was it?

than:

> who had authority to hold it and what did they do?

The story can become a local analogy in arguments about:

- detention;
- migrant treatment;
- unknown outsiders.

But not everyone accepts the analogy.

## Three Moons

Festival brings:

- overtime;
- temporary workers;
- lodging pressure;
- street commerce;
- public pride;
- resentment.

The Old Port Hand from Wurdren's opening can plausibly come from this social world.

## Current-event vulnerability

Port reform matters materially here:

- retained revenue could improve infrastructure;
- stronger harbor authority could improve coordination;
- guild centralization could reduce chaos.

The same reforms may:

- strengthen large houses;
- exclude casual labor;
- raise rents.

Therefore this neighborhood can support **Port agency without supporting the Villain personally**.

## P1 result

**PASS.**

R2 → **P1**.

# 13. Main gaps revealed by P1 conversion

The biggest missing cultural pieces are now clearer.

## A. Naming / language specificity

Profiles still use analytical labels.

Next naming work must wait until:

- local language history;
- migration;
- neighboring pressure

are attached.

## B. Religion inside ordinary community life

Current profiles mention religion only where needed.

A later pass should ask:

- which faith institutions actually live here?
- which practices are local rather than denomination-wide?
- how do mixed-faith households work?

Do not assign one faith per community.

## C. Generational change

Need explicit contrasts:

- grandparent;
- working adult;
- adolescent / young worker.

Especially important in:

- Port;
- industrial Stone Hills;
- mobile circuits;
- Longwood edge.

## D. Gender / household power

Current broad household profiles avoid overlocking roles.

The next P2 stress test should ask:

- inheritance;
- labor visibility;
- formal office;
- informal influence;
- widowhood / remarriage;
- property.

No generic regional answer.

## E. Youth culture

Strongly underdeveloped across all profiles.

Need:

- games;
- status;
- work entry;
- slang;
- music;
- courtship;
- risk-taking.

This should become a later cross-community pass.
