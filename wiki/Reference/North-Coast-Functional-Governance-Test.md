# North Coast Functional Governance Test

## Status

**Provisional structural test.**

This page tests which political functions North Coast institutions need to perform before choosing a final constitutional form.

It does **not** establish:

- one North Coast state;
- a crown or confederation;
- a capital;
- exact borders;
- named clan leagues;
- named fishery councils;
- a uniform maritime code;
- exact island representation;
- a standing regional navy.

The working question is:

> **Which North Coast functions belong to shore territory, which follow people and vessels, which follow seasonal sea use, and which require wider political coordination?**

---

# 1. Existing constraints

North Coast already contains or historically contains:

- coastal clans;
- fishing confederacies;
- harbor towns;
- island rulers;
- inland chiefs;
- raiding bands;
- shipping ports;
- fisheries whose location and productivity vary seasonally;
- winter anchorages;
- rescue obligations;
- convoy politics;
- road connections into High Roads;
- substantial dependence on imported food and goods.

The region owner already establishes an important historical fact:

> a political border on shore did not necessarily define who fished a bank or used a winter anchorage.

Any viable political model has to preserve that mismatch between land authority and maritime use.

---

# 2. The central distinction — four kinds of authority overlap

North Coast politics becomes clearer if authority is separated into four recurring categories.

## Shore authority

Controls some combination of:

- land;
- towns;
- docks;
- warehouses;
- roads;
- taxation;
- local policing;
- fixed infrastructure.

## Harbor authority

Controls or coordinates:

- berthing;
- pilotage;
- harbor safety;
- fish markets;
- quarantine where relevant;
- repairs;
- local fees;
- emergency closure.

Harbor authority may belong to a town, ruler, chartered body, clan, or mixed institution.

## People / vessel authority

Some obligations travel with:

- crews;
- captains;
- clans;
- shipowners;
- guild or professional membership;
- contracts.

These do not disappear merely because a ship enters someone else's harbor.

## Seasonal-use authority

Fishing banks, anchorages, migration routes, seal or other seasonal grounds, and rescue zones may be governed by custom or agreement that does not resemble an ordinary land border.

This is the distinctive North Coast problem.

---

# 3. Minimum functions by scale

## Household / crew

Needs practical authority over:

- provisioning;
- labor division;
- catch handling;
- ordinary onboard decisions;
- family or crew shares;
- routine repair.

A crew cannot settle access to a contested fishery by itself.

## Clan / kin network

Can plausibly provide:

- boats;
- credit;
- labor;
- rescue obligations;
- compensation;
- reputation;
- marriage alliances;
- political backing;
- inherited access claims.

Clan authority may cross several towns or islands.

It should not automatically equal territorial sovereignty.

## Harbor / town

Needs capacity for:

- docking;
- markets;
- storage;
- repair;
- local policing;
- fees;
- fire;
- quarantine or disease response where relevant;
- storm closure;
- cargo and ship disputes.

A harbor can be locally powerful even when the ships using it answer to outside families or rulers.

## Fishery / seasonal-access system

Needs mechanisms for:

- who may use a bank or seasonal ground;
- timing;
- gear conflicts where relevant;
- conservation responses;
- rescue expectations;
- boundary or access disputes;
- evidence about historical use.

These rules may be customary, negotiated, political, or mixed.

## Island / local polity

Needs some combination of:

- ordinary law;
- land taxation;
- defense;
- harbor support;
- food security;
- local infrastructure;
- relations with neighboring islands or mainland powers.

## Larger polity / coalition

Wider authority is needed somewhere for:

- major war;
- diplomacy;
- large convoy organization;
- piracy suppression beyond one harbor;
- major customs or trade policy;
- inter-harbor enforcement;
- strategic road / harbor investment;
- relations with Port and the Convergence.

The tests do not require all of these functions to sit in one permanent institution.

---

# 4. Test 1 — shifting fish stocks

## Condition

Fish stocks decline in one traditional area and become more abundant elsewhere.

Crews move farther than usual.

They enter waters used by:

- another clan;
- another harbor;
- another island;
- another polity.

## Failure of shore-border logic

If the rule is simply:

> waters belong to the nearest shore,

then long-standing seasonal rights become meaningless.

If the rule is simply:

> historical users always keep access,

then changing ecology can prevent local communities from adapting.

The system needs a way to weigh:

- old use;
- current dependence;
- changing stock location;
- conservation;
- newcomers;
- emergency access.

## Result

**PASS for fishery-specific jurisdiction.**

Fishing access must be governed by institutions or customs that can cross ordinary territorial boundaries.

That does not require one regional fisheries government.

It does require recognized forums or agreements that can handle claims not reducible to shore ownership.

---

# 5. Test 2 — piracy and convoy protection

## Condition

Raids increase.

Merchants, fishing fleets, and passenger traffic demand protection.

## Local response

Harbors can:

- improve watch;
- share sightings;
- regulate departure;
- arm local patrols;
- inspect suspicious vessels.

That does not solve piracy across a long coast.

## Wider response

Effective suppression may require:

- shared intelligence;
- convoy schedules;
- pursuit rights;
- pooled ships or crews;
- common signals;
- rules for capture;
- funding.

## Political danger

The same measures can look like:

- defensive coordination;
- naval expansion;
- privateering;
- one faction trying to dominate sea lanes.

A convoy commander may have temporary authority over ships from several jurisdictions without becoming their sovereign.

## Result

**PASS for mission-specific maritime coordination.**

North Coast needs mechanisms capable of assembling authority for a specific maritime problem without implying permanent political unification.

---

# 6. Test 3 — storm, wreck, and rescue

## Condition

A severe storm wrecks ships near a coast used by several communities.

Survivors, cargo, wreckage, and bodies come ashore.

## Immediate needs

Someone must decide:

- who searches;
- who pays;
- who can claim salvage;
- whether cargo can be seized;
- how survivors are housed;
- how deaths are recorded;
- who informs families.

## Why this matters politically

Rescue is both practical and moral.

A community may expect reciprocal help from people it otherwise competes with.

Repeated rescue cooperation can create durable obligations across:

- clan;
- harbor;
- island;
- polity.

## Result

**PASS for customary maritime obligations beyond ordinary borders.**

North Coast social cohesion can emerge from shared risk without requiring cultural uniformity.

This also gives concrete institutional meaning to the existing theme that survival requires cooperation.

---

# 7. Test 4 — harbor control and outside investment

## Condition

An outside merchant house or political power finances:

- docks;
- warehouses;
- breakwaters;
- shipyards;
- debt relief.

Over time it gains leverage over the harbor.

## Conflict

Local residents may welcome investment while fearing loss of:

- access;
- prices;
- political voice;
- repair capacity;
- fishing priority;
- strategic independence.

A clan may own many vessels but little harbor land.

A town may control the docks but depend on outside credit.

A ruler may claim sovereignty but lack money to maintain the port.

## Result

**PASS for separation of harbor control from wider sovereignty.**

Control of harbor infrastructure is major political power.

But:

> **right to use a harbor, ownership of harbor property, local civic authority, and sovereignty over the surrounding polity are not the same thing.**

This is especially important in relations with Port and foreign investors.

---

# 8. Test 5 — clan versus harbor jurisdiction

## Condition

A crew from a powerful clan commits an offense or breaks a contract while in a harbor governed by another authority.

The clan claims the dispute should be handled through its own obligations and compensation customs.

The harbor claims local law applies.

## What the system needs

A plausible resolution may require several linked questions:

- what happened in the harbor;
- what obligations attach to the crew;
- what the ship contract says;
- whether the clan will enforce compensation;
- whether the harbor can detain people or vessels;
- whether a wider political authority will recognize the result.

## Result

**PASS for overlapping personal and territorial jurisdiction.**

Clan affiliation remains politically real without turning clans into states everywhere they travel.

Harbor law remains real without automatically extinguishing outside obligations.

---

# 9. Test 6 — island winter supply failure

## Condition

Storms, ice, ship loss, or market disruption prevent expected food deliveries to an island.

## Local capacity

Island communities can:

- ration;
- use local stores;
- redirect boats;
- call on kin networks;
- postpone nonessential consumption.

## Wider dependence

A severe shortage may require:

- convoy priority;
- emergency purchase;
- outside shipping;
- credit;
- temporary price controls;
- political pressure on mainland merchants;
- relief from a larger ruler or coalition.

## Result

**PASS for local autonomy inside wider dependency.**

An island can retain strong local political identity while depending materially on systems it does not control.

That dependency gives larger authorities and merchants leverage, but not automatic legitimacy.

---

# 10. Test 7 — central fishing ban, convoy levy, or anti-piracy decree

## Condition

A larger ruler or coalition attempts one of the following:

- close a fishery;
- impose a convoy levy;
- prohibit private armed sailing;
- authorize privateers;
- require ships to sail in convoy;
- restrict foreign harbor access.

## What larger authority can plausibly do

It may control:

- official ports;
- customs;
- naval forces;
- recognized licenses;
- major courts;
- large merchant contracts.

## What it cannot assume

It may not directly control:

- every cove;
- every island boat;
- every clan;
- every seasonal anchorage;
- every customary fishing right;
- every smuggling route.

Policy therefore depends on cooperation from harbor authorities, crews, clans, and merchants.

## Result

**PASS for meaningful but negotiated central maritime power.**

A larger polity can matter greatly without possessing a modern monopoly over all maritime activity.

Attempts to centralize sea policy will produce bargaining, evasion, local resistance, and new political coalitions.

---

# 11. Test 8 — Port or Convergence maritime dispute

## Condition

A North Coast authority is accused of:

- blocking protected access;
- discriminating against foreign shipping;
- extending convoy rules beyond agreed waters;
- tolerating piracy;
- seizing cargo;
- violating a Port access guarantee.

## Who answers

The relevant actor may be:

- a ruler;
- harbor government;
- island polity;
- coalition;
- ship-owning authority;
- recognized signatory.

The Convergence should not assume one representative speaks for all North Coast communities.

## Evidence

A maritime dispute may rely on:

- harbor records;
- ship logs;
- crew testimony;
- convoy notices;
- contracts;
- charts;
- prior access practice;
- messages from other ports.

## Result

**PASS for actor-specific cross-border representation.**

A coastal cultural region can have shared traditions without possessing one diplomatic voice.

# 12. Functional map that survives all eight tests

| Level | Functions strongly supported | Functions that should not automatically be assigned here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household / crew | provisioning, routine labor, catch handling, onboard decisions | fishery access between groups, diplomacy |
| Clan / kin network | boats, credit, rescue, compensation, inherited claims, reputation | universal harbor law, territorial sovereignty everywhere members travel |
| Harbor / town | docking, markets, repair, storage, policing, fees, emergency closure | all offshore seasonal rights, all clan obligations |
| Fishery / seasonal-access system | access, timing, conservation response, rescue expectations, use disputes | unrelated inland law |
| Island / local polity | ordinary law, local defense, land taxation, local infrastructure | whole-coast policy |
| Larger polity / coalition | war, diplomacy, major convoy action, piracy suppression, strategic trade policy | direct command of every cove, crew, clan, or customary right |
| Port / Convergence | protected access, cross-border claims, agreed arbitration, treaty obligations | ordinary North Coast maritime administration |

This is a functional distribution, not a constitution.

---

# 13. Strong structural conclusion

North Coast currently works best as:

> **a layered maritime political order in which shore authority, harbor government, clan or kin obligation, and seasonal sea-use rights overlap.**

The tests strongly support:

1. strong harbor and island autonomy in ordinary local matters;
2. clan / kin institutions that remain politically meaningful across territorial boundaries;
3. fishery and anchorage rights that cannot be modeled as simple extensions of land borders;
4. mission-specific coordination for piracy, convoys, rescue, and major maritime emergencies;
5. larger political authorities capable of war, diplomacy, strategic investment, and some maritime regulation;
6. central maritime policy that depends on harbor, clan, crew, and merchant cooperation;
7. separation between harbor use, harbor property, civic government, and territorial sovereignty;
8. actor-specific Convergence representation rather than one cultural-region delegate.

The tests do **not** establish whether one crown, confederation, or other large polity dominates North Coast.

---

# 14. Political consequences

## Sea power is distributed

A ruler can possess soldiers and still lack enough:

- ships;
- pilots;
- harbor access;
- crews;
- credit;
- local knowledge

to dominate the coast.

A clan can possess ships without controlling major ports.

A harbor can possess infrastructure without commanding the crews that use it.

That distribution creates bargaining.

## Maritime coordination can create temporary governments without creating states

A convoy command, rescue coalition, fishery agreement, or anti-piracy pact may need:

- officers;
- contributions;
- signals;
- adjudication;
- records.

Those institutions can become politically important while remaining issue-specific.

Writing the Sequel should test which temporary arrangements outlive their emergency.

## Seasonal rights can become more politically durable than borders

Because fish, storms, and navigation move, people may defend old seasonal rights with greater intensity than a distant ruler's map.

That creates legal conflict which is neither purely territorial nor purely customary.

# 15. Writing the Sequel pressures

Possible later tests:

- an emergency convoy levy continues after piracy falls;
- a rescue pact becomes a general maritime court;
- a clan's historical anchorage right survives after the clan no longer lives nearby;
- a harbor financed by outsiders becomes economically dependent on them;
- a temporary fishery closure becomes precedent for wider conservation authority;
- an old anti-raider patrol becomes a ruler's first standing naval institution;
- a seasonal gathering for access disputes gradually becomes politically representative.

These are **development prompts**, not current canon.

---

# 16. Breath scene seeds

Keep these non-canon unless later promoted:

- two captains agree the fish moved and disagree completely about what that means for rights;
- a harbor official fines a famous clan vessel while privately asking the same clan for rescue help;
- an island household can identify ships by sail repair patterns before officials receive formal news;
- a convoy levy is hated most in the harbor that benefits most from it;
- a wreck dispute turns on whether rescuers saved cargo or stole it;
- an old anchorage marker is useless physically but politically explosive.

# 17. Still open

Do **not** settle without further testing:

- number of North Coast polities;
- whether one crown or confederation dominates;
- exact island representation;
- exact clan political status;
- exact fishery-access institutions;
- exact maritime law;
- exact convoy command structures;
- whether any standing regional fleet exists;
- privateering rules;
- salvage law;
- exact harbor ownership patterns;
- exact relation between inland chiefs and coastal authorities;
- exact Convergence signatories or delegates;
- whether historical North Coast control of Port is ever promoted from legacy candidate to canon.

Those questions should be answered against this functional model rather than by selecting a state form first.
