# Prose test

The temporary rule lasted long enough to acquire handwriting from four different clerks.

That was when the inspector knew it was becoming permanent.

Not permanent in law.

Permanent in the more dangerous sense:

> people had learned to expect it tomorrow.

Over several weeks, the roadhead had added:

- one guarded departure window;
- then two;
- transition verification for people already traveling when rules changed;
- a current route-status board;
- recognized escort marks;
- an inspection line that kept moving even when everyone complained.

Other roadheads had done similar things differently.

One used:

- toll receipts

for transition proof.

Another used:

- stable / station marks.

A third had better attack reporting and worse fee records.

The corridor compact eventually did what High Roads institutions often did when everybody had invented the same problem badly.

It compared them.

Then produced a common sheet.

The inspector considered this a bad sign.

Handwritten rules could still be temporary.

Printed rules had families.

The new sheet arrived with:

- three route marks;
- two town seals;
- one corridor compact reference;
- enough amendments to prove nobody had gotten exactly what they wanted.

The guard captain read over the inspector's shoulder.

"That ours?"

"Parts."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning everybody recognized enough of everybody else's practice to make one version."

"Same thing."

"No."

The captain looked at the seals.

"Who changed the departure windows?"

"Compromise."

"Who asked for that?"

"Everyone differently."

"Helpful."

The inspector turned the page.

---

The common sheet did not create one High Roads code.

It did something narrower.

For participating roadheads on this corridor:

### Guarded departures

Each roadhead would publish:

- guarded departure windows;
- available escort capacity;
- current route warnings.

### Verification

Carriers joining guarded departures needed:

- recognized commercial / cargo papers;
- current route notice;
- accepted transition evidence if already on the road when a rule changed.

### Inspection

Roadheads could verify:

- cargo marks;
- declared load class;
- carrier identity;
- passenger / guard count

where needed for:

- safe grouping;
- route-capacity decisions;
- guard planning.

### Contribution

Participating carriers paid:

- corridor-specific protection / administration charges

under local schedules.

The sheet explicitly said:

> **this is not a general High Roads levy.**

The inspector appreciated that line because someone had fought hard for it.

### Emergency adjustment

Participating route authorities could:

- delay;
- redirect;
- split

a guarded departure when:

- verified attack;
- route condition;
- capacity

made the original movement unsafe.

The inspector read that section twice.

"Too broad."

The guard captain nodded.

"Useful."

"Those are not opposites."

"They usually are."

---

By sunrise, the first argument had started.

A small carrier held up the new contribution schedule.

"I paid yesterday."

"Yesterday's departure."

"I paid for protection."

"You paid for yesterday's guarded departure."

"I am leaving today."

"Yes."

"Then protect me today."

"That is what today's charge is for."

The carrier stared.

"Do guards eat twice if I wait overnight?"

The guard captain said:

"Yes."

The carrier looked betrayed.

The inspector leaned over the counter.

"Your cargo can travel outside the guarded window."

The carrier looked toward the road.

"Safely?"

The inspector did not answer.

The carrier folded the fee notice.

"Exactly."

They paid.

Behind them, a larger merchant house presented:

- six wagons;
- hired guards;
- current route notice;
- recognized seals.

The clerk processing them moved faster.

The small carrier watched.

"Of course."

The inspector said:

"They have the documents."

"They have clerks."

"Also true."

"Same rule?"

"Yes."

"Same burden?"

The inspector looked at the six wagons.

"No."

That answer irritated both of them.

It was still true.

---

The system worked best when nobody noticed it.

Over the next weeks:

- a forged route advisory was caught because its mark did not match the current register;
- one overloaded wagon had been declared under a lighter load class and was moved out before a weak bridge crossing;
- two carriers were shifted from a departure before a closure created a dangerous backup;
- a guarded group changed route after a verified attack report.

Nothing spectacular happened.

That was the success.

Lenders began accepting the corridor notice as evidence of route status.

Some buyers preferred cargo booked through guarded windows.

A merchant house offered better financing terms for recognized departures.

The inspector heard about that from three people who wanted priority in the next window.

Success created queues.

Queues created complaints.

Complaints created more paper.

---

The compact sent another notice.

This one asked participating roadheads to preserve daily counts for:

- wagons;
- pack strings;
- guards;
- passengers inside guarded departures;
- broad cargo classes;
- delays;
- route diversions.

The inspector read it.

Then read it again.

The guard captain said:

"Good."

"Why?"

"Planning."

"For what?"

"Escorts."

"We already plan escorts."

"Badly."

The inspector looked at the columns.

"Why cargo class?"

"Some loads move slower."

"Why guards?"

"If six houses bring enough private guards, I assign differently."

That made sense.

The inspector disliked it.

Not because it was wrong.

Because the table answered more than one question.

How many wagons moved?

How many armed guards were already present?

Which loads dominated the corridor?

How quickly could the roadhead group protected traffic?

The guard captain saw none of that as ominous.

Why would they?

They needed tomorrow's plan.

The inspector opened a ledger.

"Fine."

---

Not every roadhead accepted every part.

One town refused the full passenger count.

Another accepted inspection but set its own contribution schedule.

A pastoral-route authority insisted:

- seasonal herd movement

could not be treated as commercial cargo.

The compact revised the sheet.

Again.

That was High Roads governance.

The inspector found this comforting.

A rule nobody argued with was probably governing nobody.

---

An itinerant family arrived with:

- two carts;
- livestock;
- three children;
- no intention of joining a guarded commercial departure.

The inspector began explaining the process.

The oldest adult stopped them.

"We are not cargo."

"I know."

"Then stop counting us like it."

The inspector looked at the daily ledger.

Passengers.

Animals.

Departure.

They understood the objection.

"The count is for guarded movement."

"We are not taking it."

The inspector stopped writing.

"Local road?"

"Yes."

"No guarded window?"

"No."

"Then I do not need you in this ledger."

The adult waited.

"So the rule has sense."

"Occasionally."

The family passed.

The inspector added a note for the next compact review:

> **guarded-departure count only; do not absorb ordinary local / seasonal movement.**

The rule became narrower.

Also better.

---

The first serious test came when an attack report arrived shortly before a guarded departure.

Not rumor.

Verified through:

- road station mark;
- two independent carrier reports;
- one injured guard.

The guard captain read it.

"Hold."

The inspector looked at the yard.

Twenty-three wagons.

Nine pack strings.

Several passengers.

Thirty-one private guards.

The number came to them without counting.

That bothered them.

"How long?"

"Until the east patrol checks the lower cut."

"Could be hours."

"Yes."

"Yard is full."

"Yes."

"Next window backs up."

"Yes."

"Merchants scream."

"Yes."

The inspector looked at the report.

"Hold."

The captain raised the signal.

The gate stayed closed.

People shouted immediately.

The inspector spent the next hour explaining:

- verified attack;
- temporary hold;
- reassessment.

A large merchant threatened complaint.

A small carrier threatened to leave unguarded.

A passenger demanded refund.

The inspector gave none of them what they wanted.

Two hours later the patrol returned.

The attack had happened.

The road was clear now.

The guarded departure left late.

Everybody arrived.

The next day, complaints were filed.

So were thank-you notes.

More complaints.

The inspector preferred those.

They were easier to believe.

---

By the end of the next review period, the roadhead had:

- a guarded-departure board;
- an inspection register;
- daily movement counts;
- a current route-status ledger;
- a list of recognized escort providers;
- a note beside each provider showing whether they were available for the next several departures;
- a procedure for verified emergency hold.

The inspector looked at the shelves.

None of this had existed together before the pressure began.

Each piece solved something ordinary.

The guard captain came in.

"Need tomorrow's provider list."

"It is there."

"Current?"

"As of noon."

"Good."

The captain took the sheet.

The inspector looked at:

- movement counts;
- guard counts;
- route status;
- escort availability;
- departure windows.

No army.

No mobilization.

No war.

Just a safer road.

They returned to the ledger.

That was enough.
