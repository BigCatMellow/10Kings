# Mythic Creature Community Variants — V1

## Status

**Provisional author-side cultural attachment pass — not canon.**

This page takes the strongest legend families from:

- [Mythic Creature Legend Families — Synthesis V1](Mythic-Creature-Legend-Families-Synthesis-V1.md)

and attaches them to **existing in-world community types**.

It deliberately does **not** finalize:

- names;
- exact anatomy;
- objective supernatural truth;
- specific famous sightings;
- exact dates.

The goal is to establish:

> **who tells which version, why that version makes sense there, how the story traveled, and what evidence each community believes matters.**

Related culture work:

- [Cultural Analogue Coverage Assessment — V1](Cultural-Analogue-Coverage-Assessment-V1.md);
- [Nomads and Itinerant Peoples](../Culture/Nomads.md);
- regional political-order vertical slices;
- [Religions](../Politics/Religions.md).

---

# 1. Shared-creature design rule

A legend family is not one story translated into several languages.

Use:

~~~text
shared historical / mythic core
+
community livelihood
+
local ecology
+
local institutions
+
local religion
+
local history
=
local oicotype
~~~

Every local version should answer:

> **Why would these people tell it this way?**

If the answer is only:

> because they live in this region,

the variant is not specific enough.

---

# 1A. A culture does not have one version either

Cross-cultural variation is only half the rule.

Within one community, the same legend should also differ by:

- age;
- occupation;
- class;
- faith;
- education;
- migration generation;
- institutional role.

Use the age-layer test developed in:

- [Youth, First Work, and Courtship — Comparative Research V1](Youth-First-Work-Comparative-Research-V1.md);
- [Youth and First-Work Community Profiles — V1](Youth-First-Work-Community-Profiles-V1.md).

For each major creature, ask:

### Child version

What simple rule / image survives?

### Young-worker version

How is it:

- mocked;
- dared;
- tested;
- turned into peer status?

### Experienced-worker version

What practical knowledge is encoded?

### Elder / family-memory version

Which old event / sighting belongs to family history?

### Institutional version

How do:

- scholar;
- clerk;
- guild;
- priest;
- court

classify / reinterpret it?

Therefore:

> **"the North Coast version" or "the Longwood version" is still too broad unless the context genuinely requires only that scale.**

# 2. Tier 1 family — The Knocking Below

## Shared anchors

Preserve across variants:

1. heard before seen;
2. repeated knock / tap;
3. associated with underground danger;
4. experienced workers take the pattern more seriously than outsiders do.

Everything else may vary.

---

## 2A. SH-A industrial mining district

### Local meaning

Primarily **worker safety lore**.

Older crews may stop work after a recognized sequence even when:

- supervisors;
- engineers;
- owners

say no formal hazard has been detected.

The creature may be described less as an animal and more as:

> **the one working where no crew has been sent.**

### Political meaning

This version accumulates labor history.

Worker saying:

> the deep gives warning before the office does.

Management response may be:

- superstition;
- useful shorthand for acoustic warning signs;
- dangerous work stoppage custom;
- embarrassing relic.

### Evidence cited

- accident logs where knocks were reported beforehand;
- survivor testimony;
- mine songs;
- old warning marks;
- accounts of sounds in sealed headings.

### Likely transmission

Mine labor migration between:

- SH-A districts;
- smaller Stone Hills workings;
- Underpass works.

---

## 2B. Small Stone Hills mine / craft settlement

### Local meaning

More personalized.

The presence may:

- warn good workers;
- mislead careless workers;
- refuse to warn the greedy.

This version blends more easily with:

- Eternal Forge moral language;
- household work traditions.

### Evidence cited

- family stories;
- inherited tools associated with a famous warning;
- one abandoned shaft nobody opens.

### Difference from industrial version

Industrial miners may call this version:

> children's dressing around a real warning tradition.

Small-settlement miners may answer that:

> the city crews only remember half the story.

---

## 2C. Underpass maintenance / tunnel crews

### Local meaning

Less tied to extraction.

More associated with:

- old tunnels;
- water;
- structural movement;
- routes nobody remembers building.

Some crews distinguish:

- ordinary rock noise;
- "working knocks";
- "answering knocks."

Exact terminology remains open.

### Evidence cited

- sounds from sealed branches;
- impossible response rhythm;
- maps that do not match where the sound came from.

### Supernatural remainder

This is where the family can retain its strongest genuinely difficult evidence.

---

## 2D. Port technical / scholarly version

### Local meaning

Port scholars may collapse several mine traditions into one category.

Possible interpretation:

- acoustic folklore;
- occupational risk culture;
- extinct underground animal theory.

### Cultural conflict

Miners dislike scholarly illustrations of a creature **nobody underground claims to have seen clearly**.

This becomes a recurring joke:

> Port finally drew the thing the miners only hear.

---

# 3. Tier 1 family — The Great Mouth / False Island

## Shared anchors

1. enormous marine presence;
2. still enough to resemble reef / shoal / dark water;
3. fish gather around or within it;
4. movement reveals the mistake.

---

## 3A. NC-I island communities

### Local meaning

Most literal version.

Some fishers insist:

> it was / is a real animal.

Not:

- god;
- demon;
- world-ending monster.

Simply:

> very large, very rare, and not something sensible people approach.

### Practical function

Teaches:

- do not rush unusual fish concentrations;
- observe water movement;
- respect unfamiliar feeding behavior.

### Evidence cited

- old captain logs;
- family route songs;
- bone fragments;
- place names associated with sightings.

### Attitude toward Port

Often irritated that Port versions:

- make it larger;
- give it horns / fins / impossible anatomy;
- call it supernatural.

---

## 3B. NC-M major harbor tradition

### Local meaning

More historical / occupational.

People may use the story to discuss:

- lost fisheries;
- older seas;
- what sailors used to see before traffic / hunting / ecological change.

### Skeptical version

The "creature" is:

- several whale species;
- rare feeding;
- storytelling.

### Believer version

Those explanations account for ordinary sightings, not the old largest accounts.

---

## 3C. Great Weaver coastal branch

### Local meaning

Not a monster.

A great sea-being may be interpreted as:

- unusually important thread in marine life;
- old reminder that large beings shape the food around them.

The moral is not:

> do not anger it.

Closer to:

> the sea contains relationships larger than human use.

### Important limit

Do not make this universal Great Weaver doctrine.

It is a coastal local interpretation.

---

## 3D. Port version

### Local meaning

Three competing forms:

- natural-history controversy;
- festival spectacle;
- children's cautionary tale.

Popular simplified line:

> don't step onto an island that breathes.

### Transmission

North Coast sailors  
→ Port logbooks / taverns / archives  
→ illustrators / printers / festival performers  
→ exaggerated version travels inland  
→ North Coast hears its own story returned distorted.

This is an excellent **return loop**.

---

# 4. Tier 1 family — The Marsh Lure

## Shared anchors

1. light or eye-like glow;
2. appears near water / wet ground;
3. seems to remain just ahead;
4. following it leads off the expected route.

The creature may not have a stable body in every version.

---

## 4A. Low Rivers floodplain village

### Local meaning

A practical water-warning tradition.

People may say:

> walking lights mean walking ground.

Meaning:

- channels shifted;
- floodwater changed;
- familiar path is not safe.

### Practical behavior

- do not cross alone;
- wait for daylight;
- use a pole / guide;
- do not trust remembered banks after flood.

### Evidence cited

- disappearances;
- repeated lights in known overflow ground;
- family stories of people emerging miles away.

---

## 4B. Low Rivers irrigation community

### Local meaning

The lights are associated with:

- abandoned channels;
- old ditches;
- disputed water;
- neglected gates.

The creature may be said to:

> follow water people stopped remembering.

### Social meaning

Can become a story about:

- maintenance;
- forgetting old infrastructure;
- respecting inherited water knowledge.

---

## 4C. LR-M / river-market workers

### Local meaning

More skeptical.

Boat crews may use the creature's name as shorthand for:

- bad visibility;
- false lantern;
- drifting reflection.

But some refuse to whistle / call toward a light they cannot place.

### Important tone

People can mock a belief while still obeying the associated custom.

---

## 4D. Old Cities migrant version

Low Rivers workers settling near Old Cities canals reinterpret the lights through canal-guardian / charter traditions.

Possible shift:

> the light no longer lures; it **marks stolen water** or an abandoned channel.

This is a strong example of cultural hybridization.

---

## 4E. Port version

Port romanticizes it.

Urban stories may make it:

- drowned lover;
- ghost;
- festival light.

Low Rivers visitors complain that:

> Port turned a flood warning into a love story.

---

# 5. Tier 1 family — The Forest Owner

## Shared anchors

1. boundary / use violation;
2. disorientation;
3. familiar path behaves wrongly;
4. punishment usually consists of being turned around / delayed rather than killed.

---

## 5A. LW-I interior use-right community

### Local meaning

Not necessarily "a creature."

Could be:

> **the forest behaving as though violated rights matter.**

People may personify it differently.

Core idea:

- no one owns every use;
- taking one right does not grant another;
- a person who ignores that becomes unable to move correctly through the place.

### Practical function

Encodes:

- seasonal restrictions;
- protected ground;
- route knowledge;
- local permission.

### Evidence cited

- lost outsiders;
- moved markers;
- repeated loops;
- specific places where people become disoriented.

---

## 5B. LW-W western edge community

### Local meaning

More political.

The figure becomes associated with:

- concession disputes;
- outside timber crews;
- road projects.

Some reformers may dismiss the supernatural claim but deliberately invoke the story rhetorically:

> passage isn't cutting; the forest knows the difference even if your contract doesn't.

### Internal conflict

Local merchants may consider this:

- useful cultural shorthand;
- obstructionist superstition;
- both.

---

## 5C. Longwood specialist / warden version

### Local meaning

Wardens may be among the most skeptical.

They know:

- terrain;
- seasonal path change;
- marker loss;
- acoustic confusion.

Yet they may maintain customs derived from the legend because those customs work.

### Interesting possibility

Wardens distinguish:

- **being lost**;
- and **being turned**.

They claim the second has recognizable signs.

Whether those signs have supernatural meaning remains open.

---

## 5D. Great Weaver local branch

### Local meaning

Potentially spiritualized:

- not one humanoid spirit;
- a way of speaking about the relationship among path, use, memory, and obligation.

This fits the Great Weaver better than a universal forest god.

---

## 5E. Outsider / Port version

Often literalizes the being into:

- wild man;
- antlered figure;
- tree creature.

Longwood residents may find these images:

- amusing;
- offensive;
- childish;
- commercially useful.

Different people react differently.

---

# 6. Tier 1 family — The Weather Animal

## Shared anchors

1. rare animal-like sighting;
2. associated with abrupt dangerous weather;
3. low / heavy / difficult-to-classify form;
4. physical evidence exists but is disputed.

---

## 6A. HR-N herding households

### Local meaning

Practical weather sign.

If sighted at a particular elevation / slope:

- move animals;
- leave exposed pasture;
- avoid one route.

People may disagree whether:

> the animal predicts weather

or:

> responds to weather earlier than humans do.

That distinction need not matter practically.

### Evidence cited

- tracks;
- herd behavior;
- recurring locations;
- grandparents' route diaries.

---

## 6B. HR-W pass communities

### Local meaning

More dangerous / political.

A sighting can become:

- argument for closing a pass;
- reason to delay convoy movement.

This creates incentives to:

- fake sightings;
- deny sightings.

That is useful.

### Political complication

Merchants may accuse route authorities of using folklore to justify closures.

A real cryptid claim can therefore become economically contested.

---

## 6C. HR-C scholarly / market version

### Local meaning

HR-C accumulates:

- skins;
- sketches;
- contradictory traveler reports;
- alleged bones.

Naturalists debate whether several local names refer to:

- one species;
- multiple animals;
- no species at all.

This gives High Roads its own scholarly tradition rather than outsourcing every intellectual question to Port.

---

## 6D. Mobile pastoral circuit

### Local meaning

More protective.

The animal is not causing the storm.

It appears **because competent life leaves before danger**.

Therefore:

> follow what knows when to move.

This integrates particularly well with transhumant culture.

---

## 6E. Port version

Inflated into:

- "mountain dragon";
- illustrated beast;
- expedition target.

High Roads people may hate the term.

---

# 7. Tier 1 family — The Charter Beast

## Shared anchors

1. creature mark / image appears on old legal / boundary material;
2. tied specifically to water / land rights;
3. said to remember a true boundary;
4. violating a recorded boundary attracts consequence.

---

## 7A. OC-U upstream legal tradition

### Local meaning

Primarily archaic legal symbolism.

Jurists may insist:

> the beast does not "guard" the grant; it marks the category / authority under which the grant was recorded.

Popular stories disagree.

### Evidence cited

- old charter stones;
- seals;
- headworks records;
- recurring iconography.

### Political use

Parties deliberately invoke the creature when accusing someone of:

- illegal diversion;
- erased boundary;
- charter fraud.

---

## 7B. OC-D canal-neighborhood version

### Local meaning

Much more alive.

Stories say it:

- walks dry canal edges;
- appears where flow has been stolen;
- scratches / marks altered boundaries.

### Practical role

Children and workers learn:

- do not move markers;
- do not secretly alter gates;
- report unexpected flow changes.

### Social meaning

The creature may be seen as a defender of downstream people against upstream abuse.

---

## 7C. OC-C coastal version

### Local meaning

Transformed through commerce.

Its image may appear on:

- merchant seals;
- arbitration houses;
- bonded storage;
- guarantee documents.

Meaning shifts from:

> remembers the canal boundary

toward:

> **remembers the agreement.**

This is a real cultural transformation, not a translation.

---

## 7D. Rural irrigation community

### Local meaning

Less formal legal theory, more place-based folklore.

A local version may be associated with:

- boundary stones;
- old gate ruins;
- one watercourse.

It may have a more animal-like body than urban legal imagery.

---

## 7E. Great Weaver-influenced reinterpretation

The creature may be described not as punisher but **witness**:

> land and water remember what people agreed to.

This can coexist with older legal symbolism without requiring common theology.

---

# 8. Tier 1 family — The Road Hound

## Shared anchors

1. dark dog-like form;
2. solitary;
3. unusually quiet;
4. appears to people in transit / at thresholds;
5. follows, leads, or waits rather than attacking directly.

This family should have the **widest cultural spread**.

---

## 8A. Appalachian-influenced mobile repair / seasonal-work network

### Local meaning

Ambivalent route companion.

Common possibilities:

- appearing before a safe stopping place;
- following someone who should not travel alone;
- appearing when a family member will not return.

The same household may know both protective and death-associated stories.

### Why it fits

This community is likely to value:

- route memory;
- kin continuity;
- ballads;
- repeated stopping places.

The Hound can become a carrier of **route memory**.

### Evidence cited

- family songs;
- repeated sightings on the same road decades apart;
- dogs appearing at camps where no settlement is nearby.

---

## 8B. High Roads caravan version

### Local meaning

More practical:

> if it travels ahead, do not leave the road.

Some caravan families treat this as:

- joke;
- omen;
- actual rule.

### Possible natural core

Large guardian / feral dogs may have historically followed caravans.

---

## 8C. NC-R / North Coast roadhead version

### Local meaning

Associated with people moving between:

- inland road;
- coast;
- ship.

Some stories connect the Hound with travelers / sailors who:

- never completed the journey home.

It may wait facing inland or seaward depending on local version.

---

## 8D. Infinite Compass branches

Possible religious interpretation:

- traveler guide;
- sign that the road continues;
- symbolic story about not traveling alone.

Another branch may reject literal belief entirely.

Do not make it official doctrine.

---

## 8E. Port children's version

Friendly / softened:

> the dark dog brings lost festival children back to a road they know.

Adults may use it in:
- puppet shows;
- street stories;
- signs.

Mobile visitors may find this version bizarre because their own stories include death.

---

## 8F. Rationalist explanation

- working dogs;
- scavengers;
- road dogs;
- memory convergence.

### Supernatural remainder

Certain cross-cultural features remain oddly stable:

- silence;
- solitary appearance;
- threshold / route timing.

No one has explained why those survive more consistently than body details.

---

# 9. Tier 1 family — The Estuary Captive

## Shared anchors

1. something living was caught in Port waters;
2. sufficiently humanlike to create legal / moral confusion;
3. did not communicate normally;
4. was held by Port authority;
5. returned / escaped to the water;
6. some official record survived.

This tradition originates in Port but develops **many cultural interpretations**.

---

## 9A. Old Port harbor-family version

### Local meaning

Primarily an ethical story.

The important question is not:

> what was it?

It is:

> **what did Port do to something it did not understand?**

Some families preserve:

- shame;
- anger;
- sympathy.

The captive may be treated almost as:

- stranger;
- foreigner;
- victim.

### Political use

Modern Port reformers may invoke the case in arguments about:

- migrant rights;
- detention;
- authority over outsiders.

This should remain contested, not a neat allegory.

---

## 9B. Port official / legal version

### Local meaning

An archaic jurisdiction case.

Questions include:

- was it property;
- animal;
- person;
- foreign subject;
- unknown category?

Later legal writers may cite the case as an embarrassment because old law had no category for it.

### Evidence cited

- custody entry;
- net / harbor record;
- payment / food account;
- partial examination record.

---

## 9C. Port naturalist version

### Local meaning

Famous zoological puzzle.

Competing explanations:

- marine mammal;
- human;
- foreign diver;
- hoax;
- unknown species.

The official physical description is inconsistent because:

- several people recorded it;
- terms changed;
- copies differ.

---

## 9D. North Coast fisher versions

Multiple.

One harbor says:

> obviously a known marine animal badly described by Port people.

Another:

> no known animal behaves like the old record says.

Outer-island stories may connect it to older sea-being traditions.

---

## 9E. Religious variants

### Great Weaver

Some local tellers interpret the case through:

- kinship between forms of life;
- the moral failure of treating the unknown as object.

### Radiant Path

Possible moral story about:

- cruelty;
- justice toward the powerless.

### Silent Whisper

May emphasize:

- the limits of classification;
- knowing when not knowing is honest.

These are **local teachings**, not universal doctrine.

---

## 9F. Three Moons popular version

The captive becomes:

- mask;
- puppet;
- song;
- cheap carved figure.

This produces genuine conflict over:

> who owns a painful old story once it becomes folklore?

Excellent Port texture.

# 10. Tier 2 consolidation decisions

The research produced four additional families.

Do not automatically promote them separately.

---

## Ore-Spoiler

### Working decision

**Keep as a sister tradition to The Knocking Below, not yet a separate major family.**

Reason:

- both belong to mine / underground occupational culture;
- together they show that one community can have several underground beings / explanations;
- separating too early risks creating a fantasy mine bestiary.

Could later become distinct if:

- metallurgy scenes;
- Stone Hills cultural pass

give it enough independent history.

---

## Archive Beast

### Working decision

**Treat primarily as a Port process, not necessarily a separate animal.**

It may be:

- Port's scholarly reconstruction of Great Mouth;
- a different carcass;
- conflation of several North Coast creatures.

This ambiguity is more valuable than locking a separate beast.

---

## Wind Guardian

### Working decision

**Keep as Old Cities iconographic / religious family in reserve.**

It may later intersect with:

- Charter Beast;
- city architecture;
- seasonal sickness lore.

Do not merge yet.

---

## Night Road

### Working decision

**Keep as route phenomenon separate from Road Hound but below Tier 1.**

Reason:

- the Road Hound is creature-specific;
- Night Road is procession / sound / memory.

Mobile-circuit research may eventually make it important.

---

# 11. Transmission routes now required by the world

The creature layer should use the same cultural-transmission systems as everything else.

## Mine migration

Carries:

- Knocking Below;
- Ore-Spoiler.

Path:

~~~text
Stone Hills mine
→ contract / migrant crews
→ Underpass work
→ other mine district
→ Port technical account
→ back to miners in distorted scholarly form
~~~

---

## Maritime transmission

Carries:

- Great Mouth;
- Estuary Captive interpretation;
- Archive Beast imagery.

Path:

~~~text
island / fishing community
→ harbor crews
→ Port
→ archive / print / festival
→ inland culture
→ story returns to coast
~~~

---

## River / canal migration

Carries:

- Marsh Lure;
- Charter Beast variants.

Path:

~~~text
Low Rivers worker / boat household
↔ Old Cities canal work
→ hybrid light / canal-guardian story
→ market / Port circulation
~~~

---

## Longwood / High Roads contact

Carries:

- Forest Owner;
- route / weather motifs.

Path:

~~~text
forest-edge road
→ guide / caravan contact
→ High Roads telling
→ outsider literal monster version
→ returns through merchants / workers
~~~

---

## Mobile circuits

Carry:

- Road Hound;
- Night Road;
- fragments of every other family.

This should make itinerant people some of the world's most important **myth transmitters**.

Not because they are mystical.

Because they physically carry stories across repeated routes.

---

# 12. Evidence cultures differ too

Communities should disagree not only about the creature but about **what counts as evidence**.

## Mine crews

Value:

- experienced testimony;
- sound pattern;
- accident history.

## Port scholars

Value:

- specimen;
- copied record;
- comparative classification.

## Longwood use-right communities

Value:

- place memory;
- repeated local experience;
- who knows the path.

## Old Cities legal culture

Value:

- charter;
- seal;
- boundary stone;
- archival continuity.

## High Roads mobile / pastoral communities

Value:

- route diary;
- herd behavior;
- repeated seasonal observation.

## Religious storytellers

May value:

- moral consistency;
- ritual continuity;
- preserved sacred / teaching tradition.

This is important.

Two people can possess the same facts and disagree because their **epistemic traditions differ**.

---

# 13. The myth layer should produce ordinary disagreement

Examples of normal conversation:

> "There are no Forest Owners. There are people who don't know how to walk in managed woods."

> "That's what someone who got turned says afterward."

---

> "The Great Mouth was a whale."

> "Nobody said it wasn't."

---

> "The Hound is a death omen."

> "Only coast people say that."

---

> "That carving is a charter mark."

> "Then why does my grandmother leave water under it?"

---

> "The knocks are timber."

> "Then you stay when they start."

This should be the target tone.

Belief is not binary.

---

# 14. Relationship to cultural analogue work

These legend variants should **not substitute for actual cultural development**.

Instead, the future community-to-analogue matrix should ask:

- what real-world folklore / oral-history mechanisms help us understand this community's version?
- how does the creature tradition reflect:
  - livelihood;
  - kinship;
  - law;
  - migration;
  - religion;
  - ecology?

This creates a two-way loop:

~~~text
community research
→ better local legend

local legend
→ reveals missing community detail
→ better community research
~~~

# 15. Current strongest supernatural-remainder candidates

The world should not make every creature equally mysterious.

Working distribution:

## Mostly natural / historical

- Great Mouth;
- Weather Animal.

They may involve:

- extinct / rare species;
- genuine natural behavior.

## Mostly cultural / institutional

- Charter Beast;
- much of Forest Owner tradition.

## Strong natural phenomenon + uncertain stories

- Marsh Lure;
- Knocking Below.

## Strongest genuinely unresolved candidates

### Road Hound

Why:

- shared anchors remain stable across highly different communities;
- alleged repeated individual appearances;
- boundary disappearances.

### Estuary Captive

Why:

- alleged physical custody;
- official record;
- biological / legal ambiguity;
- one detail potentially resistant to ordinary explanation.

### Deep Underpass form of Knocking Below

Why:

- sound where ordinary explanations become weakest;
- sealed works / impossible direction.

Do not confirm any of these.

This simply tells authors where to preserve the hardest uncertainty.

---

# 16. Next development dependency

The creature project is now blocked less by creature research than by **culture specificity**.

Before names / famous tales are finalized, the next useful paired pass is:

> **instantiate the first mobile circuits and community-to-analogue profiles, then use those communities to generate culturally earned creature names / variants.**

The Road Hound is especially dependent on this.

Its most important version should probably belong to an actual mobile people rather than a generic category called:

> mobile workers.

Therefore the next culture task and the next myth task now converge.
