# Low Rivers Functional Governance Test

## Status

**Provisional structural test.**

This page tests which political and economic functions the Low Rivers actually needs before assigning a final constitutional label.

It does **not** establish:

- one Low Rivers state;
- a ruler or title;
- a capital;
- exact borders;
- succession or electoral rules;
- named ministries, courts, water boards, or assemblies;
- one uniform land-tenure system;
- one uniform irrigation system.

The working question is:

> **Which functions must be coordinated across the Low Rivers, and which should remain local or institution-specific?**

---

# 1. Existing constraints

The Low Rivers already contains or historically contains:

- river towns;
- regional cities;
- estates;
- tenant villages;
- smallholders;
- cooperatives;
- irrigation leagues or water associations;
- seed-sharing systems;
- mills;
- storage;
- market towns;
- river navigation;
- crossings;
- creditors;
- politically significant land and grain interests.

The current abundance crisis also establishes that high production does not automatically produce prosperity.

Storage, credit, transport, buyer timing, land debt, and market access can turn abundance into distress.

That means the political system must be able to handle **coordination failures**, not merely scarcity.

---

# 2. Do not treat "local" and "central" as opposites

The Low Rivers problem is not:

> local government or central government?

Several recurring systems do not fit cleanly inside either category.

A water association may cross:

- village boundaries;
- estate boundaries;
- town jurisdictions;
- political borders.

A river town may regulate a market used by people from several rural jurisdictions.

An estate may exercise substantial authority over tenants while still depending on outside courts, roads, water allocations, markets, and military protection.

A cooperative may manage storage or seed without governing crime, diplomacy, or taxation.

The useful model is therefore **layered authority with functional jurisdictions**.

---

# 3. Minimum functions by scale

## Household / farm

Needs practical control over:

- planting and harvest decisions within recognized rights;
- household seed and food stores;
- livestock and tools;
- ordinary contracts and debts;
- family labor and inheritance where local law allows.

This level cannot solve shared water, flood, transport, or market problems alone.

## Village / local community

Likely needs capacity for some combination of:

- common wells, ditches, drainage, or local water turns;
- minor road and path maintenance;
- shared grazing or commons where they exist;
- seed exchange;
- mutual aid;
- local dispute mediation;
- emergency labor;
- local storage or cooperative organization.

Exact form can vary sharply from place to place.

## Estate

An estate can plausibly control:

- land leases or tenancy obligations;
- estate labor;
- private storage;
- estate roads and works;
- rents;
- advances or credit;
- local patronage.

But an estate should not automatically be sovereign.

It still depends on wider systems for:

- river water;
- crossings;
- external markets;
- recognized contracts;
- military security;
- higher dispute forums;
- export access.

## Town / city

River and market towns need authority over recurring urban and commercial problems such as:

- markets;
- weights and measures in local use;
- quays and streets;
- public order;
- warehouses and fire risk;
- crossings;
- sanitation;
- local courts or dispute forums;
- cargo and property records;
- emergency response.

A major market town can therefore be politically important without ruling the surrounding countryside.

## River / watershed

Some functions require a jurisdiction that follows water rather than ordinary political boundaries.

Likely recurring needs include:

- flow and river-condition information;
- navigation notices;
- irrigation scheduling;
- upstream/downstream allocation;
- maintenance of shared channels, embankments, gates, or diversions where they exist;
- flood warnings;
- emergency repair;
- dispute resolution among users who do not share one local government.

This does **not** require one Low Rivers water ministry.

It does require recognized mechanisms above the individual village or estate.

## Regional / polity scale

Larger political authorities are needed somewhere in the system for functions such as:

- taxation or large-scale revenue;
- military mobilization;
- major public works;
- inter-city or inter-district enforcement;
- emergency procurement or reserves;
- broad export or customs policy where claimed;
- large debt or property rules;
- diplomacy;
- Convergence obligations.

The tests below do not prove that all these powers belong to one ruler or one state.

## Cross-border / Convergence scale

The Convergence becomes relevant when disputes concern protected passage, recognized claims, cross-border infrastructure, treaty obligations, or conduct between recognized political actors.

It should not become the ordinary government of Low Rivers agriculture.

# 4. Test 1 — bumper harvest and collapsing prices

## Condition

The current abundance crisis produces:

- unusually high yields;
- full granaries;
- falling local prices;
- delayed merchant purchases;
- indebted growers;
- distressed land sales.

## What households / villages can do

They can:

- delay some sales;
- share labor;
- consolidate loads;
- use cooperative storage where available;
- renegotiate local obligations;
- preserve seed and household reserves.

But these measures fail when storage is already full or debt deadlines arrive before buyers do.

## What towns can do

Market towns can:

- extend market days;
- regulate handling and weighing;
- open or prioritize public / charitable storage if such capacity exists;
- organize temporary yards;
- publish prices;
- accelerate cargo processing.

They cannot create distant demand or unlimited warehouse capacity.

## What estates and creditors can do

Large estates can often hold grain longer than smallholders.

Creditors can determine who has enough time to wait.

That turns storage and credit into political power.

## What larger political authority can do

A larger authority might:

- defer taxes;
- purchase emergency reserves;
- finance transport;
- release public storage;
- alter export restrictions;
- guarantee some credit;
- prohibit or regulate certain distressed sales.

But a decree setting a higher grain price does not by itself create:

- empty warehouses;
- carts;
- river capacity;
- solvent buyers;
- export routes.

## Result

**PASS for layered authority.**

The crisis requires policy capacity above the village, but implementation depends on town, storage, transport, credit, and local agricultural institutions.

A strong center can change incentives.

It cannot replace the physical market system.

---

# 5. Test 2 — major flood

## Condition

Heavy rain and river rise threaten:

- farms;
- villages;
- quays;
- bridges;
- warehouses;
- dikes or embankments where present;
- navigation.

## Immediate response

The first useful authority is local because:

- people know which paths flood first;
- nearby labor can move livestock and goods;
- local crews can clear channels;
- town officials can close quays or crossings.

Waiting for a distant ruler would be destructive.

## Wider response

Local action alone is insufficient when one community's intervention pushes water onto another.

Flood management may require:

- shared warning;
- coordinated openings or closures;
- labor mobilization;
- repair material;
- temporary suspension of navigation;
- decisions about which infrastructure receives priority.

## Result

**PASS for watershed-scale coordination plus local execution.**

Floods strongly support institutions that can coordinate several communities without erasing local knowledge.

A larger polity can supply resources and coercive enforcement when necessary, but routine flood competence should not depend entirely on the capital.

---

# 6. Test 3 — drought or upstream water conflict

## Condition

An upstream community, estate, or town takes more water during a dry period.

Downstream users lose:

- irrigation;
- mill flow;
- drinking water;
- navigation depth;
- livestock access.

## Failure of purely local rule

If every landholder can act only by local right, upstream control becomes downstream coercion.

The harmed users need:

- recognized allocation rules;
- evidence about flows and prior use;
- a forum outside the immediate upstream authority;
- enforcement that can reach more than one village or estate.

## Political complication

Water rights may not all come from the same source.

Claims can be based on:

- old custom;
- estate privilege;
- town charter;
- irrigation membership;
- seasonal rotation;
- later treaty or political law.

The system therefore needs translation among claims, not necessarily one uniform water code.

## Cross-border version

If the river crosses a political boundary, the dispute can move into:

- bilateral agreement;
- attached Convergence arrangements;
- arbitration;
- reciprocal pressure.

## Result

**PASS for functional river jurisdiction.**

The strongest conclusion from this test is:

> **some Low Rivers authority must follow the watershed rather than the ordinary map.**

That authority can be an association, compact, court, customary forum, delegated office, or several different mechanisms in different basins.

Exact form remains open.

---

# 7. Test 4 — estate versus tenant dispute

## Condition

A large estate claims a tenant has:

- missed rent;
- failed a labor obligation;
- damaged irrigation;
- planted an unauthorized crop;
- violated a debt agreement.

The tenant claims:

- the estate changed the terms;
- flood or drought excused performance;
- customary rights were ignored;
- measurements are false;
- the estate is using debt to force a land transfer.

## What fails

The estate cannot be the only meaningful judge if the setting is to sustain real legal conflict rather than private sovereignty.

The tenant needs at least some path to:

- village testimony;
- town or district court;
- religious or customary mediation;
- guild / cooperative support where relevant;
- higher political appeal or review where recognized.

## Result

**PASS for layered land authority.**

Estates can be powerful without being fully sovereign.

Land tenure can remain locally diverse while larger authority still defines limits on enforcement, recognized contracts, or appeals.

This also gives tenant politics somewhere to operate other than immediate rebellion.

---

# 8. Test 5 — storage bottleneck

## Condition

Harvest arrives faster than cargo can leave.

Warehouses fill.

Grain begins to sit in:

- farm sheds;
- carts;
- temporary yards;
- mills;
- unsafe improvised spaces.

## Who gains power

Actors with access to dry storage can wait.

Actors without it must sell.

That can advantage:

- estates;
- merchant houses;
- warehouse operators;
- creditors;
- cooperatives with collective facilities.

## Governance needs

Towns need rules around:

- fire;
- spoilage;
- inspection;
- queueing;
- custody;
- temporary storage;
- street and quay obstruction.

Larger authorities may intervene with:

- reserve purchases;
- transport priority;
- public works;
- credit;
- anti-hoarding or access rules.

But every intervention creates distributional conflict.

## Result

**PASS for plural storage ownership under public rules.**

The tests do not support one state granary system or one private merchant monopoly.

They support storage as a mixed political economy in which ownership can be private, estate, cooperative, civic, religious, or public depending on place.

---

# 9. Test 6 — major river crossing failure

## Condition

A major bridge, ferry complex, or approach road fails during a busy season.

Effects include:

- local isolation;
- cargo queues;
- price differences across the river;
- diverted travelers;
- overloaded secondary crossings;
- military and messenger delays.

## Immediate authority

The town or crossing authority needs to:

- close unsafe access;
- organize ferries or temporary alternatives;
- control queues;
- protect workers;
- manage local traffic.

## Wider authority

If the crossing is strategically important, larger authorities need to:

- redirect trunk traffic;
- fund major repair;
- requisition or contract materials;
- settle cost-sharing;
- prevent predatory tolling;
- coordinate security.

A river or route body may also matter if the failure affects navigation.

## Result

**PASS for nested infrastructure authority.**

A crossing can be locally operated while remaining regionally important.

Control of infrastructure therefore creates political leverage without automatically creating territorial sovereignty.

---

# 10. Test 7 — central export or price policy

## Condition

A larger authority responds to the abundance crisis by attempting one of the following:

- minimum grain price;
- export ban;
- export subsidy;
- compulsory sale to public stores;
- emergency reserve purchase;
- restrictions on distressed land acquisition.

## What the center can plausibly control

Depending on its capacity, it may influence:

- taxes;
- customs;
- public purchases;
- licensed markets;
- roads or crossings;
- enforcement against large merchants;
- recognition of contracts.

## What the center cannot assume

It may not fully control:

- every village store;
- every estate granary;
- every river landing;
- every creditor;
- every smuggling path;
- every local measure;
- every neighboring buyer.

A price policy can therefore produce:

- evasion;
- black markets;
- storage manipulation;
- cross-border arbitrage;
- town resistance;
- estate lobbying;
- cooperative support or opposition.

## Result

**PASS for meaningful but dependent central authority.**

A larger polity can be powerful enough to matter without being administratively omnipotent.

Its policies work best when it can recruit, bargain with, or compel the existing institutions that hold records, storage, transport, water, and local legitimacy.

---

# 11. Test 8 — cross-border Convergence dispute

## Condition

A Low Rivers polity or authority is accused of:

- discriminatory export restrictions;
- closing a protected crossing;
- interfering with recognized navigation;
- violating a river agreement;
- seizing foreign cargo;
- refusing an arbitration outcome.

## Who represents whom

The Convergence should not automatically summon "the representative of Low Rivers."

The relevant actor may be:

- a territorial ruler;
- a city;
- a league;
- a river authority;
- another recognized signatory or obligated institution.

Local farmers, merchants, water users, or towns may provide evidence or bear the costs without being treaty parties themselves.

## Result

**PASS for actor-specific representation.**

Convergence participation follows actual political authority and specific obligations.

The Low Rivers cultural region does not need one diplomatic voice merely because outsiders perceive it as one region.

---

# 12. Functional map that survives all eight tests

| Level | Functions strongly supported by the tests | Functions that should not automatically be assigned here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Household / farm | production choices, household stores, ordinary property/use within recognized rights | shared river allocation, regional trade policy |
| Village / cooperative | minor water works, mutual aid, seed exchange, local storage, local testimony and mediation | diplomacy, large military action, basin-wide allocation |
| Estate | tenancy, estate works, rents, labor, private storage and credit | final jurisdiction over all tenants, external water, sovereign diplomacy |
| Town / city | markets, quays, crossings, records, warehouses, public order, commercial disputes, emergency response | all surrounding land and water |
| River / watershed | allocation, flow information, navigation condition, shared works, upstream/downstream dispute | unrelated criminal/family law, general taxation |
| Regional / polity | taxation, military capacity, large works, emergency procurement, broad trade policy, higher enforcement, diplomacy | detailed daily operation of every ditch, warehouse, village, or estate |
| Cross-border / Convergence | treaty obligations, protected passage, interstate river/crossing issues, recognized arbitration | ordinary Low Rivers agricultural administration |

This is a functional distribution, not a constitution.

---

# 13. Strong structural conclusion

The Low Rivers currently works best as:

> **a layered agrarian-river political order in which territorial authorities coexist with powerful functional institutions tied to water, towns, estates, storage, villages, and markets.**

The tests strongly support:

1. significant local autonomy because farming, flood response, tenancy, and routine water use depend on local knowledge;
2. river / watershed institutions that can cut across ordinary political jurisdictions;
3. politically important market towns with authority over storage, crossings, records, and commercial order;
4. estates as major political-economic actors but not automatically sovereign states;
5. cooperatives, seed systems, and village institutions as practical infrastructure rather than decorative culture;
6. regional-scale capacity for taxation, force, major works, emergency intervention, and diplomacy somewhere in the system;
7. central policy whose effectiveness depends on local records, storage, transport, water institutions, and enforcement partners;
8. Convergence representation by actual political actors, not by one cultural-region delegate.

The tests **do not** establish whether the Low Rivers is one large polity, several polities, or a dominant polity surrounded by smaller jurisdictions.

# 14. Political consequences

## Power follows bottlenecks as much as land

A person can own enormous acreage and still depend on someone else for:

- water;
- warehouse space;
- river access;
- a crossing;
- credit;
- recognized weights;
- export permission.

Likewise, a town with little farmland may be politically powerful because it controls a market, crossing, or transfer point.

## Crisis can strengthen institutions that did not cause it

During abundance, flood, or drought:

- cooperatives may gain members;
- warehouses may gain leverage;
- river authorities may gain emergency powers;
- estate owners may consolidate land;
- towns may impose temporary rules;
- central rulers may try to turn emergency measures into permanent authority.

Writing the Sequel should ask which emergency powers survive after the crisis.

## Centralization and decentralization can happen at the same time

A ruler may gain power over:

- taxes;
- reserve purchases;
- diplomacy;

while villages gain stronger rights over:

- seed;
- commons;
- local water.

That is more historically plausible than assuming the whole system moves in one direction.

---

# 15. Writing the Sequel pressures

If the Seven-year Blight created stronger storage, seed-sharing, and water institutions, later generations may inherit them after their original emergency purpose changed.

Possible consequences to test later:

- famine reserves become ordinary credit institutions;
- emergency grain inspectors become permanent market officials;
- a temporary water compact becomes customary law;
- estate debt relief becomes an annual political fight;
- an old public granary is now mostly ceremonial but still legally important;
- villages defend a seed-sharing privilege whose original reason is half forgotten;
- central rulers claim emergency authority using precedents created during the Blight.

These are **development prompts**, not current canon.

---

# 16. Breath scene seeds

Keep these non-canon unless later promoted:

- two villages argue over whose flood marker is the honest one;
- a town clerk insists grain is "in storage" while farmers point to carts filling the street;
- an estate steward and cooperative weighmaster use different moisture assumptions and each calls the other fraudulent;
- a ferryman becomes briefly famous because a bridge failure makes his normally minor crossing indispensable;
- a tenant carries three generations of water-turn receipts to prove a right everyone locally already remembers;
- a ruler's new price decree is discussed first as a rumor by warehouse porters who know implementation will be the real fight.

---

# 17. Still open

Do **not** settle without further testing:

- number of Low Rivers polities;
- whether one polity is currently dominant;
- ruler / title;
- capital;
- exact borders;
- succession or electoral rules;
- exact estate privileges;
- exact tenancy systems;
- named water institutions;
- whether watershed bodies are public, customary, cooperative, treaty-based, or mixed;
- exact reserve policy;
- exact tax system;
- exact military structure;
- exact degree of village legal autonomy;
- which towns possess charter-like privileges;
- exact Convergence signatories or delegates;
- whether G1 sits inside one polity or near a political boundary.

Those questions should now be answered against this functional model rather than by choosing a government label first.
