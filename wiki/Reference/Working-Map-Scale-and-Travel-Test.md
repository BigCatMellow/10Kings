## Status

**Provisional design test — not exact map canon.**

The purpose is to test whether the V1 map still works once movement takes **days**, cargo has different speeds, and seasonal rerouting costs something.

The numbers below are design ranges.

They are not intended as clockwork precision.

---

# 1. Scale target

The known world should be:

- large enough that ordinary overland travel between distant major regions takes **weeks**, not afternoons;
- connected enough that routine interregional trade remains economically normal;
- small / concentrated enough that Port, High Roads, major rivers and The Spine can genuinely shape a shared political-economic system;
- slow enough that information can arrive late, distorted or unevenly;
- fast enough that the same crisis can plausibly affect several regions within one season.

A useful target is:

> **most adjacent major economic hubs are several ordinary travel days apart; crossing multiple major regions usually takes roughly two to four weeks by ordinary surface travel, while favorable sea / river travel can compress that sharply.**

This is a network scale, not a continent-width measurement.

---

# 2. Travel-rate assumptions

These are working design rates for ordinary movement in usable weather.

## Walking traveler on maintained road

Approximately:

- **18–25 miles / 29–40 km per day**

This assumes:
- reasonable road;
- healthy traveler;
- light load;
- no long border / ferry delay.

## Pack caravan

Approximately:

- **15–22 miles / 24–35 km per day** on good road;
- **8–15 miles / 13–24 km per day** in difficult highland / forest terrain.

The caravan is slower than one determined traveler because:
- animals need care;
- loads shift;
- groups bunch up;
- stops take longer;
- minor failures affect everyone.

## Heavy carts / bulk overland freight

Approximately:

- **10–16 miles / 16–26 km per day** on good maintained roads;
- **6–10 miles / 10–16 km per day** on steep, muddy or poor roads.

This is why bulk goods strongly prefer water where possible.

## Mounted messenger

Without a formal relay system:

- approximately **30–45 miles / 48–72 km per day** for sustained urgent travel.

With prepared remounts on a high-priority corridor:

- **60–90 miles / 96–145 km per day** can be possible for short urgent dispatch chains.

This does **not** imply a universal postal network.

Relay speed only exists where an institution has:
- horses;
- stations;
- money;
- trusted riders;
- advance organization.

## Navigable river

Highly dependent on current, wind, channel and vessel.

Working design range:

- downstream bulk movement can often cover **30–60 miles / 48–96 km per day**;
- upstream travel may fall to **15–30 miles / 24–48 km per day**, sometimes much less.

River travel can still lose time at:
- shallows;
- locks / weirs if any;
- rapids;
- unload/reload points;
- bridges;
- customs;
- seasonal low water.

## Coastal sailing

Rather than assign one universal ship speed, use:

> **in favorable conditions, coastal sea movement often covers roughly two to four times the daily distance of heavy road freight.**

But sailing time includes:
- waiting for weather;
- harbor entry;
- loading;
- convoy assembly;
- piracy risk;
- coastal stops;
- seasonal storm belts.

For commercial planning, **departure uncertainty** can matter as much as sailing speed.

## The Underpass

Typical branch travel should be slower and lower-capacity than an equivalent open road.

Working assumptions:

- people / pack animals: roughly **8–15 miles / 13–24 km per day** on known routes;
- carts only on selected improved corridors;
- some sections require unloading;
- junction choice, ventilation, water, animals, darkness and local control add delay.

The Underpass's value is:

- bypass;
- weather avoidance;
- secrecy;
- route substitution;

not universal speed.

# 3. Seasonal multipliers

Do not change every route by the same percentage.

Use route-specific delay.

## Ordinary wet / muddy season

Road freight may require roughly:

- **1.3–1.8× normal travel time**

depending on drainage and maintenance.

## High pass bad season

Mountain routes may require:

- **1.5–2.5× normal time**

or close entirely.

Closure is more interesting than simply making every journey slower.

## Northern winter

The North Shoulder / R2 corridor can become:
- unreliable;
- intermittently closed;
- restricted to local guides / pack travel.

That pushes traffic toward:
- sea routes when weather allows;
- U2;
- stored supplies;
- delayed travel.

## Coastal storm season

A ship may remain fast **once underway** but wait days for:
- safer departure;
- convoy;
- harbor clearance.

Model this as waiting time, not merely slower sailing.

## River low water / flood

Low water can reduce:
- load;
- navigable reach.

Flood can:
- increase current;
- destroy crossings;
- make navigation dangerous.

# 6. What these times imply about world scale

## Adjacent systems feel connected

A merchant can plausibly:

- leave HR;
- reach Port in under a week;
- transact;
- return within two weeks if everything goes well.

That is close enough for:
- credit;
- repeat relationships;
- regular letters;
- fashion / slang movement.

It is not a daily commute.

## A multi-region overland journey is substantial

Examples:

### NC → HR → P

Good season:

- roughly **9–15 commercial days**

before:
- rest;
- customs;
- waiting;
- transfer.

Sea can beat it.

That preserves North Coast maritime orientation.

### SH → LR → P

Roughly:

- **6–12 commercial days**

depending on route and river conditions.

This supports:
- routine food / metal trade;
- Port access;
- meaningful effect of Western Gate closure.

### OC → LR → SH

Roughly:

- **10–17 commercial days**

before delays.

This is a real cross-regional journey.

### NC → HR → LR → OC

Roughly:

- **14–24 commercial days**

before transfer / border / weather delay.

That is long enough for regional differences to remain meaningful while still allowing trade, migration and political awareness.

# 7. Courier-time implication

Dedicated messages can move much faster than freight.

A practical rule:

> **on well-supported roads, urgent institutional dispatch can often arrive in roughly one-half to two-thirds of ordinary commercial travel time.**

Examples:

- HR → P commercial: 4–6 days;
- urgent rider: perhaps **2–4 days**.

But only if:
- a rider leaves promptly;
- animals are available;
- the road is open;
- the receiving office trusts the messenger.

A rumor carried by the next caravan may arrive later despite being easier to repeat.

A merchant price report may wait two days for a ship and then outrun a mounted message over the same broad distance.

This preserves [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md)'s network-latency model.

# 8. Underpass substitution tests

## U1 — Western crossing

Surface SH ↔ LR via R1:

- **4–7 days** commercial travel.

U1 should usually be:

- **4–7 days for people / pack loads** on a known branch;
- slower for bulky cargo;
- much lower capacity.

Therefore U1 is not automatically faster.

Its advantage appears when:
- snow closes part of R1;
- toll / military control becomes extreme;
- secrecy matters.

### Result

**Pass.**

The Underpass can matter strategically without replacing Western Gate.

---

## U2 — northern branch

Surface NC ↔ HR:

- **5–9 days** in good conditions.

U2 can take roughly:

- **6–10 days** for pack movement.

During some winter closures, that slower tunnel route becomes the only reliable non-maritime option.

### Result

**Pass.**

U2 gains value from reliability / access, not speed.

---

## U3 — forest branch

U3 should be poor for bulk freight but useful for:

- guides;
- refugees;
- smugglers;
- small valuable cargo;
- people avoiding a controlled road.

### Result

**Pass.**

No exact end-to-end time is needed until entrances are fixed.

# 9. Geometry V1 causal retests

## Test A — North Coast piracy

Baseline:

- NC ↔ P sea: **5–9 days**
- NC ↔ HR ↔ P land: **9–15 days**

If piracy / convoy delays add several days and substantial risk:

- high-value goods shift inland;
- bulk goods mostly wait / convoy rather than all moving overland;
- High Roads transport prices rise;
- commercial information may redirect faster than physical cargo.

### Result

**Strong pass.**

The overland alternative is viable but inferior enough that piracy still matters.

---

## Test B — Western Gate closure

Baseline:

- SH ↔ LR: **4–7 days**.

Alternatives:

- U1: similar time for small loads, much lower capacity;
- SH → western / southern port → P → LR: roughly **8–15+ days** depending on port access and sailing;
- forest-edge local routes: slower / less reliable.

### Result

**Strong pass.**

Closure does not isolate Stone Hills.

It roughly doubles time / handling for much rerouted bulk traffic and creates believable price pressure.

---

## Test C — Old Cities drought

Baseline:

- OC ↔ P sea: **4–8 days**;
- OC ↔ LR land: **6–10 days**.

When Old Cities demand more imported food:

- coastal merchants can bid quickly through Port;
- inland traders can buy directly in Low Rivers;
- Low Rivers prices respond before the entire harvest physically moves;
- Port finance / shipping reacts in parallel.

### Result

**Strong pass.**

The map supports both sea and land responses.

---

## Test D — Longwood extraction conflict

Baseline:

- LW ↔ LR: **3–6 days**;
- LW ↔ HR: **4–7 days**;
- LW ↔ OC: **4–8 days**.

A road / logging dispute in western Longwood can therefore:

- affect Low Rivers timber prices within days;
- redirect some merchants toward HR;
- create Old Cities substitution demand within roughly a week;
- spread rumor before official investigation is complete.

### Result

**Strong pass.**

Longwood is connected without becoming a transit corridor for everything.

# 10. Capacity matters more than time alone

Two routes with similar travel times are not equivalent.

For each future route, define:

1. **speed**;
2. **capacity**;
3. **reliability**;
4. **seasonality**;
5. **cost**;
6. **security**;
7. **political friction**.

Example:

```text
U1 tunnel
similar pack-travel time to Western Gate
BUT
far lower cart capacity
+ fewer animals
+ more toll/control
+ collapse risk
→ strategic bypass, not bulk replacement
```

This is the key result of the test.

# 11. Port placement result

The V1 **estuary / island-or-peninsula harbor complex just south of the central convergence** survives the travel-time test.

It explains why:

- LR bulk cargo reaches Port cheaply;
- HR remains close enough to use Port constantly;
- NC and OC can both reach Port efficiently by sea;
- SH can use Port without being dependent on it;
- Longwood reaches Port indirectly through river / road networks;
- Port becomes an information hub because several fast networks overlap there.

Therefore the **general Port placement can be promoted as the strongest working geometry**.

Still unresolved:

- island vs. peninsula vs. mainland split;
- exact estuary channels;
- city footprint;
- final coastlines.

---

# 12. North Coast connection result

The V1 southeastern highland shoulder also survives.

It produces:

- one principal NC ↔ HR overland corridor;
- several minor / seasonal alternatives;
- maritime competition;
- winter vulnerability;
- reason for U2 to matter.

Therefore this can be treated as the strongest working solution to the old fissure-versus-pass conflict.

Exact pass names / geometry remain open.

---

# 13. High Roads ↔ Old Cities border

The scale test does **not** require a direct HR ↔ OC political / geographic frontier.

The network works with:

- HR → LR → OC;
- HR → LW → OC;
- possible narrow southern plateau route later.

Therefore:

> **leave direct HR ↔ OC adjacency unresolved.**

Do not force a border merely to shorten the map.

# 14. Information-latency examples

The world now supports useful rough timing.

## Port learns first

A maritime incident near NC may reach Port by ship in:

- several days to roughly a week;

while an inland court off the route may hear later despite being geographically closer.

## Merchant network outruns government

A price shock can move:

- OC → Port → LR merchants

before a formal diplomatic report reaches every relevant ruler.

## Verification lags rumor

A rumor may arrive HR → P in one commercial journey.

Verification may require:

- return messenger;
- sealed copy;
- second witness.

So verified knowledge can lag by several additional days.

## Remote shadow

A village only one or two days from a major route can still learn important news late if nobody has a reason to detour there.

Distance is not enough.

---

# 15. Map-scale guardrail

Do not let later map art accidentally stretch or compress the world enough to break these relationships.

A future visual map should preserve approximately:

- HR and LR both within several days of Port;
- NC sea route normally faster for bulk than NC → HR → P land;
- SH ↔ LR close enough for routine heavy interdependence;
- OC ↔ P close enough for routine maritime exchange;
- full multi-region overland travel measured in **weeks**;
- Underpass bypasses low-capacity enough not to erase surface chokepoints.

Exact mileage can change.

These relationships should not.

---

# 16. Remaining scale questions

Still unresolved:

- exact distances in miles / kilometers;
- exact coastline scale;
- exact location of each representative hub;
- river navigability endpoints;
- road-quality variation;
- prevailing winds / currents;
- ferry / strait crossing times;
- actual caravan departure cadence;
- which roads support heavy carts end-to-end;
- final Underpass entrance placement.

These should now be solved **inside the tested travel-time envelope**, not from scratch.

---

# 17. Disposition

**Geometry V1 passes the first scale / travel-time test.**

Strongest surviving conclusions:

1. Y-shaped central convergence remains viable.
2. Port's estuary / strait location remains viable and should become the strongest working geometry.
3. North Coast's southeastern shoulder remains a strong solution for constrained land access.
4. Water transport is fast enough to justify Port / river importance.
5. Surface alternatives are slow enough for chokepoints to matter.
6. Underpass routes can substitute without dominating because capacity and reliability differ.
7. Ordinary cross-world travel naturally operates on a **weeks** scale while urgent messages can move materially faster.

The next physical-network work should focus on:

- river navigability endpoints;
- cart-capable roads;
- final Port local geometry;
- exact strategic chokepoints;

not on changing the overall topology unless a new contradiction appears.
