# Prose test

The payment arrived two days after the grain did.

That was late enough to matter and early enough to save something.

The Low Rivers household did not celebrate.

The money went first to people who had already been waiting for it.

A carrier account.

A mill advance.

A lender who had extended the household through planting and expected repayment after harvest.

The woman keeping the household book crossed out three lines.

Her brother watched from the doorway.

"That's all of it?"

"Most."

"Most?"

"Enough."

He looked toward the yard.

There were still sacks under cover.

Not as many as before.

The harvest had been good.

That remained the problem.

"What happens to the rest?"

"We wait."

"For price?"

"For space."

"Same thing?"

"No."

He had learned not to argue when she used that tone.

The payment from Port did not make them prosperous.

It did something less dramatic.

It meant:

- the lender did not call the pledged animals that week;
- the household did not have to sell another field immediately;
- the carrier who had moved their grain could be paid;
- the next load still had somewhere to go if a buyer appeared.

Time.

They had bought time by successfully selling grain they already owned.

The woman closed the book.

"Your sister said Port nearly lost the whole thing."

"She says Port nearly loses everything before lunch."

"Did she get paid?"

"Yes."

"Then Port worked."

He laughed.

She did not.

"Port got paid too."

"Of course it did."

That was the end of their gratitude.

---

Three days later, at a market farther downriver, the woman heard that another grower had sold a field.

She knew the name.

Not well.

Well enough.

They had shared harvest labor once.

The seller's family had good ground and bad storage.

The buyer had cash.

The seller had a debt date.

That was most of the story.

At the market, people supplied the rest whether they knew it or not.

"They should have held."

"Where?"

"Found storage."

"With whose money?"

"Taken the lower offer."

"They did."

"Then waited."

"For what?"

Nobody had a satisfactory answer.

The woman found the seller near the scale house.

They were carrying a folded copy of the transfer.

"South strip?" she asked.

The seller nodded.

"Bad price."

"I know."

"You could have sent word."

"To who?"

The woman almost said:

> me.

She did not.

Her own book was balanced because somebody in Port had accepted one promise and not another.

She had no spare miracle to lend.

The seller tucked the paper inside their coat.

"I'll buy it back."

"Maybe."

"I will."

The woman nodded.

On the road home, wagons full of grain passed in the other direction.

There was so much food that people were selling land.

That remained difficult to explain to anyone who had not seen it.

---

In Port, the Records Worker saw the Low Rivers crisis mostly as paper.

This was one of the advantages and failures of the job.

A regional buyer asked for:

- confirmation that three recently amended warehouse transfers had settled;
- recognition of a temporary storage extension;
- copies of two guarantor endorsements.

The worker retrieved the references.

One was Wurdren's grain case.

They remembered it because the warehouse runner had forgotten the date.

The other two had different sellers.

Different cargo lots.

Different warehouses.

The same correspondent house appeared on all three guarantee adjustments.

The worker had seen the mark before.

Large houses appeared on many things.

They copied it.

A junior clerk set another request beside them.

"Low Rivers again."

"What?"

"Storage."

The worker read it.

This one had not been approved.

The applicant wanted an extension against grain already sitting in recognized storage.

The guarantor had offered:

- shorter term;
- higher uncovered share.

The applicant had declined.

The worker put the request beside the completed cases.

Three helped.

One not.

The junior clerk watched.

"You think they're choosing?"

"Everyone chooses."

"That's not what I meant."

The worker looked at them.

"Then say what you meant."

The junior clerk hesitated.

"Choosing which grain matters."

The Records Worker looked back at the papers.

"No."

The clerk relaxed too quickly.

The worker continued.

"They are choosing which promises they will stand behind."

"Is that different?"

"Legally? Yes."

"Actually?"

The Records Worker took another sheet.

"Ask somebody with more free time."

The junior clerk left.

At the bottom of one guarantee notice, the same correspondent mark appeared again.

Familiar.

Nothing more than that.

The worker copied the reference number correctly.

Then went to lunch late.

---

The Port pin order reached Stone Hills four days after it left the city.

It arrived during an argument already in progress.

The workshop office had three ordinary repair slips pinned under a board marked:

> delayed.

A worker representative stood beside them.

The production supervisor stood behind the desk.

Neither was shouting.

That meant the argument had lasted a while.

The clerk opened the new Port request.

"River-yard lift."

The supervisor held out a hand.

The clerk passed it over.

"Known pattern."

"Yes."

"Inspection required."

"Yes."

"Quantity?"

The supervisor read.

"Small."

The worker representative looked at the delayed board.

"Of course."

The supervisor ignored that.

"Fit it between."

The clerk did not move.

"Priority schedule," they said.

"Which contract?"

The clerk named the number.

Everyone in the office knew it.

Nobody outside the office was supposed to know much about it.

The worker representative laughed once.

"There it is again."

The supervisor closed their eyes.

"Not now."

"When?"

"We are discussing one Port order."

"No. You are discussing the fourth ordinary order this week."

"Three."

The representative pointed at the board.

"Four when you move this one."

The supervisor looked at the slips.

One belonged to:

- a mill repair;
- one to a bridge contractor;
- one to a mine-haulage shop waiting on replacement hardware.

All ordinary.

All paying.

None protected.

"Port cannot wait three weeks," the supervisor said.

"Neither can the bridge."

"The bridge is not closed."

"Yet."

The supervisor pointed at the priority schedule.

"You know what is ahead of them."

"I know what you tell us is ahead of them."

"We signed obligations."

"So did they."

"Not the same obligations."

"No. Those can be moved."

The room went quiet.

The workshop floor could be heard through the wall:

- hammer;
- wheel;
- shouted measure;
- metal striking metal.

They were not idle.

That was the problem.

The supervisor looked at the Port request again.

"First open inspection slot."

The clerk said:

"There isn't one."

"Make one."

"Which order moves?"

The supervisor looked at the delayed board.

The worker representative did too.

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Finally the supervisor tapped the mine-haulage slip.

"That one."

The clerk took it down.

"New date?"

The supervisor gave one.

The clerk wrote it.

Then pinned the Port request in its place.

The worker representative watched the exchange.

"That's four."

The supervisor sat down.

"Yes."

# V2 assessment

## Low Rivers

**STRONG PASS.**

The helped household gets:

> time.

The losing producer gets:

> a legal but bad transaction under pressure.

They are connected socially enough to make the contrast human, not mirrored enough to feel staged.

## Records Worker

**PASS.**

The correspondent mark is now:

- familiar;
- correctly copied;
- not investigated.

## Stone Hills

**STRONG PASS.**

The Port request is visibly:

> one more order entering an already contentious priority system.

It does not create the conflict.

## Ending discipline

**PASS.**

The Stone Hills scene ends on:

- one ordinary order moving down;
- one Port order moving up.

No narrator explains allocation.

## Story consequence

Sequence 11 now establishes:

1. institutional help creates unequal afterlives;
2. documentary traces accumulate;
3. ordinary Port needs now visibly collide with Stone Hills priority production.
