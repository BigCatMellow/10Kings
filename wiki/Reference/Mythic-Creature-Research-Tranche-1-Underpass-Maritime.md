# 1. Underpass / mining research pool

## 1A. Welsh coblynau / mine-monster tradition

### Source culture / geography

Welsh mining folklore.

### Sources

- Museum Wales / Big Pit National Coal Museum:
  - https://museum.wales/bigpit/whatson/13001/Mine-Monsters-/
  - https://museum.wales/bigpit/whatson/12763/The-Coblynnau-of-Big-Pit/
  - https://museum.wales/bigpit/whatson/12390/-Mystical-Mines--Welsh-Mining-Folklore/

### Source-derived features

Museum Wales explicitly preserves stories of:

- **Coblynau** associated with mines;
- mine "monsters";
- fairy / supernatural traditions connected to mining landscapes.

The modern museum presentation confirms that mine-creature traditions remain part of Welsh mining folklore.

The available source is stronger for **tradition continuity** than for detailed historical morphology / behavior.

### Likely functions for comparative use

Research hypothesis, not directly asserted by the museum pages:

- occupational identity;
- supernatural interpretation of dangerous underground space;
- explanation / personification of noises or accidents;
- transmission of worker lore.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

- miners share creature stories outsiders know only superficially;
- one mine community's version can differ sharply from another;
- the creature tradition belongs to **workers**, not automatically to the whole Stone Hills / Underpass population;
- warning behavior can be encoded in creature stories without confirming a supernatural being.

### Do not borrow

- the name Coblyn / Coblynau;
- Welsh fairy cosmology;
- stereotyped "goblin miner" appearance;
- Welsh cultural identity.

### Potential slot

**Underpass / Stone Hills mine communities.**

# 2. Cornish Knockers → transplanted Tommyknockers

## Source culture / geography

Cornish mining traditions, later carried by migrant miners into mining communities in the United States.

### Sources

- Museum of Cornish Life:
  - https://museumofcornishlife.co.uk/2026/07/02/myths-and-stereotypes-in-performances-set-in-cornwall/
- National Coal Mining Museum:
  - https://www.ncm.org.uk/news/tommyknockers/
- Park City Museum:
  - https://parkcityhistory.org/death-knocks-at-the-door/
- Herbert Hoover Presidential Library / National Archives blog:
  - https://hoover.blogs.archives.gov/2016/09/07/herbert-hoover-and-the-tommyknockers-mythic-gnomes-of-the-mine-shafts/

### Source-derived features

The sources preserve several important layers:

- Cornish **knockers** were associated with mines;
- stories could interpret sounds as warnings of impending collapse;
- some traditions treated the beings as helpful / protective, others mischievous or dangerous;
- migrant miners transported related folklore to American mining settlements;
- in some later American traditions they became "Tommyknockers";
- the creature tradition could become part of local mining-town identity.

The Hoover Library account specifically records the interpretation that knocking before collapse could be attributed to spirits of dead miners warning living workers.

### Important research mechanism

This is a very strong example of:

~~~text
real occupational sound / hazard
+
worker interpretation
+
migrant cultural transmission
+
new local context
=
a creature tradition that changes while preserving its function
~~~

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

- Underpass / mine legends can **travel with miners**, rather than originating independently everywhere;
- different mines may use related names / motifs because workers migrated between them;
- a warning creature can be both:
  - superstition;
  - embodied safety knowledge;
- later communities can turn a frightening worker tradition into:
  - mascot;
  - joke;
  - local identity marker

without erasing older fear.

This is especially useful for Stone Hills ↔ Underpass labor migration.

### Do not borrow

- Cornish identity;
- Tommyknocker name;
- tiny bearded miner appearance;
- direct Christian / European spirit categories.

# 3. German Kobold and cobalt ore

## Source culture / geography

German / Saxon mining and metallurgical history.

### Sources

Royal Society of Chemistry:

- https://edu.rsc.org/elements/cobalt/2020006.article
- https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2019/dt/c9dt00765b
- https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/27/

### Source-derived features

The Royal Society of Chemistry records that:

- **cobalt** derives from German *Kobold*;
- miners used the term for troublesome / "bewitched" ore;
- ores mistaken for useful metal could fail to yield the expected material;
- cobalt ores could occur with arsenic;
- processing could release toxic arsenic compounds / vapors.

This is unusually valuable because the folklore term sits directly on top of a **real material hazard and failed technical expectation**.

### Mechanism

~~~text
ore looks promising
→ processing fails / sickens workers
→ harmful material is personified as malicious underground agency
→ folklore name survives into technical vocabulary
~~~

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A legendary subterranean creature does not need to originate from sightings.

It may originate from:

- toxic mineral;
- bad air;
- misleading ore;
- strange flame color;
- metallic contamination;
- unexplained sickness.

This suggests a powerful 10Kings path:

> **one Underpass "creature" may partly be the cultural personification of a geological / metallurgical hazard.**

The myth could then later acquire:

- tracks;
- body shape;
- moral behavior;
- old sightings.

### Do not borrow

- Kobold name;
- later fantasy-goblin imagery;
- exact cobalt chemistry unless the world's geology independently supports it.

# 4. Czech permoník / mine-spirit tradition

## Source culture / geography

Czech mining folklore, particularly mining regions influenced by German-speaking mining traditions.

### Sources

- Czech cultural reference:
  - https://www.abczech.cz/Permonik-P7030777.html
- Příbram mining heritage:
  - https://www.poznejpribram.cz/en/hornicke-muzeum-pribram

Supplementary traditional-folklore summaries:

- https://www.ceskezvyky.cz/permonici-hornicti-duchove-a-povery/
- https://www.ceskezvyky.cz/povesti-a-povidacky-o-permonikovi/

### Source-derived features

The Czech reference describes the **permoník** as:

- a small mine spirit;
- associated with tunnels / miners;
- part of mining oral tradition;
- likely influenced linguistically / culturally by German mining terminology such as *Bergmännchen* / *Berggeist*.

The mining-museum material confirms the continuing importance of mining folklore within Czech mining heritage.

Traditional story collections portray mine spirits as:

- helpers;
- disciplinarians;
- dangerous when disrespected;
- beings whose behavior comments on good / bad workers.

### Useful mechanism

This adds another dimension beyond simple hazard warning:

> **the mine being can embody occupational ethics.**

A worker may interpret:

- carelessness;
- dishonesty;
- greed;
- disrespect for established practice

as inviting the being's anger.

That can preserve safety culture through morality.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A Stone Hills / Underpass legendary creature can accumulate:

- practical hazard warning;
- labor ethics;
- worker identity;
- migration history.

Different classes may interpret it differently:

- miners: warning / old companion;
- owners: superstition;
- reformers: encoded labor memory;
- priests: spirit / metaphor;
- naturalists: geological explanation.

### Do not borrow

- permoník name;
- dwarf / goblin iconography;
- Christian saint integration;
- Czech ethnic markers.

# 5. Underpass research synthesis — what repeats

Across the mining comparators, several functions recur.

## Strong repeating mechanisms

### Sound before danger

Underground knocks / shifts / echoes can become agency.

### Worker-specific knowledge

Outsiders hear superstition.

Workers hear:

- warning;
- history;
- occupational identity.

### Moralized safety

The underground being can punish:

- arrogance;
- greed;
- ignoring warning;
- poor work.

### Material hazard personification

Toxic / deceptive ore can become a malicious being.

### Migration

Workers carry creature traditions between mines and adapt them.

### Ambivalent temperament

Helpful versus harmful is often conditional rather than absolute.

That is useful.

A good 10Kings subterranean legend should probably **not** be a straightforward predator.

# 7. Maritime research pool

## 7A. Hafgufa / aspidochelone and whale trap-feeding

### Source tradition

Old Norse *hafgufa* traditions connected by modern researchers to the older bestiary **aspidochelone** tradition.

### Sources

Flinders University / peer-reviewed research:

- https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2023/03/03/mysterious-new-behaviour-seen-in-whales-may-be-recorded-in-ancient-manuscripts/
- https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/parallels-for-cetacean-trap-feeding-and-tread-water-feeding-in-th/

Smithsonian overview:

- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/medieval-manuscripts-reveal-hidden-history-whales-180982348/

### Source-derived features

Researchers identified strong parallels between historical descriptions and whale **trap feeding**:

- whale remains near the surface;
- mouth held open;
- fish collect / enter;
- whale closes mouth.

The historical tradition later accumulates more fantastical interpretation.

Researchers argue this may preserve **accurate environmental observation in mythic / bestiary form**.

### Why this is exceptionally useful

This is almost a perfect model for 10Kings.

~~~text
rare real animal behavior
→ accurate oral / written observation
→ unfamiliarity / rarity
→ copied retellings
→ creature becomes increasingly monstrous
~~~

The legend may be wrong about:

- what the animal is;
- why it behaves that way;

while remaining surprisingly right about:

- what people actually saw.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

North Coast legends can preserve:

- rare feeding behaviors;
- lost migration routes;
- behaviors more common before hunting / ecological change;
- observations dismissed by later scholars because the legend became too fantastical.

A "monster" may therefore preserve **better old ecological knowledge than modern naturalists realize**.

### Do not borrow

- hafgufa name;
- aspidochelone theological symbolism;
- exact whale behavior as the final creature concept unless transformed strongly.

# 8. The Sea Monk — strange specimen becomes cultural image

## Source tradition

16th-century northern European natural history.

### Source

Smithsonian Institution:

- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/reports-sea-monster-looked-like-monk-wearing-fish-scales-horrified-renaissance-europe-180960885/

### Source-derived features

The "sea monk":

- was reported / illustrated by important 16th-century naturalists;
- entered scholarly natural-history works;
- was not directly observed by all the writers depicting it;
- accumulated through copied accounts and images;
- has been variously proposed to represent:
  - giant squid;
  - anglerfish;
  - seal;
  - walrus;
  - angel shark;
  - altered ray / shark specimens;
- remains not definitively identifiable.

The story also became entangled with contemporary religious interpretation.

### Core mechanism

This is not primarily:

> ignorant sailor sees monster.

It is:

> **ambiguous evidence becomes increasingly authoritative because respectable people copy and classify it.**

That is extremely useful.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

A legendary creature can emerge from:

- one carcass;
- one partial specimen;
- one secondhand drawing.

Then:

- guild records copy it;
- monastery / religious art changes it;
- scholars repeat it;
- merchants sell alleged remains;
- later people assume the old authorities had direct evidence.

This supports a creature whose legend is partly generated by **the archive itself**.

### Do not borrow

- monk morphology;
- Christian sectarian interpretation;
- exact species theories.

# 9. Sea-serpent / carcass / misclassification traditions

## Source

Smithsonian Biodiversity Heritage Library overview:

- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/five-real-sea-monsters-brought-life-early-naturalists-180953155/

### Source-derived features

Historical sea-monster traditions could be fed by:

- sailor reports;
- unfamiliar animals;
- decomposing carcasses;
- repeated copying by naturalists;
- mistaken classification of ordinary / deformed animals.

The Smithsonian account gives examples where:

- basking-shark remains could appear monstrous after decomposition;
- a deformed common snake was briefly classified as the young of a sea serpent;
- naturalists could sincerely investigate reports and still reach the wrong conclusion.

### Core mechanism

~~~text
partial physical evidence
+
credible witness
+
weak comparative zoology
+
institutional authority
=
monster gains scientific legitimacy
~~~

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

This gives us a way to create:

- museum / guild specimens;
- competing scholar schools;
- real bones incorrectly attached to the wrong creature;
- frauds mixed with genuine evidence.

Especially strong for Port:

> a cosmopolitan archive may contain more "proof" of monsters than remote fishing communities—and understand it less well.

### Do not borrow

- specific historical sea-serpent descriptions;
- exact early-modern European scholarly structure.

# 10. Orkney / North Atlantic creature traditions as a community system

## Sources

Historic Environment Scotland / Orkney sources:

- https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2024/09/orkney-folktales-the-outrun/

- https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/scottish-storytelling-traditions

### Source-derived features

The sources demonstrate that island / coastal communities can maintain a **dense ecology of different supernatural traditions**, including:

- selkies;
- sea monsters;
- sea serpents;
- Finfolk;
- mermaids;
- stories attached to dangerous tidal places.

Historic Environment Scotland notes that versions of selkie-wife narratives could vary island by island.

Orkney sources also connect stories strongly with:

- specific sounds / islands / shores;
- fishing / farming communities;
- oral storytelling;
- dangerous tidal waters.

### Important mechanism

Do not build "the North Coast sea monster."

Build:

> **many overlapping local maritime traditions whose stories change island by island and harbor by harbor.**

This confirms the community-level model already chosen for 10Kings culture.

### Useful 10Kings mechanisms

- same creature family can differ across islands;
- dangerous tide / reef / sound can generate a local creature tradition;
- fishermen and inland people can tell different versions;
- marriage / kinship stories can become attached to seals / sea animals;
- creatures can encode relationships between shore and sea rather than simply predation.

### Do not borrow

- selkie / Finfolk identities wholesale;
- living Scottish / Orcadian cultural markers;
- exact tales.

# 11. Maritime research synthesis — what repeats

## Real observation survives inside exaggeration

Hafgufa is the clearest example.

## Ambiguous specimens become authoritative

Sea Monk / sea-serpent material demonstrates how records create monsters.

## Ecological change changes credibility

If:

- animal populations decline;
- behavior changes;
- old habitats disappear;

later people may see an accurate old description as impossible.

This is valuable for 10Kings historical ecology.

## Story traditions are hyperlocal

Island-by-island variation should be normal.

## The ocean creates evidence problems

- partial sightings;
- distance;
- weather;
- carcass decomposition;
- few recoverable remains.

That preserves uncertainty naturally.

# 13. Cross-slot lesson

The first tranche already suggests a strong general rule:

> **legendary creatures often emerge where reliable knowledge is difficult but practical stakes are high.**

Mining:

- darkness;
- acoustics;
- invisible gas / geology;
- dangerous work.

Sea:

- distance;
- depth;
- storms;
- decomposing evidence;
- rare animal behavior.

That means 10Kings creature design should prioritize places where people have:

- repeated experience;
- incomplete visibility;
- high consequence.

Those are the best environments for truth to become folklore.

# 15. First-tranche conclusion

Two highly promising 10Kings creature families are already emerging at the **mechanism level**.

## Underpass family

Not:

> underground goblin.

But:

> **a worker legend built from warning sounds, deceptive geology, toxic mineral hazards, dead-worker memory, and traditions carried by migrant miners.**

Whether any actual creature ever existed can remain open.

## Maritime family

Not:

> giant sea serpent.

But:

> **a creature tradition assembled from rare real animal behavior, wreck / tide geography, fragmentary sightings, ambiguous carcasses, and authoritative copying of bad reconstructions.**

That tradition may contain more genuine ecological memory than later scholars expect.

Do not synthesize final creature names / bodies yet.

Next research tranche should test whether equally strong mechanisms exist for:

1. **Longwood / forest-boundary traditions**;
2. **Low Rivers / marsh-water traditions**;
3. **High Roads / mountain-pass traditions**.

Only after that should the first actual 10Kings legendary creatures be designed.
