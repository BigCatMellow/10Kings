# 1. Old Cities research pool

## 1A. Pazuzu — dangerous force used protectively

### Source tradition / geography

Ancient Mesopotamia / Assyria.

### Sources

Metropolitan Museum of Art:

- https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2014/assyria-to-iberia/blog/posts/pazuzu
- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327485

British Museum:

- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1914-0407-145

### Source-derived features

The Met identifies Pazuzu as:

- king of wind demons;
- associated with dangerous winds believed to bring illness;
- visually frightening;
- not simply a benevolent figure.

Yet images / amulets of Pazuzu were used **protectively**, especially against Lamashtu.

The Met explicitly cautions against a simple modern:

> good being / evil being

classification.

Supernatural beings could be:

- dangerous;
- manipulated;
- appeased;
- directed against other dangers.

### Core mechanism

~~~text
dangerous environmental / supernatural force
→ embodied as frightening being
→ same being becomes protection against a worse danger
~~~

### 10Kings application

Old Cities traditions should not require:

> frightening creature = evil creature.

A legendary dry-wind / dust / sickness creature could be:

- feared outside the walls;
- carved over doors;
- invoked against another danger;
- interpreted differently city by city.

This is particularly useful for cultural variation.

Possible pattern:

- upstream city: creature is a **wind warning**;
- downstream city: same being protects canals / households from sickness;
- coastal city: sailors reinterpret it as a storm sign;
- rural households: treat images as old protective custom;
- reform theologians: call all of these misunderstandings of one older sacred story.

### Do not borrow

- Pazuzu name;
- exact iconography;
- Mesopotamian demonology;
- Lamashtu relationship wholesale;
- living occult / popular reinterpretations.

# 2. Lamassu / apkallu — threshold beings and civic scale

### Source tradition / geography

Ancient Assyria / Mesopotamia.

### Sources

Metropolitan Museum of Art:

- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/322608
- https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/mesopotamian-magic-in-the-first-millennium-bc
- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/324338
- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/322487

### Source-derived features

The sources document several relevant practices:

- monumental human-headed winged bull / lion guardians placed at important gateways;
- protective supernatural figures throughout palace spaces;
- smaller protective figures buried beneath floors / doorways;
- figures interpreted as connected with apkallu, wise beings / sages of distant antiquity;
- protective imagery operating at different scales:
  - monumental;
  - architectural;
  - hidden household / room protection.

The exact meaning of some figures remains uncertain even to modern scholarship.

### Core mechanism

> **the same broad supernatural category exists simultaneously as public monument, household protection, old myth, and state symbolism.**

### 10Kings application

An Old Cities legendary creature can survive without anyone reporting a living sighting for centuries because it remains:

- on gates;
- canal houses;
- legal seals;
- household thresholds;
- old protective carvings.

Different cities can change:

- posture;
- animal components;
- number;
- gender;
- story;
- political meaning.

One city may say:

> the creatures guard the city.

Another:

> they guard the agreement.

Another:

> they are merely old architecture.

That creates shared mythology without shared theology.

### Do not borrow

- lamassu / apkallu names;
- human-headed winged bull design;
- Assyrian royal ideology;
- exact ritual practice.

# 3. Kudurru / charter imagery — monsters protecting law and water rights

### Source tradition / geography

Babylonian / Mesopotamian legal monuments.

### Sources

British Museum:

- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1882-0522-1800
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1908-0411-96
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1882-0522-1799
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1863-0826-2

Metropolitan Museum:

- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327048

### Source-derived features

Kudurru / naru monuments record:

- land grants;
- ownership disputes;
- charters;
- boundaries often described using **rivers / canals**;
- curses against violating or destroying the record.

They also carry dense divine / creature symbolism, including:

- horned dragons;
- serpents;
- scorpion-men;
- goat-fish;
- dogs;
- composite beings;
- divine emblems.

The Met notes that these symbols could protect / guarantee the gift.

The British Museum examples show the creatures literally coexisting on the same object with:

- legal history;
- land description;
- canal boundaries;
- disputes;
- curses.

### Why this is exceptionally useful

This gives Old Cities a way for a legendary creature to live inside **law**.

Not merely:

> monster beside canal.

But:

> **creature whose image means a boundary / water-right / grant is protected and should not be violated.**

### 10Kings application

A creature family may be shared across Old Cities but interpreted differently:

- OC-U: guardian of legitimate water allocation;
- OC-D: creature that remembers stolen water / broken charters;
- OC-C: transformed into merchant / port seal imagery;
- rural canal users: dangerous being believed to walk erased boundary lines;
- lawyers: archaic symbol with no literal zoological meaning;
- children: creature comes for people who move boundary stones.

This is exactly the sort of layered truth desired for the setting.

Possible historical core:

- old legal emblem;
- real dangerous canal animal;
- remembered violence over water rights;
- later creature story merges all three.

### Do not borrow

- exact kudurru layout;
- Mesopotamian gods;
- mushhushshu / scorpion-man morphology directly.

# 4. Egyptian cippi — dangerous fauna turned into protective ritual imagery

### Source tradition / geography

Ancient Egypt.

### Source

Metropolitan Museum of Art:

- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/546133
- https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545766

### Source-derived features

Cippi depict Horus dominating:

- crocodiles;
- snakes;
- scorpions;
- lions;
- other dangerous animals.

Some cippi were used in healing practices for:

- snake bite;
- scorpion sting.

Water poured over the stela could be used in curative ritual.

### Core mechanism

~~~text
real local fauna hazard
→ sacred / mythic mastery image
→ protective / healing practice
~~~

### 10Kings application

Old Cities creature stories can accumulate around **very real dryland / canal hazards**.

A composite legendary beast may encode:

- snake;
- scorpion;
- crocodilian / large river predator;
- disease / poison.

Different communities can treat the same beast as:

- the danger;
- the guardian against the danger;
- an emblem that means the danger has been ritually contained.

### Do not borrow

- Horus;
- Egyptian healing ritual;
- exact sacred iconography;
- composite design.

# 5. Old Cities synthesis

Strong mechanisms:

1. **dangerous being can also protect**;
2. **creature images can protect thresholds**;
3. **mythic animals can become legal / charter symbols**;
4. **real environmental dangers can be compressed into one legendary being**;
5. **the same creature can mean different things in neighboring cities**.

This suggests Old Cities creature traditions should be especially rich in:

- architecture;
- seals;
- law;
- water;
- protective images;
- disputed interpretation.

Unlike High Roads / North Coast legends, they may survive as **objects and institutions** even when sightings disappear.

# 6. Port / estuary research pool

## 6A. Wild Man of Orford — captured creature, official custody, unresolved identity

### Source tradition / geography

Medieval Orford, Suffolk, England.

### Source

English Heritage:

- https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/orford-castle/history/
- https://production.english-heritage.org.uk/siteassets/home/visit/places-to-visit/orford-castle/schools/accordion/orford-castle-teachers-kit-2023.pdf

### Source-derived features

A 13th-century chronicler, Ralph of Coggeshall, recorded a story that fishermen caught a strange hairy man in their nets.

The account says he:

- was brought to the castle;
- was held in custody;
- did not speak;
- was tortured;
- was allowed into the sea;
- eventually escaped.

The chronicler explicitly leaves the identity unresolved:

- human being;
- or some kind of fish / marine being.

The story remained attached to the place and is represented in local visual tradition.

### Why this is strong

This is not a vague sighting.

It contains:

- fishermen;
- physical capture;
- institutional custody;
- abuse;
- escape;
- written record;
- explicit contemporary uncertainty.

That is almost ideal for Port.

### 10Kings application

Port could possess an old famous case:

> **something was caught alive in the estuary and entered into official records.**

Centuries later:

- harbor families insist it was an unusual person / diver / survivor;
- naturalists think marine animal;
- religious storytellers interpret it differently;
- Port officials are embarrassed by how it was treated;
- outsiders call it proof of monsters.

The story becomes as much about:

> **how Port treats the unknown**

as about what was caught.

### Do not borrow

- merman appearance;
- Orford story directly;
- torture details as spectacle;
- English medieval context.

# 7. Ghost ships — real derelicts generate supernatural traditions

### Source

Royal Museums Greenwich:

- https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/maritime-history/library-archive/ghost-ships

### Source-derived features

The museum distinguishes:

- real derelict ships found without living crews;
- fictional / folkloric phantom ships.

Its collected accounts show how a particular ship can accumulate a curse reputation through:

- deaths;
- mutiny;
- repeated accidents;
- deserted crews;
- later alleged ghost sightings.

### Core mechanism

> **an object can become the creature-like carrier of a legend.**

This matters even though a ship is not biologically a creature.

The same mechanism can apply to a legendary beast:

- one identifiable specimen / vessel / place;
- repeated unrelated tragedies;
- story converts correlation into agency.

### 10Kings application

Port can be the place where:

- wreck records;
- survivor accounts;
- insurance claims;
- sailor folklore

collide.

A legendary estuary creature may become blamed for several accidents that actually have:

- different causes;
- same location;
- same tide;
- same shipping practice.

Or a **specific animal** seen near wrecks becomes transformed into a harbor omen.

### Do not borrow

- Octavius / Squando stories;
- Flying Dutchman style imagery as default.

# 8. Port as a legend-transformer

### Source support

English Heritage's general folklore essay notes that:

- stories spread through migration;
- the same broad tale can appear far apart;
- local versions adapt.

Royal Museums Greenwich material demonstrates how maritime stories become:

- literature;
- ship names;
- figureheads;
- museum objects;
- public memory.

### 10Kings application

Port should not merely have its own creatures.

Port should **transform other people's creatures**.

Possible mechanisms:

- North Coast sailor story becomes Port broadsheet;
- High Roads road-creature becomes children's theater;
- Old Cities protective beast becomes guild seal;
- Longwood boundary spirit appears in caricature;
- alleged specimen enters a Port cabinet / archive;
- scholars standardize several local creatures under one name locals dislike.

This is a major worldbuilding opportunity.

Port becomes:

> **the place where folklore gets translated, commercialized, classified, misunderstood, and sent back out.**

# 9. Mobile-route research pool

## 9A. Black Dog traditions — same creature family, radically different local meanings

### Sources

English Heritage:

- https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/inspire-me/the-top-dogs-at-english-heritage-sites/

Folklore Society / scholarly resources:

- https://www.folklore-society.com/resources/cryptozoology-and-fabulous-beasts-virtual-special-issue-7-2016/
- https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.28460415

Supplementary academic source:

- Mark Norman conference abstract:
  https://folklore-society.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/ABSTRACTS_updated_6_May.pdf

### Source-derived features

English Heritage identifies the **Barghest** as a mythical black dog associated with North Yorkshire.

The Folklore Society's bibliography shows that black-dog traditions have generated substantial scholarly study.

Mark Norman's work describes:

- many regional Black Dog traditions;
- "travelling" apparitions associated with routes;
- continuing / changing traditions.

The JSTOR description of *The Folklore of Devon* treats Black Dog lore as a broad tradition with many variations.

### Most useful mechanism

The "creature" is not one stable zoological profile.

Instead it is a **motif-family**.

Depending on location, a black dog can be:

- death omen;
- frightening apparition;
- road / place guardian;
- helper;
- recurring route phenomenon.

### 10Kings application

This directly supports the user's shared-creature idea.

A single road-hound legend family could move with:

- caravan households;
- drovers;
- migrant workers;
- pilgrims.

Then local versions diverge.

Example:

- High Roads: silent dog appears before dangerous weather / route closure;
- mobile repair families: dog follows safe camps and is protective;
- North Coast: dog is associated with drowned travelers returning home;
- Port: reduced to a children's "follow the black dog home" story;
- Radiant Path branch: moralized as guide for the lost;
- another faith: considers that interpretation nonsense.

Shared anchors might remain:

- dark coat;
- appears alone;
- does not behave like an ordinary dog;
- appears near movement / transition.

Everything else can vary.

### Do not borrow

- Black Shuck / Barghest names;
- glowing-eye cliché by default;
- English church / devil associations;
- one canonical moral meaning.

# 10. Wild Hunt traditions — moving motif changes under cultural contact

### Sources

Lithuanian Institute of Literature and Folklore journal:

- https://journals.vu.lt/td/en/article/view/29179

Folklore / comparative research:

- https://doi.org/10.55086/sp2623357

English Heritage also preserves examples of the dead / supernatural riding along a road in local belief:

- https://production.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/journey-into-witchcraft-beliefs/

### Source-derived features

The Lithuanian study records local Wild Hunt material in a region shaped by:

- political separation;
- German cultural influence;
- local adaptation.

The 2026 comparative article emphasizes that "Wild Hunt" is an umbrella label covering a set of related but not identical traditions.

English Heritage records a local belief in dead riders traveling along a road toward a market cross.

### Core mechanism

> **a moving supernatural procession is especially suited to cultural transmission because every route community can change who is traveling and why.**

### 10Kings application

A mobile-circuit legend does not need one monster.

It can involve:

- animal;
- rider;
- sound;
- procession.

Different communities may agree only on:

- heard at night;
- follows a known route;
- travel should stop / change when encountered.

One group says:

- dead caravan.

Another:

- old kings.

Another:

- storm spirits.

Another:

- smugglers invented the story.

This can become one of the best examples of:

> **same phenomenon, culturally different truth.**

### Do not borrow

- Wild Hunt name;
- Odin / Germanic mythology;
- exact dead-rider imagery.

# 11. Folktale oicotypes — scholarly model for local variants

### Source

Craig Mishler, *The Blind Man and the Loon: The Story of a Tale*:

- https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1ddr7fr

### Source-derived insight

Mishler traces a living tale across:

- Greenland;
- Alaska;
- Arctic Canada;
- Northwest Coast;
- Great Basin / Plains.

As the tale crossed:

- cultures;
- languages;
- ecosystems;

storytellers added:

- local ecological details;
- material culture;
- kinship structures;
- spiritual meanings.

The source discusses the folkloristic concept of an **oicotype**:

> a local form of a widely distributed tale.

### Why this matters to 10Kings

This gives a scholarly model for legendary creatures shared across cultures.

Use:

~~~text
shared legendary core
+
local ecology
+
local religion
+
local work
+
local history
=
local creature oicotype
~~~

Therefore a creature family should not have:

> six cultures telling the same paragraph with different names.

Each variant should reveal:

- what that community fears;
- what it values;
- what it notices;
- how it lives.

# 13. Cross-cultural variation rule — research confirmation

The user's proposal:

> **cultures can share the same creature while giving it culturally specific interpretations**

is strongly supported by the research method.

The most useful real-world patterns are:

- Black Dog regional variation;
- Wild Hunt umbrella traditions;
- the Blind Man and Loon oicotype research;
- island-by-island maritime variation from Tranche 1 / 2;
- shared Near Eastern protective creature imagery used differently by place / scale.

Therefore future 10Kings creature design should distinguish:

## Legendary core

The minimum set of features linking variants.

## Cultural oicotype

What each community changes.

## Transmission history

How the story reached that community.

## Scholarly classification

Whether outsiders incorrectly collapse multiple local traditions into one creature.
