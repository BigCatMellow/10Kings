# War Escalation Causal Chain — V1

## Status

**Superseded as active planning model; retained as causal-proof provenance.**

The active model is [Adaptive War Escalation Graph — V2](Adaptive-War-Escalation-Graph-V2.md).

This page exists to answer one question:

> **Can the Villain's numerous small, apparently independent dominoes plausibly produce an actual war without a leap in logic?**

Current answer:

**Yes, if the war emerges through commercial protection, reciprocal restrictions, security precautions, competitive-sphere guarantees, mobilization, and finally a limited armed incident that occurs after the political system has made backing down difficult.**

The reader and Wurdren should **not** receive this chain as exposition.

# 1. War-causality rule

No link may use:

> tension rises → war.

Every transition must specify:

1. **actor** — who acts;
2. **known problem** — what they believe they are responding to;
3. **rational action** — why the action makes sense locally;
4. **capability change** — what material / legal / military condition changes;
5. **outside interpretation** — what another actor thinks the action means;
6. **next incentive** — why that interpretation causes another rational action.

A domino fails if the next actor must behave irrationally merely because the plot needs escalation.

# 2. Historical memory that makes escalation believable

The modern actors do not encounter current events without history.

They inherit memories of:

- mine / ore conflicts;
- famine raids and food insecurity;
- route and toll wars;
- maritime raids and fishing-right disputes;
- forest-edge extraction conflicts;
- Old Cities water / merchant rivalries;
- previous struggles over control of Port.

The exact old campaigns remain messy and contested.

That is enough.

Current precautions therefore have historical echoes.

Examples:

~~~text
strategic-metal export controls
→ Stone Hills officials see supply management
→ neighbors remember earlier ore coercion

stronger convoy rules
→ North Coast officials see anti-piracy policy
→ rivals remember maritime exclusion / raiding periods

road guards / toll inspection
→ High Roads authority sees route security
→ merchants / rivals remember toll wars

Longwood access closure
→ local communities see rights defense
→ outsiders remember extraction conflict / violent closure

food stockpiling
→ polity sees resilience
→ neighbors with famine memory see preparation for exclusion

Port military-access debate
→ Port sees neutrality law
→ outsiders remember why control of the harbor once threatened everyone
~~~

History does not force war.

It changes what present actions **mean** to people.

# 3. Existing alignment field

The current competitive-sphere work supports two loose political / commercial networks.

## Charter / Convergence sphere

Tends to favor:

- inherited treaty prestige;
- recognized procedures;
- older route / arbitration institutions;
- established political actors;
- preservation of the existing settlement.

## Ascendant / revisionist sphere

Tends to favor:

- newer wealth;
- secondary cities;
- ambitious merchant / guild interests;
- actors who believe post-Convergence prestige no longer matches present power.

## Port

Port remains outside both.

That matters because both networks:

- need Port;
- recruit there;
- trade there;
- fear the other gaining privileged access there.

The exact belligerent roster remains provisional.

The war does **not** need to begin as "Sphere A declares war on Sphere B."

The spheres provide the pre-existing relationships through which local security decisions gradually become bloc commitments.

# 4. Phase I — background friction

These events are mostly independent.

They should be visible as ordinary current events long before a conspiracy is suspected.

## D1 — fish shift

**Actor:** North Coast fishing communities / harbor authorities.

**Problem:** fish stocks shift unevenly.

**Rational action:** crews travel farther, enter disputed access areas, demand new agreements.

**Capability change:** more boats operate in politically sensitive waters.

**Outside interpretation:** rivals see encroachment.

**Next incentive:** demand for patrol / enforcement grows.

**Villain touch:** none required.

---

## D2 — piracy becomes more expensive than usual

**Actor:** merchants / captains / NC-M security interests.

**Problem:** genuine raids and losses.

**Rational action:** avoid risky lanes; seek escorts; share threat reports.

**Capability change:** shipping becomes more concentrated and scheduled.

**Outside interpretation:** organized convoying looks like expansion of maritime state power.

**Next incentive:** NC-I / harbors / rivals debate obligations and access.

**Villain touch:** selectively amplify a few accurate danger reports.

---

## D3 — Low Rivers bumper harvest

**Actor:** farmers / warehouses / LR-M merchants.

**Problem:** too much supply.

**Rational action:** seek distant buyers; extend storage; accept lower prices.

**Capability change:** producer solvency becomes dependent on transport and credit.

**Outside interpretation:** none yet.

**Next incentive:** maritime / Port credit shocks can now hit Low Rivers much harder.

**Villain touch:** none.

---

## D4 — Old Cities drought anxiety

**Actor:** OC-U / OC-D water institutions and merchants.

**Problem:** fear of poor water conditions.

**Rational action:** inspect works, compare records, secure reserves and purchase options.

**Capability change:** more coordination and more stockpiling.

**Outside interpretation:** OC-C sees repeated coordination and long-term purchase commitments.

**Next incentive:** OC-C seeks its own guarantees and suppliers.

**Villain touch:** none initially.

---

## D5 — Spine movement / route damage

**Actor:** route authorities / carriers / local jurisdictions.

**Problem:** damaged or uncertain routes.

**Rational action:** inspections, temporary closures, rerouting.

**Capability change:** spare route capacity shrinks.

**Outside interpretation:** alternate corridors gain sudden strategic value.

**Next incentive:** more investment / security pressure elsewhere.

**Villain touch:** none.

---

## D6 — SH-M labor dispute

**Actor:** workers / guilds / owners / civic authorities.

**Problem:** wages, safety, debt, production control.

**Rational action:** bargaining, work slowdown, strike preparation.

**Capability change:** strategic production becomes less predictable.

**Outside interpretation:** buyers worry about future metal / tool access.

**Next incentive:** advance purchasing begins.

**Villain touch:** none initially.

# 5. Phase II — commercial protection

The world is still far from war.

Most decisions are commercial.

## D7 — convoy policy tightens

**Actor:** NC-M / participating harbors.

**Known problem:** piracy.

**Rational action:** stronger convoy windows, patrol concentration, reporting requirements.

**Capability change:** organized armed maritime presence becomes more regular.

**Outside interpretation:** NC-I and some foreign actors worry that temporary anti-piracy coordination is becoming permanent maritime control.

**Next incentive:** demands for guarantees / exemptions.

**Villain touch:** selected intelligence helps convoy advocates win a close policy dispute.

---

## D8 — Port reprices maritime risk

**Actor:** Port lenders / merchants / warehouse interests.

**Known problem:** delay and losses.

**Rational action:** higher risk terms, shorter credit, fewer marginal voyages.

**Capability change:** less credit available for low-margin long-distance cargo.

**Outside interpretation:** affected merchants blame Port finance rather than North Coast security.

**Next incentive:** contracts are canceled.

**Villain touch:** a handful of marginal credit renewals are withdrawn at a useful moment.

---

## D9 — Low Rivers distant purchases weaken

**Actor:** buyers / grain merchants.

**Known problem:** shipping / finance costs.

**Rational action:** cancel or shrink marginal orders.

**Capability change:** grain remains inland; storage fills.

**Outside interpretation:** farmers see merchant abandonment.

**Next incentive:** defaults and emergency policy.

**Villain touch:** none required once D8 occurs.

---

## D10 — distress buying / debt acquisition increases

**Actor:** lenders, land buyers, larger merchants, Villain-linked alternative buyers.

**Known problem:** producers need liquidity.

**Rational action:** purchase debt, land, future output, or distressed cargo.

**Capability change:** ownership / control begins consolidating.

**Outside interpretation:** local reformers see outside interests profiting from crisis.

**Next incentive:** LR-A / local jurisdictions face pressure to restrict purchases.

**Villain touch:** his network genuinely buys some cargo and helps some producers.

---

## D11 — temporary Low Rivers protective rules

Possible forms remain open:

- review of large land / debt transfers;
- emergency storage rules;
- temporary export / purchasing controls;
- priority rules for local buyers.

**Actor:** LR-A / local commercial institutions.

**Known problem:** rapid distress consolidation.

**Rational action:** stop permanent loss of productive assets during a temporary crash.

**Capability change:** outside merchants face less predictable access.

**Outside interpretation:** import-dependent actors see politically controlled food access.

**Next incentive:** secure long-term supply elsewhere / stockpile.

**Villain touch:** publicize genuine examples of aggressive acquisition immediately before the policy debate.

---

## D12 — food-security contracting spreads

**Actor:** Old Cities and other importers.

**Known problem:** Low Rivers purchase uncertainty + drought anxiety.

**Rational action:** lock in contracts, reserves, alternate suppliers.

**Capability change:** more food is pre-committed before reaching open markets.

**Outside interpretation:** competitors see stockpiling.

**Next incentive:** they secure their own reserves.

**Villain touch:** none required.

# 6. Phase III — scarcity created by precaution

This is the first major self-reinforcing section.

Nobody needs to want conflict.

## D13 — reserve buying raises prices

**Actor:** multiple polities / merchant networks.

**Known problem:** uncertain future access.

**Rational action:** buy early.

**Capability change:** spot-market supply shrinks.

**Outside interpretation:** "they know something we don't" / "they are preparing."

**Next incentive:** more reserve buying.

**Villain touch:** quietly ensure several large purchases become visible at nearly the same time.

---

## D14 — timber / rope / repair materials tighten

**Actor:** shipyards, road authorities, merchants, Port infrastructure interests.

**Known problem:** convoy needs, route repairs, harbor maintenance.

**Rational action:** advance contracts.

**Capability change:** strategic transport materials become harder to source.

**Outside interpretation:** maritime and route buildup appears broader than anti-piracy needs.

**Next incentive:** Longwood extraction / road pressure increases.

**Villain touch:** none required.

## D15 — Longwood redundancy project advances

**Actor:** route / merchant / infrastructure interests and one or more Longwood authorities.

**Known problem:** spare route capacity is too low.

**Rational action:** repair or open an alternate connection.

**Capability change:** another corridor could carry strategic cargo if main routes fail.

**Outside interpretation:** affected Longwood rights-holders see outside actors using crisis to expand permanent access.

**Next incentive:** challenge / closure / protest.

**Villain touch:** release a real concession before the full rights process is settled.

## D16 — Longwood route conflict delays redundancy

**Actor:** rights-holders / local authorities / contractors.

**Known problem:** conflicting legitimate claims.

**Rational action:** halt work pending dispute or physically block contested activity.

**Outside interpretation:** traders and governments see Longwood politics as a threat to system reliability.

**Next incentive:** escorts, alternate routing, stronger political pressure.

**Villain touch:** none once the conflict is public.

## D17 — strategic metal advance buying

**Actor:** governments, shipyards, route contractors, merchants.

**Next incentive:** others reserve metal too.

## D18 — SH-M labor dispute hardens

**Rational action:** workers demand disclosure / better terms; owners resist exposing clients.

**Capability change:** production disruption becomes more likely.

# 7. Phase IV — economic rules become security rules

This is the bridge that must exist before war is plausible.

## D19 — strategic export licensing

**Actor:** SH-A or relevant Stone Hills authority.

**Known problem:** labor instability + reserve buying + strategic demand.

**Rational action:** require approval for certain strategic grades / quantities.

**Capability change:** government can now decide who receives critical material.

**Outside interpretation:** neighbors remember older ore coercion and assume the controls may be political.

**Next incentive:** diversify supply, accelerate own purchases, pressure allies.

**Villain touch:** none required.

---

## D20 — route inspections expand

**Actor:** High Roads corridor authorities.

**Known problem:** banditry, false notices, strategic cargo diversion, armed escorts.

**Rational action:** inspect manifests, weapons, seals, escort size.

**Capability change:** authorities gain greater control over military-use cargo moving through chokepoints.

**Outside interpretation:** traders / aligned polities see selective interference.

**Next incentive:** demand exemptions or route around the inspection regime.

**Villain touch:** one forged / contradictory route document helps justify stricter authentication.

---

## D21 — commercial disputes align with competitive spheres

**Actor:** merchant houses / cities / polities.

**Known problem:** access is increasingly uncertain.

**Rational action:** prefer counterparties whose governments / institutions can guarantee delivery.

**Capability change:** trade and credit begin sorting into Charter-leaning and Ascendant-leaning networks.

**Outside interpretation:** old commercial rivalry now looks strategic.

**Next incentive:** political guarantees become more valuable.

**Villain touch:** none. Existing competitive spheres do this work.

---

## D22 — guarantees expand from commerce to security

**Actor:** sphere patrons / allied authorities.

**Known problem:** a partner's cargo, route, or access can be disrupted by another authority.

**Rational action:** promise diplomatic support, replacement supply, escort help, or reciprocal access.

**Capability change:** local disputes acquire outside backers.

**Outside interpretation:** rivals see an emerging bloc.

**Next incentive:** counter-guarantees.

**Villain touch:** privately encourage actors to request guarantees they already have reason to want.

---

## D23 — Port emergency backlog becomes political

**Actor:** Port government / harbor institutions / outside guarantors.

**Known problem:** congestion, repair backlog, security burden, displaced labor / people.

**Rational action:** Port requests faster funding, materials, or emergency authority.

**Capability change:** Port could gain greater independent capacity if requests are granted.

**Outside interpretation:** each external power fears rivals using the emergency to gain privileged influence.

**Next incentive:** consultation / delay / conditional offers.

**Villain touch:** none required.

---

## D24 — private coordination outruns public Port aid

**Actor:** Council-linked houses / commercial networks.

**Known problem:** trade disruption threatens system stability.

**Rational action:** redirect cargo, credit, storage, procurement.

**Capability change:** essential commerce continues selectively.

**Outside interpretation:** still mostly invisible.

**Next incentive:** Port frustration grows because public requests remain slower.

**Villain touch:** collect evidence; do not expose yet.

# 8. Phase V — preparation begins to look like mobilization

At this stage the word **war** may still sound alarmist to many people.

But material capability is changing.

**Known problem:** piracy, banditry, strategic cargo risk.

**Rational action:** larger escorts.

**Capability change:** more armed groups operate near borders, harbors, and chokepoints.

**Outside interpretation:** escorts can become troops with little warning.

**Next incentive:** strengthen checkpoints / patrols.

**Villain touch:** none required.

---

## D26 — old fortifications / posts are repaired

**Actor:** route / border authorities.

**Known problem:** protect inspection points, depots, bridges, passes.

**Rational action:** repair existing defensive infrastructure.

**Capability change:** positions useful in war become usable again.

**Outside interpretation:** "Why repair that old fort if this is only about bandits?"

**Next incentive:** neighbors improve their own posts.

**Villain touch:** ensure rumors of repairs travel faster than the mundane maintenance explanation.

**Villain touch:** none required.

# 9. Phase VI — alignments harden

## D30 — one local dispute tests a guarantee

Example class:

- detained strategic cargo;
- convoy access dispute;
- road inspection refusal;
- disputed fishing / patrol detention.

**Actor:** two local authorities with outside relationships.

**Known problem:** enforcement of a new security rule.

**Rational action:** one detains / denies; the other protests.

**Capability change:** a commercial-security guarantee must either be honored or revealed as meaningless.

**Outside interpretation:** everyone watches whether the patron backs its partner.

**Next incentive:** patrons provide visible support.

**Villain touch:** choose / encourage the dispute most likely to force the guarantee test; do not invent the underlying rule.

# 10. Phase VII — the immediate pre-war security dilemma

At this stage, leaders do not need to desire war.

They need to believe waiting is dangerous.

## D34 — partial mobilization

**Actor:** at least two substantial polities / aligned networks.

**Known problem:** rival escorts, reserves, strategic controls, guarantee commitments.

**Rational action:** call up limited forces / crews to protect routes, depots, or borders.

**Capability change:** forces can now act quickly.

**Outside interpretation:** "limited" mobilization can become offensive mobilization faster than before.

**Next incentive:** counter-mobilization.

**Villain touch:** none required.

---

## D35 — mobilization disrupts trade further

**Actor:** markets / transport systems.

**Known problem:** soldiers / escorts consume animals, ships, food, labor, metal.

**Rational action:** prices rise; civilian transport is displaced.

**Capability change:** economic pain becomes visibly tied to the rival bloc.

**Outside interpretation:** publics conclude the confrontation is already costing them.

**Next incentive:** political demands to either back down immediately or "finish this."

**Villain touch:** propaganda / selective attribution only.

---

## D36 — one side fears being caught unready

**Actor:** leadership facing intelligence about rival readiness.

**Known problem:** first-mover advantage at a chokepoint / route / harbor may be significant.

**Rational action:** move forces closer before talks conclude.

**Capability change:** armed units are physically close enough for mistakes / local initiative to matter.

**Outside interpretation:** imminent attack.

**Next incentive:** local commanders receive broader defensive authority.

**Villain touch:** ensure one accurate movement report reaches the rival before its diplomatic explanation.

# 11. Phase VIII — spark and war

The spark should be **small relative to the structure already built**.

It must not carry the whole burden of causing war.

## D37 — armed chokepoint incident

Strong current class:

> a convoy / strategic cargo movement reaches a road, river, harbor, or maritime inspection point under newly hardened rules.

Possible sequence:

1. escort believes it possesses a valid exemption / guarantee;
2. local authority believes inspection / detention is legally required;
3. both sides have armed personnel because earlier dominoes normalized escorts;
4. delay threatens politically / strategically important cargo;
5. commanders have broader defensive discretion because of mobilization;
6. a boarding, arrest, seizure, warning shot, collision, or panic becomes lethal.

The exact site should be chosen later from the final map.

### Why this is enough now

A year earlier the same incident might have produced:

- apology;
- compensation;
- arbitration.

Now it occurs after:

- commercial restrictions;
- food anxiety;
- strategic export controls;
- hardened spheres;
- reserve buying;
- armed escorts;
- mobilization;
- public accusations;
- tested guarantees.

The deaths are interpreted through that accumulated history.

## D40 — war becomes self-sustaining

Once several actors are engaged:

- routes close;
- Port access becomes contested;
- food / metal / timber controls harden;
- private shipping collapses or militarizes;
- civilians flee;
- old border claims are revived;
- opportunists attack weak positions;
- Council tools are politically compromised;
- arbitration loses practical speed.

At this point the Villain no longer needs to keep knocking dominoes over.

The system is falling under its own weight.

# 13. Villain touch count

The conspiracy is large.

His **direct causal touches should still be a minority of the observable events**.

Working target:

- **40 major author-side dominoes / state changes** in this V1 chain;
- roughly **10–14 meaningful Villain nudges**;
- only **a few** need to be unambiguously criminal or violent;
- many more small operational acts can occur beneath those nudges through agents, brokers, money, records, and timing.

This distinction matters:

> **a large conspiracy does not mean the Villain directly causes every consequence.**

His achievement is selecting leverage points where the world will supply the next several dominoes.

# 15. Ah-ha structure

The story should support several levels of realization.

## Ah-ha 1 — things are connected economically

Wurdren / reader realizes:

> the missing cargo here explains the price problem somewhere else.

No mastermind required.

## Ah-ha 2 — somebody is exploiting the connections

Repeated timing / records / intermediaries indicate deliberate nudges.

Still unclear who or why.

## Ah-ha 3 — the apparent target is not the regions

The chain repeatedly increases pressure on **Port** and on the rules that govern access to it.

## Ah-ha 4 — war is not the Villain's desired possession

War is the forcing mechanism.

His objective is the moment when:

- every power needs Port;
- Port cannot remain passive;
- the old neutrality settlement cannot answer the emergency;
- renegotiation becomes unavoidable.

## Ah-ha 5 — he has begun sacrificing Port to save Port

Evidence from the later chain proves he allowed preventable Port suffering because it improved the political effect.

That is the moral reveal.

# 16. Failure / plausibility test

Before canonizing the exact war sequence, test every edge:

> If the Villain vanished immediately after this domino, would the next two or three reactions still plausibly occur?

For most links, the answer should be **yes**.

Also test:

> If one domino fails, can the system reach the same broad security dilemma through another current pressure?

The conspiracy should have redundancy.

Examples:

- if piracy falls, High Roads insecurity can still drive route / supply precautions;
- if Longwood compromise succeeds, food / metal / water security can still polarize trade;
- if one exposure fails, Port's emergency delay can still radicalize local politics.

The Villain is brilliant because he builds a plan with **multiple paths toward the same strategic condition**, not because reality obeys one perfect script.
