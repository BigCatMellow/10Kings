# Prose test

By the time Wurdren left the shared hall, Port had become harder to cross than several countries he remembered.

Not farther.

Harder.

Every street seemed to contain:

- somebody arriving;
- somebody leaving;
- somebody selling something to both.

Three Moons had filled the city with temporary signs, temporary stalls, temporary rules, and people behaving as if their temporary problem should receive permanent priority.

Wurdren had a packet of certified copies under one arm.

He checked them once.

Then stopped himself.

The Grain Agent had already taught him what happened when he began counting things that were not his responsibility.

A sailor nearly walked into him.

Not because the sailor was drunk.

Because the sailor was arguing backward over one shoulder.

"If they set the window, they command the convoy."

The person behind him said:

"They set the window because six harbors cannot all arrive when they please."

"Then six harbors can pay for it."

"Your harbor wants the escort."

"My harbor wants escorts. It does not want somebody else's officer deciding when we sail."

They passed Wurdren without seeing him.

A third sailor followed.

"Both of you are wrong."

Neither turned around.

The third sailor looked at Wurdren.

"They are."

Wurdren nodded.

"I believe you."

The sailor hurried after them.

That was the entire conversation.

Ten steps later Wurdren passed a stall where a merchant was trying to sell grain for less than the cookshop had charged for his lunch by any sensible measure.

A buyer laughed at the offer.

"Festival week and you are cutting price?"

"I need the space."

"Then keep it on the boat."

"Boat leaves."

"Warehouse."

"Full."

"Another warehouse."

"Costs more than the grain does if I hold it three days."

The buyer looked at the sacks.

"Then your price is too high."

The merchant stared at them.

Wurdren kept walking.

Yesterday he might have found the exchange absurd.

Today he knew enough to find it ordinary.

He was not sure that was improvement.

A group of children ran past carrying painted moon shapes on sticks.

One of them wore a Netman mask.

The teeth had multiplied.

Wurdren wondered whether that was how traditions worked.

Every child added one.

He turned into a market lane and immediately regretted it.

Somebody had decided the narrowest street in Port was the correct place to sell:

- travel blankets;
- rope;
- dried fruit;
- cheap knives;
- copied maps;
- religious tokens;
- three different cures for sore feet.

Wurdren needed:

- lamp oil;
- cord;
- food for the first day out;
- a new buckle for the strap on his travel bag.

He bought the buckle first.

This was not the order he had intended.

At the far end of the lane, a High Roads carrier was arguing with a stable-yard clerk.

"No teams?"

"No teams on that departure."

"You have animals."

"I have animals booked."

"For what?"

"Work."

The carrier leaned across the counter.

"I am work."

"Then you understand."

"I booked this route every season for eleven years."

"And this season everybody booked before you."

"Why?"

The clerk shrugged.

"Escorts. Closures. People don't trust the western road. People do trust the western road. Depends who you ask."

"That does not mean anything."

"It means the safe windows are full."

The carrier looked toward the yard, where several animals were being loaded.

"Those are mine."

"Those belong to a cloth house until fourth day."

"Cloth?"

"Cloth pays."

The carrier said something Wurdren did not catch.

The yard clerk's face suggested it had not been praise.

Wurdren bought the lamp oil from the next stall.

He had now heard enough strangers complain about travel to decide Port was not improving anyone's mood.

A bell sounded somewhere ahead.

Not ceremonial.

Too angry.

A cart had blocked an intersection.

Two performers in bright cloth were trying to carry a painted frame around it.

A guild clerk was trying to carry a box through both.

Nobody was succeeding.

Wurdren went around.

The hiring boards stood under a long awning near one of the older commercial halls.

The red-cornered notice was still there.

He stopped.

The paper offered:

- armed escort;
- northbound;
- good pay;
- departure the following morning;
- preference for experienced independent hands.

Wurdren read it twice.

This was, on paper, exactly the sort of work he had imagined when he decided to return to the road.

Risk.

Distance.

Unknown trouble.

A destination he had not seen.

He found the hiring desk listed at the bottom of the notice.

It was occupied.

A woman with a scar under one eye looked up from a ledger.

"You here for north escort?"

"Maybe."

"Experience?"

"Some."

"That means yes or no depending on whether you're lying."

"Yes."

"Recent?"

Wurdren considered the grain.

"Very."

She looked at him.

"Armed?"

"The grain was difficult."

"No."

"Then not recent."

"Fair."

She named the pay.

It was better than the Longwood fee.

Considerably.

"Departure?"

"First light."

Wurdren looked down at the certified Longwood copies under his arm.

"Of course."

"You have another contract?"

"Something like one."

"Better?"

"No."

"Then why are you standing here?"

That was a good question.

He disliked it.

"I said I'd go."

The recruiter shrugged.

"Then go."

No argument.

No attempt to persuade him.

That was almost insulting.

"If the place is still open tonight?"

"It won't be."

"Good."

"Why good?"

Wurdren had no answer worth giving.

He left.

The Old Port Hand found him before he reached the end of the awning.

"You went to the board."

"I looked."

"You took something?"

"No."

They seemed unsurprised.

That annoyed him too.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Everyone in this city has a face today."

"Festival."

They handed him a folded procurement slip.

Wurdren stared at it.

"No."

"You haven't read it."

"I have developed caution around paper."

"North lift."

He remembered the young worker.

"Pins."

"Pins."

"Still not here?"

"Now officially not here."

Wurdren opened the slip.

Most of it meant very little to him.

One line was clear enough:

> **supplier capacity committed under priority terms; ordinary delivery deferred.**

"How long?"

"Too long."

"Can somebody here make them?"

"Yes."

"Then why not?"

"They can make something that fits. I need something we know will hold the load."

"Inspection?"

"Inspection. Known work. Known iron. Known maker."

Wurdren handed the slip back.

"So order elsewhere."

"We are."

"Problem solved."

"No."

The Old Port Hand pointed toward the quay.

"The lift stays half-useful until something arrives. During Three Moons."

"Problem not solved."

"Now you understand management."

A runner waited behind them with a leather message case.

The Old Port Hand folded the slip with two other papers and gave the packet over.

"Guild copy first. Supplier copy after. If the first office says wait until after festival, make them write that."

The runner nodded.

"Where's it going?" Wurdren asked.

"West."

"Stone Hills?"

"Eventually."

The runner tucked the packet away.

Wurdren watched it disappear into the crowd.

"Priority what?"

The Old Port Hand shrugged.

"Priority somebody."

"Helpful."

"It means somebody with more leverage than a broken lift."

They did not sound suspicious.

Only irritated.

That mattered.

A festival procession began somewhere upriver.

Wurdren could not see it.

He could hear:

- drums;
- bells;
- a cheer rising in sections as something moved through the streets.

People around the hiring boards began drifting toward the sound.

The Old Port Hand did not.

They were looking at the lift schedule.

"You don't want to see it?" Wurdren asked.

"I have seen Three Moons."

"Not this one."

"They are all this one while you're in them."

Then they folded the schedule.

"I'll see the evening part."

Wurdren looked toward the sound.

For one moment he felt the old pull:

- crowd;
- event;
- somewhere important;
- the possibility that the thing worth remembering was happening one street away.

The Longwood papers under his arm felt very dry.

"Your lanterns," the Old Port Hand said.

Wurdren closed his eyes.

"No."

"You promised."

"I did not."

"You failed to refuse clearly."

"That is not agreement."

"Shared hall might rule against you."

"Your understanding of law has become dangerous."

The Old Port Hand handed him a wrapped bundle.

It was light.

Offensively light.

"Three?"

"Three."

"I hate you."

"Tonight means tonight."

Wurdren carried the lanterns.

They walked together for part of the way.

The streets became denser closer to the residential blocks.

Festival visitors filled doorways.

People leaned from upper windows.

Food smoke hung above the lane.

Three painted moon disks had been tied over one doorway, each made by a different hand and none quite round.

A group of musicians had taken a corner where Wurdren remembered a cart repairer.

He did not mention it.

At the next crossing, the Old Port Hand stopped.

"Here."

Wurdren handed over the lanterns.

"I thought I was carrying them home."

"I am home."

Wurdren looked at the building.

Then at them.

"You live over a cooper."

"Used to be a cooper."

"Of course."

A voice from an upper window called:

"Did you get them?"

The Old Port Hand raised the bundle.

"Three."

"All the same?"

They looked at Wurdren.

He had no idea.

"Yes," he said.

The Old Port Hand looked inside the wrapping.

"No."

The voice above groaned.

Wurdren said:

"I have fulfilled my obligation."

"You have created a new one."

"That is Port's fault."

"Works at distance, apparently."

He laughed.

They stood there for another moment.

The drums sounded again.

Somewhere above the roofs, people cheered.

"Morning," the Old Port Hand said.

"What?"

"You leave in the morning."

"Yes."

"For the forest paperwork."

"Apparently."

"Very grand."

"Extremely."

The Old Port Hand nodded.

"Good."

Wurdren waited for them to say something else.

They did not.

A child came down the stairs behind them and grabbed the lantern bundle.

"These are different."

"So I've been told," Wurdren said.

The child looked offended by his failure.

The Old Port Hand smiled.

Wurdren left them there.

At the end of the lane he could still hear the festival.

He had not seen the procession.

He had missed whatever the cheering was for.

The northbound escort would leave without him in the morning.

The Longwood party would not.

He needed a bed.

That, at least, was one problem Port understood how to make difficult without involving the rest of the world.

He went looking for one.
