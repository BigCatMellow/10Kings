# Nomads and Itinerant Peoples

## Status

**Working canon at the structural level; specific peoples, names, routes, and legal privileges remain open.**

The world contains enough seasonal labor, pastoral movement, caravan traffic, river travel, pilgrimage, displaced populations, and itinerant work that **mobile ways of life should exist as ordinary parts of the social world**.

This does **not** establish one culture called "the nomads."

Different mobile communities can have different:

- origins;
- languages;
- livelihoods;
- religions;
- political status;
- route systems;
- relationships with settled populations.

One favored design direction remains an **Appalachian-influenced mobile network**, but its name, exact origin, and degree of ethnic unity are not yet canon.

# 1. Source reconciliation

The preserved nomad material contains two useful layers.

## Comparative research

[Nomads — Types](../../legacy-notes/2026-09-22/consolidated-package/04_Cultures_Peoples_Daily_Life.md#nomads---types) surveys real mobile societies built around:

- seasonal pastoralism;
- highland / lowland movement;
- trade;
- clan networks;
- oral history;
- craft;
- water and pasture access;
- conflict with expanding settled institutions.

This is reference material, not in-world canon.

## Appalachian-influenced concept

[Nomads — Appalachian](../../legacy-notes/2026-09-22/consolidated-package/04_Cultures_Peoples_Daily_Life.md#nomads---appalachian) proposes a mobile people shaped by:

- extended kin;
- mountain routes;
- seasonal movement;
- portable crafts;
- music and ballads;
- herbal / practical knowledge;
- repair and reuse;
- suspicion of extractive authorities;
- repeated trade with settled communities.

It also contains many specific legacy names, costume ideas, supernatural touches, and one-to-one cultural mixtures.

Those specifics are **not current canon**.

The current owner retains the structural mechanisms and discards the need to preserve the old named tribes.

# 2. Mobility must solve a real problem

Do not make people mobile because "nomads are interesting."

A durable mobile way of life needs a recurring reason to move.

Current world conditions support several possibilities.

## Pastoral movement

Animals may need:

- summer upland pasture;
- winter lowland pasture;
- seasonal water;
- access to different grazing zones.

## Itinerant trades

Some livelihoods benefit from moving between settlements:

- repair;
- metalwork;
- woodwork;
- basketry;
- leatherwork;
- animal care;
- entertainment;
- specialized seasonal services.

## Seasonal labor

Workers may follow:

- harvests;
- orchards;
- fisheries;
- construction seasons;
- ship work;
- mining surges;
- caravan demand;
- major fairs.

## Caravan and route work

Some households can specialize in:

- animal handling;
- guiding;
- repair;
- provisioning;
- interpretation;
- caravan support.

## River and boat mobility

Households may move through:

- navigable rivers;
- seasonal river markets;
- fishing grounds;
- cargo work;
- ferry / barge networks.

## Pilgrimage and religious travel

Some people may spend substantial parts of life moving between:

- shrines;
- communities;
- seasonal gatherings;
- hospitality networks.

## Displacement made permanent

Families displaced by:

- pre-Convergence wars;
- lost land;
- border regularization;
- debt;
- environmental change;

may develop mobility into a durable livelihood rather than eventually settling.

Different causes can produce different mobile cultures.

---

# 3. Mobility usually means circuits, not wandering

The strongest working model is:

> **repeatable circuits rather than random wandering.**

A circuit has anchors.

Possible anchors include:

- pasture;
- water;
- harvest work;
- fairs;
- markets;
- mines;
- ports;
- caravan junctions;
- religious gatherings;
- kin households;
- winter quarters;
- repair customers.

A household may therefore travel widely while knowing exactly where it expects to be during different parts of the year.

~~~text
season / demand
→ destination
→ work / grazing / market / kin visit
→ next destination
→ eventual return
~~~

This creates **geographic familiarity**, not placelessness.

---

# 4. A mobile household can be deeply attached to place

Do not treat mobility as the opposite of belonging.

A mobile family may have strong attachment to:

- one winter settlement;
- one mountain pass;
- one burial ground;
- one spring;
- one annual fair;
- one cluster of kin households;
- one grazing valley;
- one road;
- one religious site.

A place can matter because people **return to it**, not only because they live there continuously.

That distinction should shape:

- songs;
- inheritance;
- memory;
- disputes;
- hospitality;
- political claims.

---

# 5. Durable circuits require access arrangements

This is a **derived constraint** from repeated mobility.

If people return to the same places for generations, some relationship must exist between the travelers and the places they use.

That relationship does **not** need to be one universal legal right.

Possible arrangements include:

- customary access;
- rent;
- seasonal lease;
- grazing agreement;
- work contract;
- guild privilege;
- kin hospitality;
- religious hospitality;
- market privilege;
- reciprocal arrangement;
- local toleration;
- unauthorized use.

Therefore:

> **Do not invent universal "nomad stopping rights." Derive access stop by stop from the institutions already present there.**

One circuit can contain several legal relationships.

---

# 6. Circuits cross jurisdictions

See [Law, Jurisdiction, and Dispute Resolution](../Law-and-Jurisdiction.md).

A mobile household may cross:

- several polities;
- guild jurisdictions;
- toll systems;
- Port;
- High Roads route authorities;
- culturally mixed border zones.

That creates recurring questions:

- Who can charge them?
- Who can exclude them?
- Where can animals graze?
- Where can wagons or boats stop?
- Which court hears a dispute?
- Which authority recognizes a contract made elsewhere?
- What happens if a household is accused after it has moved on?

There is no current basis for one trans-regional "nomad law."

Legal status should vary by route and polity.

---

# 7. Political identity can differ from residence

A mobile person may have:

- birth ties to one polity;
- kin in several;
- work relationships across borders;
- guild membership;
- religious membership;
- no strong attachment to the government of the place currently occupied.

This does **not** automatically mean statelessness.

Possible statuses can include:

- citizenship somewhere despite seasonal absence;
- local residency rights;
- household affiliation;
- recognized traveling status;
- foreigner status;
- disputed status.

Exact citizenship models remain open because most polity citizenship systems are also still open.

---

# 8. Property does not have to mean landlessness

Do not assume mobile people own nothing fixed.

A mobile household might own or share rights in:

- animals;
- wagons;
- boats;
- tools;
- stored goods;
- a winter house;
- a workshop;
- a burial plot;
- pasture rights;
- orchard shares;
- land occupied seasonally;
- property managed by settled kin.

Different mobile groups can have different property systems.

Mobility is an economic pattern, not proof of poverty.

---

# 9. Work creates relationships with settled communities

A durable circuit requires customers, employers, hosts, buyers, or partners.

This means mobile and settled populations are not separate worlds.

Repeated relationships can include:

- the same repair family serving the same mining towns;
- the same herders using the same market;
- the same harvest crews returning to the same Low Rivers district;
- the same performers appearing at the same fair;
- the same caravan households staying at the same High Roads inns;
- the same boat families trading with the same river settlements.

Over generations these relationships can become:

- friendships;
- debts;
- marriages;
- patronage;
- feuds;
- customary obligations;
- stereotypes.

---

# 10. The route is a social institution

A circuit is more than geography.

Repeated movement produces knowledge about:

- safe camps;
- wells;
- road conditions;
- honest merchants;
- dangerous officials;
- seasonal prices;
- local dialects;
- ferry schedules;
- marriages;
- deaths;
- missing people;
- which authority currently controls a crossing.

This knowledge can be inherited.

Therefore mobile households may function as **living route archives** without needing a formal organization.

---

# 11. Information role

See [Movement and Information](../Movement-and-Information.md).

Mobile households are particularly good at carrying:

- local news;
- family news;
- route warnings;
- prices;
- job information;
- songs;
- stories;
- reputations;
- marriages;
- missing-person information;
- religious ideas.

Their network has distinctive strengths:

- repeated contact with small communities;
- difficult-route knowledge;
- long memory;
- kin-based trust.

And weaknesses:

- seasonal cadence;
- incomplete coverage;
- information shaped by the circuit;
- outsider distrust.

They are not a universal courier service.

---

# 12. Information can move farther than one household

Several circuits can intersect.

At:

- markets;
- fairs;
- Port;
- High Roads caravan yards;
- shrines;
- seasonal gatherings;

different mobile households can exchange information.

That creates a relay effect:

~~~text
local settlement
→ recurring mobile household
→ shared market / gathering
→ another mobile circuit
→ distant settlement
~~~

This can move social information widely without any central institution coordinating it.

---

# 13. Mobile culture can spread without people permanently settling

Because households repeatedly visit many communities, they can carry:

- tunes;
- stories;
- tool designs;
- repair methods;
- words;
- clothing techniques;
- recipes;
- religious practices.

The process works both directions.

A traveling group should change too.

A practice picked up in one place may later appear elsewhere through the circuit.

This supports the world's wider cultural-bleed model.

---

# 14. Appalachian influence — what to keep

The useful Appalachian influence is **social and historical**, not costume shorthand.

Good inspiration includes:

- dense extended-kin networks;
- family reputation;
- music and ballads as social memory;
- songs preserving people, crimes, disasters, loves, and places;
- attachment to difficult upland landscapes;
- repair and reuse;
- mutual aid;
- suspicion of distant extractive authority;
- humor and storytelling as ways to negotiate status;
- strong local attachments despite wider mobility.

Historical Appalachia was not generally nomadic.

Therefore mobility itself should come from actual mobile economic systems:

- transhumance;
- itinerant craft;
- seasonal labor;
- caravan work;
- river travel;
- displacement.

Do not turn Appalachian influence into:

- caricatured spelling;
- "hillbilly" behavior;
- one costume;
- moonshine jokes;
- mystical mountain wisdom.

---

# 15. Ballads can function as memory without becoming perfect archives

The legacy concept strongly supports song and storytelling.

This fits the current information world well.

Ballads may preserve:

- disasters;
- routes;
- murders;
- marriages;
- betrayals;
- famous court cases;
- vanished settlements;
- family reputations.

But oral tradition changes.

Different versions can:

- disagree;
- omit names;
- favor one family;
- turn history into comedy;
- merge events;
- preserve a detail official records forgot.

That makes songs valuable evidence of memory without making them infallible records.

---

# 16. Kin networks can cross political borders

Extended kin is one plausible source of continuity for mobile communities.

Kin can provide:

- lodging;
- introductions;
- childcare;
- marriage ties;
- emergency aid;
- information;
- storage;
- contacts in local markets.

This creates a trans-regional network that is neither a state nor a guild.

It also creates internal politics:

- old feuds;
- obligations;
- favored branches;
- contested marriages;
- unequal wealth.

Do not make "the family" one harmonious bloc.

---

# 17. Households do not all need to travel together

Mobility can vary inside one family.

Possible pattern:

- some adults travel;
- older relatives remain at a winter settlement;
- young adults take seasonal work elsewhere;
- children spend part of the year with settled kin;
- one branch operates boats while another works overland.

This can create a **mobile network** even when not every member is continuously itinerant.

Exact household organization remains open.

---

# 18. Settling does not automatically end cultural membership

Some members may become:

- town residents;
- Port workers;
- guild craftspeople;
- farmers;
- innkeepers.

They can retain:

- kin obligations;
- circuit knowledge;
- songs;
- marriage links;
- hospitality duties;
- seasonal participation.

Likewise, settled outsiders may marry into or join mobile households.

This prevents the culture from becoming biologically or territorially sealed.

---

# 19. Mobile peoples should have internal class differences

Do not romanticize mobility as egalitarian.

Differences may arise from:

- herd size;
- wagon / boat ownership;
- specialized skills;
- access to valuable routes;
- merchant credit;
- kin prestige;
- religious status;
- ownership of fixed property;
- relationships with settled patrons.

One traveling household may be wealthy.

Another may be precarious seasonal labor.

They need not share political interests.

---

# 20. Route closure hits mobile people differently

A road closure is not only a transport problem.

For a circuit-dependent household it may mean:

- lost pasture;
- missed employment;
- inability to reach winter shelter;
- separation from kin;
- missed market;
- debt;
- forced trespass;
- pressure to use the Underpass;
- settlement somewhere not planned.

This makes route politics personal.

---

# 21. The Convergence likely changed mobility without inventing it

Mobile ways of life should predate the Convergence.

The Convergence may have changed:

- border predictability;
- tolls;
- protected passage;
- market access;
- route safety;
- legal recognition.

But current sources do **not** establish a special Convergence status for mobile peoples.

Some old customary routes may even have become harder when borders were formalized.

This is a strong future historical question, not current fact.

---

# 22. Border regularization can create losers

A route used for generations may cross land that later becomes:

- privately owned;
- taxed;
- fenced;
- restricted;
- reserved for another use;
- divided by a new political boundary.

The Convergence made some movement safer while potentially turning other customary movement into legal ambiguity.

That fits the broader post-Convergence world.

---

# 23. Mobile households and guilds

Some itinerant crafts may overlap with guild systems.

Possible relationships include:

- full guild membership;
- temporary permits;
- tolerated informal practice;
- competition with local guilds;
- contracting through guild branches.

Do not assume mobile craftspeople are automatically outside professional institutions.

Do not assume guilds automatically welcome them.

Exact relationships should be local.

---

# 24. Mobile households and religions

Religious networks can provide:

- hospitality;
- shrines;
- food;
- mediation;
- safe stopping places;
- social ties.

Mobile people can also spread religious practice between communities.

Do not assign one faith to all itinerant groups.

A single extended family may contain several traditions.

---

# 25. Mobile households and Port

Port can be:

- winter work;
- a market;
- a performance destination;
- a place to repair equipment;
- a relay between land and sea circuits;
- a place where some relatives settle permanently.

This does not mean every mobile circuit reaches Port.

Port's value is network convergence, not universal necessity.

---

# 26. Mobile households and High Roads

High Roads is especially compatible with mobile livelihoods because it concentrates:

- caravan work;
- livestock movement;
- markets;
- repair;
- route information;
- multilingual contact.

But mobile communities should not become a High Roads subculture by default.

Their circuits may cross several cultural cores.

---

# 27. Mobile households and the Underpass

Some households may know or use Underpass routes.

This can be valuable when surface routes are:

- seasonal;
- closed;
- politically restricted;
- dangerous.

But [The Underpass](../Places/The-Underpass.md) has:

- fragmented authority;
- limited capacity;
- dangerous branches;
- specialized local knowledge.

Do not turn mobile peoples into people who secretly know every tunnel.

---

# 28. Outsider suspicion has material causes

Settled people may distrust travelers because:

- they leave before local disputes conclude;
- their kin cross borders;
- they know unofficial routes;
- they compete with local workers;
- they are difficult for tax or guild authorities to monitor;
- rumor follows unfamiliar people.

Mobile people may distrust settled authorities because:

- access can be revoked;
- tolls change;
- local officials exploit unfamiliar status;
- landlords enclose customary stopping places;
- guilds protect local competitors.

These tensions should not be written as natural ethnic hatred.

They arise from institutions and incentives.

---

# 29. Crime is not a cultural function

Mobility can make smuggling possible.

That does not make itinerant peoples criminals.

Some individuals may use:

- route knowledge;
- kin networks;
- legal ambiguity;

for illicit trade, just as merchants, sailors, officials, and guild members do.

See [Crime and the Underworld](../Politics/Crime-and-Underworld.md).

Avoid using a whole mobile culture as shorthand for the black market.

---

# 30. Government attitudes should vary

Different polities may see the same mobile group as:

- useful labor;
- reliable traders;
- suspicious outsiders;
- taxable travelers;
- military scouts;
- unwanted competition;
- old customary partners.

This should vary by:

- local economy;
- current politics;
- route importance;
- history;
- labor demand.

There is no reason for one continent-wide policy.

---

# 31. Legal status should be built from ordinary questions

Before inventing special nomad law, ask:

1. Where is this household considered legally attached, if anywhere?
2. Who taxes them?
3. What property do they own?
4. What permits or contracts do they actually need?
5. Who guarantees access to the next stop?
6. Which court can hear a dispute?
7. What happens when they cross a border?
8. What happens when a child is born away from a home jurisdiction?
9. Where are marriages, deaths, debts, or apprenticeships recorded?
10. What happens if a route becomes illegal or unusable?

Many of these answers should differ from polity to polity.

---

# 32. A circuit-design test

For any proposed mobile community, trace one full year.

At every stop record:

| Question | What to establish |
| --- | --- |
| Why here? | work, pasture, market, kin, religion, shelter |
| Why now? | season, demand, weather, calendar |
| Access | custom, contract, rent, hospitality, toleration, unauthorized |
| Income | what is sold / earned |
| Supplies | what must be acquired |
| Authority | who can regulate or expel them |
| Relationships | recurring local partners / rivals |
| Information | what they learn and carry onward |
| Risk | weather, law, debt, route failure, conflict |
| Next move | why they leave |

If a stop exists only because "nomads travel there," the circuit is underbuilt.

---

# 33. A legal stress test

Take the same household through three different jurisdictions.

Ask:

- does the same property right survive?
- does the same guild credential matter?
- is the same marriage recognized?
- can the same animals graze?
- can the same debt be enforced?
- can an official detain them?
- can a local employer refuse agreed wages once they leave?

If every answer is identical, the political world is probably too uniform.

---

# 34. What is structurally established

The current world supports:

- multiple forms of mobile livelihood;
- recurring circuits rather than random wandering;
- mobile information networks;
- strong route knowledge;
- movement across cultural and political borders;
- repeated economic relationships with settled communities;
- the possibility of an Appalachian-influenced kin / ballad / repair tradition;
- mobile people as cultural transmitters rather than isolated outsiders.

---

# 35. Derived constraints

Any durable circuit requires:

- reliable reasons to move;
- repeat destinations;
- access arrangements of some kind;
- economic exchange;
- information about route conditions;
- accommodation to multiple jurisdictions.

Long-lived mobility should also create:

- memories attached to routes and stops;
- settled relationships;
- legal disputes;
- cross-border kin;
- cultural exchange.

---

# 36. Still open

- whether the Appalachian-influenced concept is one people, several related peoples, or a broad social network;
- self-name and outsider names;
- origin history;
- exact seasonal circuits;
- exact livelihoods by circuit;
- languages / dialects;
- fixed settlements or winter quarters;
- citizenship / legal attachment;
- taxation;
- grazing and stopping agreements;
- guild status;
- education;
- marriage / inheritance across jurisdictions;
- burial practices;
- exact political organization;
- current conflicts over route access.

These should be solved through specific circuits and household histories rather than a universal nomad template.

---

# 37. Design rule

Use:

~~~text
WHY MOVE?
→ WHERE REPEATEDLY?
→ WHAT ECONOMIC RELATIONSHIP EXISTS THERE?
→ WHAT ACCESS ARRANGEMENT MAKES THE STOP POSSIBLE?
→ WHICH JURISDICTION APPLIES?
→ WHAT DO THEY CARRY AWAY?
→ WHY DO THEY MOVE AGAIN?
~~~

Do not begin with:

> What do the nomads look like?

Begin with:

> **What recurring movement pattern makes this household's life possible?**

Culture should then accumulate around that lived system.
