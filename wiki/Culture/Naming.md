# Naming

## Status

**Working cultural framework.** Naming rules are established at the design level; exact language families, local endonyms, etymologies and most personal-name systems remain provisional.

## Rule

Names should feel as if they have histories.

The setting should use the *logic* of real place naming rather than random fantasy phonetics.

Useful naming sources include:
- geography: river mouth, red hill, narrow pass, clear water;
- settlement function: market, fort, port, bridge, shrine;
- founder or lineage;
- older ethnic or linguistic groups;
- industry or resource;
- aspirational names;
- religious or philosophical associations;
- conquest and renaming;
- translation layers where an old name survives in a newer language.

## Real-world pattern pools already discussed

The project has explicitly looked at:
- American descriptive, occupational, founder, Indigenous, immigrant, and aspirational place names;
- Middle Eastern names tied to geography, settlement, religion, rulers, and function;
- Scandinavian compound names tied to terrain, farms, harbors, markets, and people;
- Chinese names built from geographic position, symbolic concepts, administrative function, and older historical references.

The lesson is **not** to copy the sound of these languages indiscriminately. It is to give each region a coherent history of naming.

## Regional direction

Exact naming languages remain open, but a useful model is:

- **Ironcrest:** older upland/river names beneath later mining, fort, family, and guild names.
- **Northwind:** coastal geography, bays, islands, currents, lineages, seasonal sites.
- **Greenvale:** rivers, soils, old settlements, family lands, markets, former estates.
- **Highridge:** route names, passes, wells, caravan families, old toll sites, translated names from multiple languages.
- **Deepwood:** waterways, groves, species, older peoples, landmarks that may not be obvious to outsiders.
- **Sunplains:** old city-state names, founders, civic ideals, waterworks, forts, ports, estates.
- **Port:** layers upon layers — indigenous/old geographic name, official charter name, district nicknames, immigrant neighborhood names, merchant terminology.

## Border names

Border towns are especially likely to have:
- two accepted names;
- one name translated differently by each side;
- a hybrid name;
- an old political name nobody uses officially;
- family names crossing linguistic boundaries.

## Personal names

Do not assign one real-world ethnic naming system wholesale to a region.

Instead develop:
- sound patterns;
- common roots;
- patronymic/matronymic/family systems;
- class differences;
- marriage practices;
- regional spelling variation;
- religious names;
- older names revived for political reasons.

Several previously proposed character names are retained as **provisional** until this system is finalized.

See [Personal Naming Framework](../Reference/Personal-Naming-Framework.md) for the current design method.


## Working name-layer model

A place can accumulate several names across time.

Useful layers include:

1. **substrate name** — older than the present polity or regional identity;
2. **local descriptive / lineage name** — founder, family, market, shrine, bridge, spring, fort, mill, quarry, waterwork;
3. **conquest / administrative name** — imposed, translated, respelled or appended by later rulers;
4. **Convergence standard form** — a spelling or translation stabilized by treaties, maps, courts or merchants;
5. **commercial nickname** — traveler, guild, sailor, soldier or market shorthand;
6. **political revival** — an older name deliberately restored as a claim about identity or legitimacy.

Two or more layers can remain in ordinary use at the same time.

A naming dispute can therefore be a political or historical dispute rather than a spelling error.

## Macro-region naming convention

The current labels:

- Ironcrest;
- Northwind;
- Greenvale;
- Highridge / Highridge Plateau;
- Deepwood;
- Sunplains;
- The Spine;
- The Underpass;
- Port;

should be treated as stable **reader-facing / interregional labels** while exact local-language forms remain open.

They may ultimately represent:

- translations;
- common diplomatic forms;
- trade exonyms;
- shortened legal names;
- broad geographic labels used by outsiders.

This prevents their transparent English form from implying that every historical language in the setting builds names the same way.

Do not rename these labels merely to make them sound more fantastical.

Instead determine what naming layer they belong to and which older/local names coexist with them.

## Naming and The Convergence

The Convergence likely became a major naming event even if nobody intended a language reform.

Treaties, maps and arbitration require negotiators to decide what places are called.

Plausible consequences include:

- one spelling becoming the treaty standard;
- translated boundary features gaining international names;
- an interpreter's simplification becoming legally durable;
- two treaty copies preserving different names for the same place;
- governments later promoting official regional forms;
- older local names surviving in speech after disappearing from maps.

This fits the established history in which the Convergence strengthened regional identities through law, institutions and historical standardization.

## Design test

Before accepting a new important place name, ask:

1. Who first used it?
2. In what language or register?
3. What did it mean then?
4. Is that meaning still understood?
5. Did another population translate, shorten or respell it?
6. Is the current form local, legal, commercial or foreign?
7. Does an older name survive?
8. Would class, generation or politics change which form a speaker uses?

If none of these questions has an answer, the name is probably still a placeholder.
