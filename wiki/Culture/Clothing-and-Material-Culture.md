# Clothing and Material Culture

## Status

**Working cultural framework; specific garments, fashions and regional styles remain provisional unless established elsewhere.**

This page owns clothing, textiles, repair, personal equipment, ordinary objects, status display, fashion circulation and the material things people carry through daily life.

## Core rule

Clothing should be generated from:

```text
climate
+ available fiber / leather / hides
+ trade
+ occupation
+ class
+ law / religion
+ technology
+ repair culture
+ fashion
+ personal taste
= what somebody actually wears
```

Do not assign one costume to one region.

A city porter, court official, miner, farmer, sailor, religious teacher, wealthy merchant and child from the same region should not look like palette-swapped versions of one outfit.

## Source disposition

Legacy clothing notes contain useful candidates for:

- wool, linen, hemp, cotton-like fibers where ecologically plausible;
- leather, fur, felt, bark / plant fibers and woven materials;
- work aprons;
- layered cold-weather clothing;
- sun protection;
- dust / wind scarves;
- practical belts, pouches and tool carrying;
- patching and reuse;
- imported fabric as status;
- guild / household / clan identifiers;
- clothing varying by class and occupation;
- border and itinerant people acquiring garments from several trade zones.

Legacy material is **not** authoritative where it assigns:

- one signature silhouette to an entire region;
- one color palette to a people;
- “forest camouflage” as ordinary Deepwood dress;
- decorative motifs simply because they match a region's theme;
- direct copies of modern Earth ethnic clothing;
- personality through clothing (“stoic,” “gritty,” “elegant,” etc.).

Recover materials and mechanisms, not costume sets.

# 1. Textile economy

Clothing begins with production.

For any community, ask:

1. What fibers are locally available?
2. Which are imported?
3. Who spins?
4. Who weaves?
5. Who dyes?
6. Who tans leather?
7. Who makes shoes?
8. Who repairs clothing?
9. Are textile trades guilded?
10. Which materials are taxed or controlled?
11. What is cheap?
12. What is prestigious?

Possible materials include, where ecology supports them:

- wool;
- linen / flax-like fiber;
- hemp-like fiber;
- cotton-like fiber;
- felt;
- leather;
- fur;
- hide;
- bark cloth;
- reeds / grasses;
- silk-like imported luxury fiber if later established;
- woven hair / animal fiber;
- rope / cord.

Do not assume every region can grow every fiber.

---

# 2. Clothing is expensive

In a pre-industrial world, most people should own fewer garments than modern readers expect.

Consequences:

- repair matters;
- garments are altered;
- children wear hand-me-downs;
- clothes are inherited;
- fabric is reused;
- damaged garments become patches, wrappings, bags or stuffing;
- secondhand markets matter;
- a fine garment can outlive its original owner.

Visible mending is normal.

Invisible or decorative mending can become a class / skill marker.

## Social effect

A poor person may own a high-quality garment because it is:

- inherited;
- bought secondhand;
- received from an employer;
- guild-issued;
- repaired for decades.

Clothing should not function as a simple wealth meter.

---

# 3. Layers and climate

## Cold / wet conditions

Clothing may prioritize:

- layered wool;
- felt;
- fur where available;
- leather;
- water-resistant oils / waxes;
- closeable cuffs;
- head / neck protection;
- footwear insulation;
- replaceable outer layers.

Northwind and exposed Highridge communities are likely to use such strategies often, but the exact garment forms should vary locally.

## Hot / dry conditions

Clothing may prioritize:

- breathable woven cloth;
- shade;
- loose layers;
- head / neck covering;
- sun protection;
- dust protection;
- air circulation.

Sunplains forms should vary strongly by city, occupation, wealth and religious practice.

## Wet / forest environments

Clothing may prioritize:

- fast drying;
- replaceable leg / foot protection;
- insect protection;
- rain shedding;
- light layers;
- locally available fibers;
- carrying tools without snagging.

Do not convert this into universal “camouflage clothing.”

## Industrial / dirty work

Garments may include:

- aprons;
- oversleeves;
- gloves;
- caps;
- reinforced knees / elbows;
- replaceable outer layers;
- leather or heavy cloth protection;
- eye / face protection where plausible;
- work footwear.

This is occupational clothing, not “Ironcrest national dress.”

---

# 4. Workwear

Occupation should often predict clothing more strongly than region.

Examples:

### Smith / foundry worker
- heat protection;
- leather / dense cloth apron;
- tied-back hair or covering;
- minimal loose fabric near fire;
- repairable boots.

### Miner
- layered clothing for temperature change;
- durable knees / elbows;
- lamp / tool carrying;
- dust coverings in some mines;
- boots suited to mud / stone.

### Sailor / fisher
- weather-resistant outer layer;
- secure clothing that does not snag;
- gloves;
- replaceable wet footwear;
- belt / knife / line tools.

### Farmer
- sun / rain protection;
- layers that can be removed;
- durable lower garments;
- tool carrying;
- seasonal variation.

### Caravan worker
- wind / dust protection;
- layered temperature control;
- pockets / pouches;
- sturdy footwear;
- repair kit.

### Clerk / interpreter
- clothing that signals cleanliness, office, employer or education;
- portable writing / seal equipment;
- perhaps less physically protective clothing.

### Healer
- washable layers where practice recognizes contamination;
- pouches / containers;
- institution or school identifiers where relevant.

These are starting points, not uniforms.

---

# 5. Shoes and feet

Footwear deserves attention because it is expensive and geographically sensitive.

Possible forms depend on:

- mud;
- snow;
- stone;
- ship decks;
- hot ground;
- indoor / outdoor custom;
- animal products;
- available leather;
- woven plant material.

Poor people may:

- repair soles repeatedly;
- use wooden components;
- go barefoot seasonally where practical;
- own one good pair for travel / ceremony.

Footwear can reveal occupation more reliably than decorative clothing.

# 6. Head, hair and weather protection

Headwear can serve:

- heat;
- cold;
- rain;
- dust;
- occupational safety;
- modesty;
- religion;
- marital status;
- rank;
- fashion.

Do not assign one hat to a region.

Hair customs may vary by:

- profession;
- class;
- faith;
- generation;
- mourning;
- military service;
- fashion.

Specific rules remain open.

---

# 7. Pockets, belts and carried things

People need to carry things.

Possible systems:

- sewn pockets;
- belt pouches;
- shoulder bags;
- baskets;
- satchels;
- tool rolls;
- document tubes;
- knife sheaths;
- key rings;
- coin purses;
- religious tokens;
- lunch containers.

Material culture becomes visible through what a person automatically checks before leaving home.

For example:

- dock worker → gloves, knife, cord;
- caravan clerk → seal, tablet / paper, weights;
- farm worker → twine, knife, cloth, food;
- healer → packets, bandages, containers;
- Port runner → passes, messages, small coin, district tokens.

Exact technology depends on later owner decisions.

---

# 8. Repair culture

Repair is culturally significant because replacement is expensive.

Ask:

- who is expected to mend?
- is mending domestic or professional?
- are patches hidden or displayed?
- does a guild certify repairs?
- when does a garment become shamefully worn?
- when does wear signal experience?
- what is worth re-dyeing?
- what gets turned into children's clothing?

A city can have entire secondhand and repair economies.

Port especially should have:

- sailcloth reuse;
- imported secondhand garments;
- ship surplus;
- damaged luxury fabric cut down into smaller goods;
- immigrant tailoring traditions;
- rapid fashion copying.

---

# 9. Color and dye

Do not assign one palette per region.

Color depends on:

- dye plants / minerals;
- mordants;
- water;
- trade;
- cost;
- fading;
- guild control;
- sumptuary law if later established;
- fashion.

A common local dye may be bright.

An imported dull color may be expensive.

Color symbolism can vary by:

- city;
- faith;
- guild;
- political faction;
- mourning tradition;
- generation.

---

# 10. Pattern and decoration

Decoration can come from:

- weave structure;
- embroidery;
- appliqué;
- quilting;
- beadwork;
- metal fittings;
- painted cloth;
- printed / stamped methods if technology supports them;
- decorative repair;
- trim.

Motifs should emerge from actual institutions and histories.

Avoid assigning:

- waves to all Northwind;
- vines to all Greenvale;
- flames to all Ironcrest;
- leaves to all Deepwood;
- suns to all Sunplains.

Those can exist as specific guild, household, civic or religious symbols.

---

# 11. Class and status

Wealth can appear through:

- fabric quantity;
- fineness;
- labor-intensive construction;
- clean white cloth;
- difficult dyes;
- imported material;
- tailoring fit;
- embroidery;
- jewelry;
- spare garments;
- fashion turnover;
- ability to avoid visibly repaired clothing;
- ability to wear impractical garments.

But status varies by context.

A master craftsperson may display:

- excellent construction;
- rare tools;
- guild marks;

rather than courtly luxury.

A sailor may value a proven weather garment more than a fashionable coat.

---

# 12. Guilds and occupational identity

Guilds may influence clothing through:

- badges;
- seals;
- work standards;
- protective clothing;
- apprenticeship markers;
- rank;
- ceremonial dress;
- funeral clothing;
- issued equipment.

Do not make every guild uniformed.

A small marker can matter more than a full outfit.

# 13. Religion

The nine major traditions may affect clothing through local schools.

Possible effects:

- ritual garments;
- modesty norms;
- colors;
- mourning;
- initiation tokens;
- pilgrim clothing;
- hair practices;
- jewelry;
- prohibited materials.

Because faiths cross regions, religious clothing can create visual similarities between people from distant places.

Exact rules remain open.

# 14. Marriage, age and life stage

Clothing can mark:

- childhood;
- apprenticeship;
- adulthood;
- marriage;
- widowhood;
- parenthood;
- office;
- retirement;
- mourning.

Do not assume all communities visibly mark all stages.

Some may use jewelry, hair, belts, pins or name forms instead of garments.

# 15. Fashion

Fashion is not modernity.

Pre-industrial societies can still have rapid fashion change among people connected by:

- courts;
- markets;
- pilgrimage;
- guilds;
- cities;
- performers;
- trade.

## Fashion circulation

A plausible chain:

```text
Port performer / merchant
→ copied by Port youth
→ adopted by caravan merchants
→ appears in Cliffhaven / Summithold
→ imitated cheaply elsewhere
→ condemned as vulgar by older elites
→ later becomes respectable
```

This is exactly the kind of cultural circulation [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md) predicts.

---

# 16. Port

Port should be a major secondhand, tailoring and fashion center because it combines:

- imports;
- migrants;
- sailors;
- merchants;
- performers;
- guilds;
- wealth;
- poverty;
- damaged cargo;
- foreign cloth;
- fast imitation.

Port-born fashion should not simply equal “one item from every region.”

It should create new forms optimized for:

- city crowding;
- dock work;
- cosmopolitan display;
- rapid social signaling;
- mixed weather;
- access to imported material.

Some Port styles can later be copied elsewhere.

---

# 17. Border communities

Border clothing should arise from practical exchange.

Examples:

- a cut preferred in one area made from cloth traded from another;
- foreign boots adopted because they work better in mud;
- a wedding garment using one family's weave and another family's jewelry;
- bilingual guild insignia;
- a politically contested hat or sash;
- secondhand military clothing repurposed by civilians.

Do not create “half Ironcrest / half Greenvale costume.”

---

# 18. Region-level material pressures

These are pressures, not costumes.

### Ironcrest
- wool / linen-like cloth where available;
- leather;
- work protection;
- repair;
- guild production;
- imported dyes / luxury cloth;
- worker versus owner distinction.

### Northwind
- insulation;
- water / wind resistance;
- maritime workwear;
- leather / fur / wool where available;
- imported fine cloth concentrated in ports.

### Greenvale
- agricultural fibers;
- broad rural textile production;
- washable workwear;
- seasonal sun / rain protection;
- large internal differences between estate, village and city.

### Highridge
- layers;
- wool / felt;
- travel wear;
- dust / wind protection;
- imported textiles;
- caravan secondhand trade.

### Deepwood
- rain / humidity adaptation;
- forest fibers where plausible;
- leather / woven plant material;
- river trade;
- local dye / plant knowledge;
- strong variation between city, logging district, river town and remote community.

### Sunplains
- heat / sun adaptation;
- linen / cotton-like fibers where plausible;
- scarves / head coverings where locally useful;
- strong urban fashion competition;
- imported luxury cloth;
- city-specific styles.

### Port
- imported everything;
- local tailoring;
- secondhand markets;
- workwear;
- fashion churn;
- neighborhood variation.

- bedding;
- cooking vessels;
- storage boxes;
- lamps;
- stools / chairs;
- mats;
- tools;
- toys;
- mirrors;
- combs;
- washing basins;
- locks;
- keys;
- religious objects;
- writing materials;
- eating vessels;
- travel containers.

A household should reveal:

- work;
- class;
- family size;
- migration;
- religion;
- trade access;

without an explanatory paragraph.

---

# 20. Scene test

When a character enters a room, clothing and objects should let the reader infer some combination of:

- work;
- wealth;
- origin;
- neighborhood;
- faith;
- age;
- recent travel;
- institutional affiliation;
- personal taste.

But no outfit should answer all of those perfectly.

People borrow, inherit, lie, imitate and dress aspirationally.
