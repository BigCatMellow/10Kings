# Language and Thought

## Principle

Regional languages should influence **habits of attention**, not determine intelligence or personality.

The useful model is weak linguistic relativity: grammar and vocabulary can make speakers routinely notice distinctions that another language leaves optional.

Do not use corny occupational metaphors as the main marker of regional speech.

## Language families

**Working canon.** James decided on 2026-09-29 that the regional languages fall into **a few families**: languages in the same family are partly intelligible, languages across families are not, a trade tongue bridges them, and an older language survives in place names along The Spine. The language names below come from the project's earlier notes; the family names (Northern, Heartland) are working labels, **Provisional**.

| Family | Languages | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Northern** | **Northic** (Northwind); **Ironet** (Ironcrest) | the cold coasts and the ore uplands |
| **Heartland** | **Heartland Tongue** (Greenvale); the **Sunplains** city languages; Highridge's **plateau speech** | the lowlands, the south and the plateau |
| **Deepwood** | the **Deepwood languages** | the forest; unrelated to either family above |
| *(contact language)* | **Trade Tongue** | Port, the caravan routes, border markets |
| *(older substrate)* | **Old Spine speech** | survives only in place names |

**Mutual intelligibility.** Within a family, neighbors half-understand each other: a Northic speaker can follow the gist of slow Ironet, and Greenvale, Sunplains and plateau speakers catch much of each other's meaning, especially about prices, weather and food. Across families there is no understanding without learning. Deepwood languages are the hardest for outsiders, which reinforces Deepwood's reputation for closedness without making its people mysterious.

**Trade Tongue.** A simplified contact language that grew along the caravan routes and in Port, with pidgin and creole forms in different places. Words for money, shipping and contracts are heavily borrowed from Ironet; greetings and food words often come from Heartland Tongue or the Sunplains languages. Traders, caravan crews, dock workers and clerks speak it; many farmers and forest villagers don't. Port's own native speech is not simply Trade Tongue (see [Port](#port) below).

**Old Spine speech.** Before the present families spread, an older language was spoken along The Spine and its passes. Nobody speaks it now. It survives in place names, especially mountain, pass, spring and Underpass names that no living language can explain, and sometimes in names that each side of a border translates differently.

**Why Highridge sits in the Heartland family.** The earlier notes describe Highridge's speech as Heartland Tongue mixed with borrowed terms from every neighbor. Its trade registers (below) come from Trade Tongue and long contact, so plateau speech is the most mixed member of its family, and Highridge is highly multilingual in practice.

Still open: personal-name systems (see [Naming](Naming.md#personal-names)), the names of the Sunplains and Deepwood languages, and what Old Spine speech was called.

## Working structural differences

These are design directions, not finalized conlangs.

### Ironcrest languages

Possible recurring features:
- strong distinction between completed, inspected, and merely claimed work;
- grammatical or lexical precision around responsibility: who ordered, performed, witnessed, or guaranteed an action;
- compact forms useful in noisy work environments;
- extensive technical registers differing by craft and district.

Likely cognitive/social effect: people may habitually ask who is accountable and whether something is actually finished, certified, or only promised.

This should not make Ironcrest people inherently blunt.

### Northwind languages

Possible features:
- inclusive and exclusive forms of "we";
- evidential distinctions between personally seen, heard from a trusted person, and rumored information;
- rich spatial/deictic systems for seaward/landward, up-coast/down-coast, windward/leeward;
- future constructions that encode uncertainty or conditions.

Effect: speakers habitually clarify group membership, information source, and situational conditions — useful where survival depends on reliable reports and coordinated crews.

### Greenvale languages

Possible features:
- detailed aspect: beginning, ongoing, recurring, completed, interrupted, seasonal;
- possession structures distinguishing personal property from family/communal stewardship;
- calendrical vocabulary tied to agricultural and social cycles;
- grammatical habits emphasizing sequence and dependency.

Effect: speakers may naturally discuss plans as processes with stages and shared obligations rather than isolated actions.

### Highridge languages

Highridge is likely highly multilingual.

Common trade registers may feature:
- explicit markers of certainty and obligation;
- conditional constructions;
- standardized numbers, measures, dates, and contract vocabulary;
- ways to quote another speaker precisely and distinguish one's own claim from a reported claim.

Effect: habitual negotiation rewards explicit assumptions and degrees of commitment.

### Deepwood languages

Possible features:
- animate/inanimate or agency distinctions that place many plants, waterways, animals, and places in grammatically important categories;
- fine-grained vocabulary for land use, succession, forest age, water condition, and ecological change;
- evidentiality based on tracks, signs, direct sight, inherited knowledge, and inference.

Effect: speakers may habitually represent environmental entities as participants in decisions rather than passive background.

Do not turn this into "the language makes them spiritual."

### Sunplains languages

Possible features:
- developed politeness and status registers in older urban centers;
- explicit distinctions between public/formal and private/familiar address;
- strong rhetorical traditions tied to courts, assemblies, markets, and civic competition;
- grammatical or lexical tools for face-saving disagreement and indirect refusal.

Effect: speakers often attend closely to social positioning and the public consequences of words.

## Border generations

First-generation bilingual adults may code-switch: one register at home, another in market or law.

Second- and third-generation border populations often create stable mixed dialects. More importantly, they may combine habits:
- direct responsibility marking from Ironcrest + process/aspect distinctions from Greenvale;
- Northwind evidentiality + Highridge contractual conditionals;
- Deepwood ecological agency + Highridge precision about obligations.

This can produce people who are unusually effective mediators not because bilingualism makes them wiser, but because they have learned multiple conventions for what must be stated explicitly.

## Port

Port should contain:
- native Port speech;
- trade pidgins and creoles;
- heritage languages;
- guild jargons;
- religious registers;
- neighborhood dialects.

A third-generation Port family should not sound simply like diluted Northwind or Sunplains.
