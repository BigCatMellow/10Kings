# Food

## Status

**Working cultural owner.** Broad food ecology, preservation pressures, and interregional dependencies are established at the pattern level. Exact dishes, crop species, recipes, meal names, religious restrictions, and local etiquette remain provisional unless established elsewhere.

## Core principle

Cuisine follows:

```text
ecology
+ fuel
+ cookware
+ preservation
+ labor
+ trade
+ class
+ season
+ religion
+ migration
+ history
= cuisine
```

A region can have recognizable food families without having one menu, one flavor profile, or one “signature dish.”

## Naming rule

Food names follow the same grounded rule as place names.

Prefer names that arise naturally from:

- ingredient;
- method;
- vessel;
- place;
- family;
- maker;
- occasion;
- market;
- shape;
- practical nickname.

Avoid naming a dish to summarize a region's theme.

Legacy names such as “Forgefire Roast,” “Guardian Stew,” “Icy Caravan Curry,” or “Sunlit Orchard Couscous” remain design-history examples, not current canon.

# Source disposition

The legacy food volume contains useful material in four categories.

## Strong mechanisms retained

- smoking;
- drying;
- salting / brining;
- fermentation;
- pickling;
- cellar / cool storage;
- communal ovens;
- hearth or cauldron cooking;
- griddles / braziers;
- clay / earthenware vessels;
- pit cooking where local fuel / soil / plant material permit;
- portable caravan cooking;
- market food;
- food shaped around work breaks;
- imported foods becoming status goods;
- household and communal preservation.

## Retained only as local possibilities

- cooking with residual workshop heat;
- ice storage;
- leaf wrapping;
- stacked steaming;
- specialized clay ovens;
- public / communal cooking facilities.

These depend on local architecture, fuel, climate, plants, craft technology, and custom.

## Not promoted automatically

- direct copies of named modern cooking traditions;
- one flavor personality per region;
- “forge,” “frost,” “forest,” or “sun” themed dishes;
- one region-wide dining etiquette;
- one region-wide meal schedule;
- ingredients whose existence has not been established;
- modern-style sealed canning without a separate technology decision.

The source remains useful as research material, not a menu bible.

# 1. Food begins with fuel

The same ingredients produce different cuisines under different fuel constraints.

Possible fuels include:

- firewood;
- charcoal;
- crop residue;
- dung where pastoral systems support it;
- coal or similar mineral fuel in limited settings where cooking practice makes it tolerable;
- waste heat from workshops;
- portable braziers.

Fuel affects:

- whether people bake or boil;
- whether long simmering is cheap;
- whether communal ovens make sense;
- whether several households share one fire;
- whether food is cooked once per day or repeatedly;
- whether street vendors can sustain high heat;
- whether preservation relies more on drying, smoking, fermentation, or salt.

A cuisine should not receive a cooking method merely because it looks culturally appropriate.

---

# 2. Cookware

Cookware is an economic product.

Possible common forms include:

- ceramic pots;
- iron or bronze pots where affordable;
- griddles;
- baking stones;
- ovens;
- wooden vessels;
- baskets;
- spits;
- racks;
- mortars;
- knives;
- ladles;
- storage jars;
- barrels;
- fermentation crocks.

Material differences matter.

A poor household may own:

- one main pot;
- a griddle;
- a knife;
- a few bowls;

while a wealthy kitchen has many specialized vessels.

Iron cookware can connect ordinary food to Stone Hills metal production.

Ceramics can connect food to local clay, kilns, fuel, and pottery guilds.

---

# 3. Preservation

Preservation is one of the strongest common drivers of cuisine.

Methods can include:

- drying;
- salting;
- brining;
- smoking;
- fermentation;
- pickling;
- rendering fat;
- cool cellar storage;
- ice / snow storage where available;
- grain drying;
- fruit drying;
- cheese / cultured dairy;
- sealed jars only where the relevant technology is independently established.

Preservation infrastructure matters as much as household recipes.

Examples:

- smokehouses;
- drying racks;
- granaries;
- salt stores;
- cool cellars;
- cheese rooms;
- warehouse storage;
- communal ovens;
- fermentation rooms.

Failure of preservation can create a crisis even when production was adequate.

---

# 4. Meal timing follows work

Do not impose modern breakfast / lunch / dinner everywhere.

Useful patterns may include:

- light food before field labor;
- large midday meal when heat stops work;
- portable food during mine / forge / road work;
- tide-timed meals in harbor communities;
- late meals after markets close;
- caravan food eaten whenever animals and routes permit;
- one cooked household meal plus cold or reheated food at other times.

Exact meal structure should be local.

## Work food

Work meals should answer:

- can it be carried?
- does it keep?
- can it be eaten quickly?
- does it require utensils?
- is it cheap?
- is it filling?
- can it be bought near the workplace?

This gives street food and worker food a material reason to exist.

---

# 5. Class

Food reveals class through more than meat versus no meat.

Wealth can buy:

- fresh food out of season;
- imported ingredients;
- fine flour;
- labor-intensive dishes;
- more fuel;
- better cookware;
- larger storage;
- reliable salt;
- sugar / sweeteners if scarce;
- spices;
- frequent meat;
- servants / cooks;
- dining space.

Poor households may still eat excellent food when:

- local staples are strong;
- labor is skilled;
- ingredients are seasonal;
- households cooperate.

Luxury and taste are not identical.

---

# 6. Household labor

Food requires labor:

- fetching water;
- fuel;
- milling;
- grinding;
- baking;
- preserving;
- brewing;
- washing;
- animal care;
- shopping;
- carrying;
- storage management.

Who performs that labor should be determined locally through:

- household structure;
- class;
- age;
- occupation;
- gender where later established;
- servants;
- apprentices;
- market access.

Do not silently assign all food labor to women or to one household member without a local social model.

---

# 7. Markets and street food

Urban food depends on:

- market schedules;
- fuel access;
- water;
- regulation;
- rent;
- transport;
- guilds;
- spoilage.

Street food should often be:

- portable;
- cooked quickly;
- made from inexpensive ingredients;
- suited to workers / travelers;
- sold near markets, docks, gates, workshops, caravan yards, courts, or festivals.

Port and High Roads should have especially diverse market-food systems because of repeated travelers.

---

# 8. Hospitality

Hospitality can be visible through food without requiring one universal “guest dish.”

Questions:

- Is water offered first?
- Is bread / staple food expected?
- Is refusing food rude?
- Does accepting food create obligation?
- Who receives the best portion?
- How long must a traveler be fed?
- Does a guild credential change hospitality?
- Are strangers fed at the household table or elsewhere?
- What happens during shortage?

Hospitality becomes politically meaningful during crisis when households can no longer afford older expectations.

---

# 9. Religion

The nine major traditions can influence food through local schools rather than one world-wide dietary code.

Possible mechanisms:

- fasting;
- feast days;
- prohibited animals / preparation methods;
- alcohol rules;
- ritual sharing;
- charity kitchens;
- burial meals;
- pilgrimage food;
- monastery / temple production;
- food distributed to the poor.

No exact rule is established merely because a faith's theology seems to suggest one.

---

# 10. Children

Children should have ordinary food.

Possible distinctions:

- softer food for very young children;
- portable school / apprenticeship food;
- cheap snacks;
- treats associated with markets or festivals;
- foods adults dislike but children seek;
- foods children are expected to grow into.

Do not make every child eat miniature adult ceremonial cuisine.

---

# 11. Travel food

Travel food emphasizes:

- durability;
- weight;
- water use;
- preparation time;
- fuel;
- packability.

Caravan, ship, pilgrim, soldier, and migrant foods can become influential because travelers introduce them elsewhere.

Food circulation is therefore part of cultural circulation.

---

# 12. Stone Hills

## Established anchors

Current owners support:

- hardy grains;
- roots;
- legumes;
- dairy;
- upland / valley livestock;
- lower-valley orchard goods;
- mushrooms;
- substantial imported Low Rivers staples in industrial districts;
- dense mining / craft settlements;
- dangerous labor;
- metal cookware and tools.

## Working culinary directions

Likely recurring pressures:

- portable worker food;
- thick soups / porridges;
- breads;
- pickled vegetables;
- cheese;
- preserved meat;
- filling combinations of grain + legumes + fat;
- market food near workshops;
- strong use of leftovers.

### Local technique candidates

Legacy source supports as plausible local practices:

- cauldron / hearth simmering;
- griddle cooking;
- smoking;
- cooking near workshop heat.

But workshop cooking is occupational, not a regional sacred tradition.

### Class difference

Industrial districts can import large amounts of food while upland farming communities eat much more locally.

Mine owner, skilled guild worker, casual laborer, herder, and valley farmer should not share one “Stone Hills diet.”

---

# 13. North Coast

## Established anchors

Current owners support:

- fish / shellfish;
- sea greens;
- hardy grains;
- roots;
- berries;
- dairy in pastoral districts;
- preserved meat;
- imported grain and warmer-climate foods;
- strong seasonality;
- maritime risk.

## Working culinary directions

Preservation is especially important:

- drying;
- salting;
- brining;
- smoking;
- fermentation;
- cold storage where climate allows.

### Infrastructure

Likely important:

- smokehouses;
- drying racks;
- barrels;
- cool stores;
- fish markets;
- communal work yards.

### Variation

A wealthy major port can have imported flour, oil, fruit, wine, spices, and foreign cooks.

A remote island household may eat a much narrower seasonal diet.

North Coast should therefore not equal “fish every meal.”

# 14. Low Rivers

## Established anchors

Current owners support the broadest agricultural base:

- grains;
- legumes;
- vegetables;
- dairy;
- eggs;
- livestock;
- orchard fruit;
- herbs;
- mills;
- storage;
- market towns;
- river transport.

## Working culinary directions

This region should have **many local cuisines**, not one abundance cuisine.

Differences can follow:

- river basin;
- estate;
- soil;
- livestock;
- market access;
- religion;
- tenancy;
- floodplain versus upland edge.

### Infrastructure

Strong candidates:

- mills;
- granaries;
- ovens;
- cellars;
- dairying facilities;
- fermentation;
- shared village baking where locally useful.

### Legacy correction

Communal ovens are plausible.

Modern-style “canning days” are **not established** and should not be promoted without compatible sealing technology.

# 15. High Roads

## Established anchors

Current owners support:

- hardy grains;
- legumes;
- dairy;
- sheep / goat / other upland livestock;
- dried meat;
- roots;
- imported goods;
- caravan yards;
- multilingual markets;
- fuel constraints in exposed areas.

## Working culinary directions

Separate:

### Local household food
Likely conservative and dependent on locally reliable staples.

### Traveler / market food
More mixed, portable, and influenced by trade.

### Elite merchant food
Can use imported spices, fruit, wine, fish, fine flour, and specialty goods.

### Technique candidates

Legacy source suggests plausible mechanisms:

- portable braziers;
- heavy pots;
- quick flatbreads;
- dumpling-like or filled portable foods;
- stacked / efficient cooking where fuel is expensive.

Do not copy a specific real-world oven or cuisine automatically.

# 16. Longwood

## Established anchors

Current owners support:

- cultivated food in clearings;
- grains / tubers depending on district;
- nuts;
- mushrooms;
- greens;
- fish;
- game;
- domestic animals;
- berries;
- honey;
- imported grain and salt.

Foraging **supplements** agriculture rather than replacing it.

## Working culinary directions

Cuisine should vary between:

- river towns;
- market cities;
- managed woodland;
- remote settlements;
- logging districts;
- agricultural clearings.

### Technique candidates

Plausible local methods include:

- earthenware cooking;
- smoking;
- drying;
- fermentation;
- leaf wrapping where suitable plants exist;
- pit / ash cooking where fuel and soil make it useful.

These methods should arise from practical conditions, not “forest spirituality.”

## Established anchors

Current owners support:

- grains;
- pulses;

- nuts;
- oils;
- dairy;
- sheep / goat / poultry;
- coastal fish;
- dried fruit;
- irrigated agriculture;
- city markets;
- strong civic variation.

## Working culinary directions

Old Cities should probably have some of the world's strongest **city-specific cuisines**.

Differences can follow:

- water source;
- port access;
- religious rule;
- merchant connections;
- local crops;
- patronage;
- class;
- rivalry.

### Technique candidates

Plausible mechanisms include:

- earthenware slow cooking;
- flatbreads;
- ovens;
- braziers;
- grilling;
- frying;
- drying fruit;
- oil-based preservation where appropriate.

Do not promote tagines, couscous, specific tea ritual, or Mediterranean ingredients simply because they match the inspiration pool.

# 18. Port

Port cuisine should develop its own history.

Important food worlds may include:

- dockworker food;
- ship provisioning;
- warehouse / market food;
- immigrant household food;
- religious kitchens;
- merchant banquets;
- cheap boarding-house meals;
- street food;
- refugee food;
- second-generation Port dishes.

Port is not “fusion cuisine.”

Its food should contain forms that Port-born people consider ordinary even when outsiders can identify multiple historical sources.

## Port pressures

- food arrives through trade;
- rents are high;
- kitchens may be small;
- many workers eat away from home;
- shift work disrupts household meals;
- imported condiments circulate quickly;
- cheap food businesses can become culturally important.

# 19. Cultural crossover and contact cuisines

When food traditions meet repeatedly, the result should not remain two separate cuisines sitting beside one another.

Long contact can produce a **third local tradition**.

A useful model is:

```text
Technique A
+ ingredient / preservation system B
+ local fuel / cookware
+ repeated intermarriage / neighborhood contact
+ ordinary household repetition
→ new local food
```

For example:

- a North Coast tradition of salted fish meets a Low Rivers supply of fresh vegetables, herbs and pickling produce;
- households begin combining preserved fish with fresh or pickled vegetables because the ingredients are cheap and available together;
- the combination becomes common in one market town or mixed neighborhood;
- children grow up treating it as ordinary local food;
- later generations may no longer think of it as “North Coast + Low Rivers.”

That is **cultural bleed becoming culture**.

## Contact-zone rule

Crossover foods should emerge especially in:

- border towns;
- mixed cities;
- Port neighborhoods;
- caravan settlements;
- migrant farming districts;
- military settlements;
- pilgrimage centers;
- intermarried households;
- guild communities that recruit across cultural traditions.

## What can cross over

Not only ingredients.

Cultural bleed can transfer:

- preservation methods;
- cooking fats;
- souring agents;
- bread forms;
- spice use;
- meal timing;
- serving vessels;
- etiquette;
- feast customs;
- work food;
- market-food formats;
- ideas about what counts as breakfast, guest food, children's food, or festival food.

A borrowed **technique** can matter more than a borrowed ingredient.

## Generational effect

Contact cuisine becomes more convincing when it changes over generations:

```text
first generation: "their food with our ingredient"
second generation: familiar mixed household version
third generation: ordinary local dish
fourth generation: argument over the "proper" version
```

By that point, the food may be culturally distinct from both source traditions.

## Avoid arithmetic fusion

Do not design crossover cuisine as:

> one North Coast ingredient + one Low Rivers ingredient = border dish.

The food needs a reason to become stable:

- price;
- availability;
- preservation;
- work schedule;
- marriage;
- migration;
- shared kitchens;
- market demand;
- religious compatibility;
- local cookware.

The goal is **historical synthesis**, not a themed mashup.

# 20. Border food

Border cuisine should not be “half A + half B.”

A border dish should have a causal history:

```text
available staple
+ imported ingredient
+ borrowed technique
+ local fuel / cookware
+ repeated intermarriage / trade
→ stable local food
```

After generations, locals may reject both neighboring regions' versions as “wrong.”

# 21. Current crises

Food should make current events tangible.

## Stone Hills labor unrest

Possible effects:

- cheaper worker meals;
- guild kitchens;
- debt at food shops;
- imported grain becoming political;
- employers providing meals as compensation.

## North Coast fish pressure

Possible effects:

- preserved stocks rise in value;
- poorer households substitute grain / dairy / roots;
- imported food demand rises;
- fishing-right disputes reach the table.

## Low Rivers abundance crisis

Possible effects:

- food can be plentiful while farms fail financially;
- cheap grain can coexist with debt;
- oversupply changes preservation and storage decisions;
- distressed households sell animals / land despite full markets.

## High Roads caravan attacks

Possible effects:

- imported spices / fruit / salt change price;
- inns simplify menus;
- caravan provisioning becomes expensive;
- substitute routes spread different foods.

## Longwood extraction conflict

Possible effects:

- access to hunting / gathering areas changes;
- logging can damage watersheds or local food species;
- road construction can increase imported food while reducing local autonomy.

## Old Cities drought anxiety

Possible effects:

- water-intensive crops become political;
- urban food prices shift before actual famine;
- irrigation disputes change which foods reach markets.

## Port instability

Possible effects:

- shipping delays alter fresh versus preserved food;
- refugee neighborhoods introduce new foods;
- credit problems close food stalls;
- warehouse losses produce sudden local shortages.

# 22. Scene test

A food scene should answer some of these without exposition:

- What is cheap today?
- What is expensive?
- What had to be preserved?
- What fuel cooked it?
- Who did the labor?
- Is this household eating at home?
- Is the food local or imported?
- Is the diner working, traveling, celebrating, mourning, fasting, or hosting?
- What would a richer household add?
- What would a poorer household substitute?
- What does a child want instead?
- What has changed since the current crisis began?

If the only thing the meal communicates is “this is North Coast food,” it is too shallow.
