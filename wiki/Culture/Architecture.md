# Architecture

## Status

**Working cultural owner.** The material and historical logic is established at the design level; exact building traditions, neighborhood plans, civic codes, construction guilds, and named monuments remain provisional unless established elsewhere.

## Core principle

Architecture is **accumulated adaptation**.

A building should emerge from:

```text
site
+ climate
+ materials
+ labor
+ ownership
+ water
+ waste
+ fire
+ transport
+ technology
+ class
+ law
+ rebuilding history
= built environment
```

No major culture has one house type.

No political border marks the point where one building style stops and another begins.

Building knowledge moves with:

- craftspeople;
- migrants;
- guilds;
- military occupation;
- merchants;
- religious institutions;
- marriage;
- rebuilding after disasters.

The same construction technique can therefore appear in several regions for different reasons.

# Source disposition

The legacy architecture volume contains useful material in three main categories.

## Strong mechanisms retained

- stone foundations and retaining walls;
- timber framing;
- earth / brick construction;
- raised floors in flood-prone areas;
- courtyards;
- roof terraces where climate and structure permit;
- steep roofs where snow / rain demand them;
- compact forms in exposed or cold conditions;
- windbreaks;
- cisterns;
- wells;
- canals;
- warehouses;
- smokehouses;
- caravan yards;
- mixed workshop / residential buildings;
- communal storage;
- attached animal yards;
- ventilation for industrial buildings;
- street width shaped by transport and fire;
- buildings changing by city / town / isolated household.

## Retained only as local possibilities

- extensive rooftop work areas;
- buildings partly cut into slopes;
- large communal courtyards;
- public fountains;
- covered markets;
- longhouse-type communal buildings;
- raised walkways;
- specialized harbor engineering.

Each requires a local material, climate, political, and economic cause.

## Not promoted automatically

- one visual style for each cultural core;
- “industrial fortress” as the Stone Hills look;
- “Scandinavian coastal” as the North Coast look;
- “communal agrarian” as the Low Rivers look;
- “Tibetan/Andean caravan” as the High Roads look;
- “forest-integrated tribal” as the Longwood look;
- “Moorish/Mediterranean” as the Old Cities look;
- decorative motifs chosen mainly to signal regional theme;
- direct copies of one Earth architecture;
- geothermal infrastructure unless independently established;
- large glass use without cost / production support;
- industrial-scale metal framing inconsistent with pre-industrial craft limits.

Legacy images and descriptions remain research material, not architectural canon.

# 1. Site comes first

Before designing a settlement or building, ask:

1. Why is it here?
2. Where does water come from?
3. What floods?
4. What burns?
5. What freezes?
6. What slides, collapses, erodes, or blows away?
7. What goods need to enter and leave?
8. What was here before the current settlement?
9. Who owns the ground?
10. What is expensive to move?

A settlement built at a ford grows differently from one built around:

- a mine;
- a shrine;
- a harbor;
- a market;
- a pass;
- an irrigation gate;
- a fort;
- a mill;
- a warehouse district.

The reason a place exists should shape its streets.

---

# 2. Materials and distance

Pre-industrial building materials are expensive to move.

That makes proximity important.

Common materials may include:

- fieldstone;
- quarried stone;
- timber;
- earth;
- clay brick;
- fired brick;
- tile;
- thatch;
- reeds;
- lime / plaster;
- rope;
- hides;
- simple glass where affordable;
- iron fittings;
- reused masonry;
- salvaged ship / workshop timber.

## Local versus imported

Imported material should usually signal one of:

- wealth;
- strategic need;
- unusual durability;
- prestige;
- reconstruction after disaster;
- strong transport access.

A poor house should not casually use material hauled hundreds of miles unless there is a clear trade reason.

## Reuse

Old buildings are quarries.

Stone, beams, tiles, doors, hinges, bricks, paving, and foundations are reused.

A city can physically contain political history because later buildings sit on or inside earlier ones.

---

# 3. Construction labor

Buildings require people.

Possible builders include:

- household labor;
- village work groups;
- specialist masons;
- carpenters;
- guild crews;
- estate labor;
- military engineers;
- religious builders;
- hired migrants;
- coerced labor where politically relevant.

Large civic projects should raise questions:

- who pays?
- who supplies food?
- who supplies timber / stone?
- who loses land?
- who maintains it later?

A monument is also a labor and tax story.

---

# 4. Property and land value

Architecture changes when land becomes expensive.

## Low land value

Possible results:
- detached compounds;
- yards;
- gardens;
- animal pens;
- horizontal expansion.

## High land value

Possible results:
- shared walls;
- vertical additions;
- subdivided houses;
- narrow lots;
- rented rooms;
- workshops beneath living space;
- crowded courtyards;
- buildings extended over older structures.

Port should display this strongly.

So should any major market city.

## Property history

A building can be shaped by:

- inheritance;
- subdivision;
- marriage;
- debt;
- guild ownership;
- religious ownership;
- landlord investment;
- rebuilding law.

An awkward floor plan can be more believable than a perfect one.

---

# 5. Water

Every settlement needs a water system.

Possible sources:

- river;
- spring;
- well;
- cistern;
- canal;
- aqueduct-like gravity system where engineering supports it;
- rain capture;
- carried water;
- snow / ice melt.

## Water access is social

Ask:

- who owns the well?
- who maintains the canal?
- who draws first during shortage?
- how far do poor households carry water?
- can industries pollute drinking water?
- which buildings have private cisterns?
- does a wealthy household buy water rather than carry it?

Water infrastructure can create class distinctions without requiring ornate architecture.

---

# 6. Waste and drainage

Waste should not disappear.

Settlements produce:

- human waste;
- animal waste;
- ash;
- food waste;
- tannery waste;
- slaughter waste;
- industrial slag;
- wastewater;
- broken pottery;
- garbage.

Possible systems:

- pits / privies;
- night-soil collection;
- drains;
- open gutters;
- cesspits;
- river disposal;
- manure reuse;
- ash collection;
- organized cart removal.

Different districts can have radically different sanitation.

A wealthy center can be clean because its waste is pushed into a poorer district.

---

# 7. Fire

Fire is one of the strongest urban design forces.

Risks include:

- timber buildings;
- thatch;
- workshops;
- kilns;
- ovens;
- warehouses;
- lamps;
- dense housing;
- wind.

Possible responses:

- masonry firebreaks;
- regulated ovens / kilns;
- wider market streets;
- water barrels;
- neighborhood watches;
- bans on some trades in dense districts;
- rebuilding codes after major fires.

A city that has burned repeatedly should look different from one that has not.

Fire history is architecture.

---

# 8. Streets and movement

Street form follows:

- terrain;
- older property lines;
- carts;
- pack animals;
- foot traffic;
- drainage;
- defense;
- markets;
- later widening.

## Goods matter

A mining town needs to move:
- ore;
- fuel;
- timber;
- tools.

A river market moves:
- grain;
- animals;
- barrels;
- carts.

A caravan town moves:
- pack animals;
- wagons where routes allow;
- feed;
- baggage.

A harbor moves:
- cargo;
- fish;
- rope;
- barrels;
- carts;
- ship supplies.

The width and paving of a street should have a reason.

---

# 9. Building age and layers

A believable city is not built all at once.

Possible layers:

```text
old settlement
→ wall / fort
→ market expansion
→ fire / flood / conquest
→ rebuilding
→ new road
→ immigrant quarter
→ guild district
→ subdivision
→ wealthy redevelopment
→ poor rooftop / yard additions
```

The result can contain:

- blocked doors;
- reused walls;
- buried roads;
- mismatched floor levels;
- obsolete gates;
- old wells inside later buildings;
- houses built against former fortifications.

This matters more than a unified style guide.

---

# 10. Housing and class

## Poor households

Possible features:
- rented rooms;
- shared kitchens;
- shared privies;
- little storage;
- poor light;
- overcrowding;
- repair using salvaged material;
- dangerous proximity to work.

## Middling households

Possible features:
- mixed home / shop;
- one or more private rooms;
- secure storage;
- yard or shared court;
- apprentices / boarders;
- some specialized furniture.

## Wealthy households

Possible features:
- more space;
- better water access;
- separation of dirty / clean work;
- guest rooms;
- storage;
- servants;
- imported material;
- private court / garden;
- multiple kitchens or work areas.

Wealth should often be visible through **space and privacy**, not just decoration.

# 11. Household architecture

Housing affects social life.

Ask:

- who sleeps together?
- where are guests placed?
- where does cooking happen?
- where is food stored?
- where are tools kept?
- where do animals go?
- can household members be alone?
- is work done inside the home?
- do apprentices / servants / boarders live on site?

This links directly to [Daily Life and Customs](Daily-Life-and-Customs.md).

# 12. Food architecture

Food requires buildings and space.

Possible infrastructure:

- ovens;
- mills;
- smokehouses;
- granaries;
- cellars;
- drying lofts;
- fermentation rooms;
- dairies;
- market stalls;
- slaughter yards;
- public kitchens;
- warehouses;
- wells;
- cisterns.

A cuisine cannot be separated from the built environment that makes preservation and cooking possible.

# 13. Work buildings

## Workshops

Need:
- light;
- ventilation;
- fuel;
- storage;
- loading access;
- water where required.

## Industrial craft buildings

Forge, kiln, tannery, dye works, brewery, mill, slaughter, pottery, and similar trades create different hazards.

That often shapes zoning before anyone calls it zoning.

## Guild halls

May combine:
- offices;
- court / meeting space;
- archives;
- training;
- welfare distribution;
- storage;
- ceremony.

A guild hall should reflect what the guild actually does.

# 14. Public buildings

Useful civic structures can include:

- market halls;
- courts;
- guild halls;
- meeting houses;
- religious compounds;
- baths where locally supported;
- schools;
- warehouses;
- bridges;
- gates;
- toll houses;
- caravan yards;
- dock offices;
- granaries.

Prestige can be shown through:
- permanence;
- scale;
- difficult materials;
- location;
- controlled access;

not merely towers and ornament.

---

# 15. Fortification

Fortification follows actual threats.

Possible forms:
- walls;
- palisades;
- fortified gates;
- towers;
- earthworks;
- defended bridges;
- harbor chains / works where technology permits;
- hill forts;
- refuge compounds.

Not every city needs walls.

Old walls can become:
- property lines;
- housing foundations;
- roads;
- markets;
- symbols of obsolete political boundaries.

---

# 16. Climate adaptation

Climate creates constraints, not one style.

## Cold / exposed
Possible responses:
- compact form;
- insulation;
- smaller openings;
- vestibules;
- steep roofs where snow requires them;
- shared walls;
- protected yards.

## Hot / dry
Possible responses:
- shade;
- courtyards;
- thick walls;
- small exposed openings;
- roof terraces;
- water storage;
- narrow shaded streets.

## Wet / flood-prone
Possible responses:
- raised floors;
- drainage;
- sacrificial lower storage;
- bridges / walkways;
- rot-resistant detailing;
- steep roofs.

## Wind
Possible responses:
- orientation;
- lower profiles;
- windbreak walls;
- protected courts;
- strong roof fastening.

Several of these solutions can appear in several regions.

---

# 17. Cultural traditions travel

Architecture is part of cultural migration.

A migrant community may initially reproduce:

- room arrangements;
- courtyard use;
- roof form;
- religious building layout;
- construction rituals;
- privacy customs.

Then local constraints change them.

A Longwood-descended community living in another forest may preserve some building knowledge but adopt:
- different timber;
- local foundations;
- neighboring roof technology;
- new fire rules.

A North Coast family living in Port may preserve:
- interior household layout;
- storage practice;
- storm shutters;

while occupying a completely Port-built structure.

This is more realistic than assigning a full regional style wherever the people go.

# 18. Stone Hills core

Historical pressures may often include:

- steep or uneven ground;
- stone access;
- mines;
- workshops;
- fuel storage;
- retaining walls;
- worker housing;
- polluted / dangerous industrial districts.

Possible recurring solutions:
- stone / earth lower walls;
- timber upper additions where economical;
- strong ventilation around hot trades;
- mixed home / workshop buildings;
- heavy goods routes;
- yards for tools / animals;
- reused industrial structures.

Do not turn the entire core into a fortress aesthetic.

Farms, river villages, merchant towns, and wealthy residential districts should differ sharply.

---

# 19. North Coast core

Historical pressures may often include:

- wind;
- cold;
- rain / snow;
- salt exposure;
- harbors;
- smokehouses;
- boat repair;
- seasonal crowding.

Possible recurring solutions:
- protected coves / orientations;
- stone footings;
- timber where available;
- insulation;
- secure roofs;
- compact settlements;
- storage / drying / boat yards.

Inland North Coast communities may have very different architecture.

---

# 20. Low Rivers core

Historical pressures may often include:

- flood risk;
- river transport;
- farms;
- mills;
- granaries;
- estates;
- market towns.

Possible recurring solutions:
- raised floors or flood-tolerant lower spaces in some districts;
- courtyards / work yards;
- farm compounds;
- river quays;
- granaries;
- mills;
- drainage;
- bridges / ferries.

Do not make every settlement a pastoral village.

Some of the largest commercial storage cities can be here.

---

# 21. High Roads core

Historical pressures may often include:

- slope;
- wind;
- cold nights;
- caravan traffic;
- pack animals;
- lodging;
- water scarcity in some high districts;
- retaining walls.

Possible recurring solutions:
- terracing;
- compact stone / earth construction;
- cisterns;
- caravan courts;
- stables;
- warehouses;
- protected yards;
- route-side inns;
- repair districts.

A remote herding village does not need to look like a caravan capital.

---

# 22. Longwood core

Historical pressures may often include:

- rain;
- humidity;
- timber;
- insects;
- rivers;
- seasonal flooding;
- forest management;
- rot.

Possible recurring solutions:
- raised floors where useful;
- ventilation;
- steep roofs;
- covered work areas;
- timber / clay combinations;
- river-oriented settlement;
- drainage;
- detached work shelters.

Do not make every settlement visually disappear into the forest.

Longwood contains cities, roads, farms, warehouses, religious compounds, and poor neighborhoods.

---

# 23. Old Cities core

Historical pressures may often include:

- heat;
- water management;
- dense urbanism;
- irrigation;
- old walls;
- political fragmentation;
- merchant wealth.

Possible recurring solutions:
- courtyards;
- shade;
- thick earth / masonry walls;
- cisterns;
- roof terraces where useful;
- narrow shaded streets;
- arcades / covered markets where supported;
- garden compounds among the wealthy.

Each old city-state should have its own building history.

The category **Old Cities** should not become one architectural style.

---

# 24. Port

Port should be architecturally distinct because of **urban history**, not because it mixes one building from every region.

Key pressures:
- high land value;
- docks;
- warehouses;
- fire;
- migration;
- rebuilding;
- legal pluralism;
- imported material;
- crowding;
- vertical additions;
- unstable employment;
- neighborhood specialization.

Port architecture should show:
- old harbor infrastructure;
- warehouses converted to housing / workshops;
- foreign institutions altered to local codes;
- shared walls;
- boarding houses;
- courts;
- alleys;
- dock yards;
- repeated fire / storm rebuilding.

A building can begin as a North Coast merchant warehouse and become a Port tenement three generations later.

That is cultural layering.

---

# 25. Border and mixed settlements

Border architecture is **adaptive transmission**, not decorative fusion.

A mixed town may combine:
- local foundations;
- migrant room arrangements;
- imported roof practice;
- neighboring drainage;
- guild-standard workshop dimensions;
- religious building traditions;
- later fire codes.

Do not describe it as “half Stone Hills / half Low Rivers.”

Trace the actual building decisions.

# 26. Architecture and current crises

## Stone Hills labor unrest
Possible effects:
- deferred repairs;
- crowded worker housing;
- unsafe workshops;
- guild-funded boarding;
- owners improving prestige districts while labor districts deteriorate.

## North Coast fish pressure
Possible effects:
- empty smokehouses;
- boats / sheds repurposed;
- harbor families take boarders;
- maintenance deferred.

## Low Rivers abundance crisis
Possible effects:
- full granaries beside indebted farms;
- warehouses controlling prices;
- abandoned tenant houses;
- estate consolidation changing settlement pattern.

## High Roads caravan attacks
Possible effects:
- inns empty;
- gates / security added;
- caravan yards fortified;
- warehouses accumulate unsold goods;
- alternate roads create new settlements.

## Longwood extraction conflict
Possible effects:
- logging roads;
- worker camps;
- river damage;
- new bridges;
- old rights physically bypassed by new infrastructure.

## Old Cities drought anxiety
Possible effects:
- private cistern politics;
- dry fountains;
- canal guards;
- water-rich elite compounds becoming controversial;
- building restrictions tied to water use.

## Port instability
Possible effects:
- warehouse vacancies;
- refugee overcrowding;
- improvised subdivisions;
- fire risk;
- rent spikes;
- food stalls / workshops occupying residential space.

# 27. Settlement test

Every important settlement should answer:

1. Why is it here?
2. Where does water come from?
3. Where does waste go?
4. What burns?
5. What floods?
6. Where is food stored?
7. Where do animals go?
8. How do heavy goods move?
9. What does poor housing look like?
10. What does wealthy housing look like?
11. What is older than the current government?
12. What has been rebuilt?
13. Who owns the ground?
14. Which building tradition came from migrants?
15. What part of the settlement is currently changing fastest?

If the answer is mainly “because this is the Stone Hills style,” the architecture is too shallow.
