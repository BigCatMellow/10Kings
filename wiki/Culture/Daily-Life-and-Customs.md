# Daily Life and Customs

## Status

**Working cultural framework; specific customs remain provisional unless established elsewhere.**

This page owns the setting's everyday social logic: households, life stages, visiting, hospitality, care, courtship, mourning, neighbor obligations, daily rhythm, etiquette, and generational change.

## Core rule

There is no single “Stone Hills custom,” “North Coast marriage,” or “Old Cities etiquette.”

Daily life emerges from overlapping pressures:

```text
household
+ class
+ occupation
+ faith
+ neighborhood
+ polity
+ region
+ generation
+ migration history
+ material conditions
= lived custom
```

Regional patterns can be recognizable without being universal.

A wealthy merchant household, a tenant household, a guild workshop household, and a migrant boarding house in the same city should not live identically.

## Source disposition

Older culture notes support the existence of:

- market-day social life;
- coming-of-age traditions tied to new responsibilities;
- guild initiations;
- communal meals and seasonal gatherings;
- occupational rites;
- naming traditions in some communities;
- hospitality and barter among some itinerant groups;
- regional memories being used to justify modern customs.

Those sources do **not** establish one universal marriage system, child-rearing model, funeral tradition, hospitality code, or daily schedule for each region.

Those remain open and should be built from the current world rather than silently inferred.

# 1. Household

A household is not automatically a nuclear family.

Plausible household forms include:

- parents and children;
- multigenerational kin;
- siblings and their families;
- apprentices living with a master;
- unrelated workers sharing rooms;
- widowed relatives;
- fostered children;
- boarders;
- servants;
- guild housing;
- religious households;
- seasonal labor households;
- ship or caravan crews functioning as temporary households.

## Household questions

For any community, ask:

1. Who is normally expected to live together?
2. Who cooks?
3. Who controls household money?
4. Who owns or rents the dwelling?
5. Who cares for children?
6. Who cares for elderly or injured people?
7. Who inherits tools, land, debt, contracts, animals, or business rights?
8. What happens after death, divorce, migration, or bankruptcy?
9. Which household tasks are visible and which are socially ignored?
10. What kind of overcrowding is normal for poor people?

Do not answer all ten identically across a region.

---

# 2. Work and the day

The time structure of ordinary life depends more on work than on abstract culture.

Possible rhythms include:

- daylight agriculture;
- tide-dependent harbor work;
- furnace shifts;
- market schedules;
- caravan departures;
- irrigation turns;
- religious observance;
- seasonal preservation;
- night watches;
- school / apprenticeship schedules.

## Work is social

Workplaces also provide:

- gossip;
- meals;
- childcare;
- introductions;
- courtship opportunities;
- informal credit;
- political discussion;
- news;
- social rank.

A workshop, dock, market stall, mill, caravan yard, kitchen, field, shrine or guild hall can be as culturally important as a palace.

---

# 3. Childhood

Children should exist inside the economy and social world without being written as miniature adults.

Useful distinctions:

- very young children near household work;
- older children given errands and supervised tasks;
- apprentices or students entering more formal training;
- children of elites receiving literacy, languages or political grooming;
- children of mobile families learning routes and practical skills;
- children in poor households contributing labor earlier.

## Childhood is not uniform

A dockworker's child in Port, a herder's child in High Roads, a farm child in Low Rivers and a clerk's child in Summithold may all have different relationships to:

- work;
- play;
- schooling;
- independence;
- danger;
- adults;
- siblings;
- public space.

## Coming of age

Legacy notes repeatedly proposed profession-based trials.

The useful principle is broader:

**adulthood should be marked by a change in recognized responsibility, not necessarily one universal ceremony.**

Possible markers include:

- completing an apprenticeship stage;
- first independent voyage;
- first legal contract;
- inheritance of a household duty;
- religious initiation;
- military or civic service;
- managing a field / flock / shop;
- being allowed to testify, vote, marry, own tools or join a guild.

Exact ages and rites remain open.

# 4. Courtship, marriage and partnership

No world-wide system is established.

Marriage can involve several institutions at once:

- household;
- lineage;
- property;
- religion;
- guild;
- polity;
- inheritance;
- migration rights.

## Questions to answer locally

- Who can marry whom?
- Is marriage primarily household, religious, legal, or several at once?
- Who must consent?
- Is bridewealth, dowry, shared property, service, or no transfer expected?
- Can partners keep separate property?
- What happens to names?
- Can people divorce?
- What happens to children after separation?
- Do guilds or noble houses regulate marriage?
- How are mixed-faith or mixed-polity marriages handled?

## Courtship

Courtship can happen through:

- work;
- family introductions;
- religious communities;
- markets;
- festivals;
- guilds;
- travel;
- neighborhood life;
- correspondence.

Do not make every culture use elaborate formal courtship.

---

# 5. Friendship and informal belonging

Friendship should have practical consequences.

Friends may provide:

- introductions;
- childcare;
- food;
- lodging;
- job referrals;
- loans;
- alibis;
- burial support;
- travel help;
- political information.

Some friendships cross institutions more easily than formal alliances do.

This is one way [Social Fabric](../Social-Fabric.md) becomes visible at human scale.

---

# 6. Hospitality and visiting

Hospitality should vary according to risk, scarcity and social expectation.

Useful distinctions:

- invited guest;
- traveler asking shelter;
- guild member carrying credentials;
- pilgrim;
- family visitor;
- political envoy;
- stranger with no sponsor;
- refugee;
- rival-region merchant.

Hospitality can involve:

- food;
- water;
- fire;
- sleeping space;
- washing;
- animal care;
- introductions;
- protection;
- responsibility for a guest's conduct.

A household may be generous to kin and suspicious of strangers, or the reverse.

## Hospitality is political

In route societies, ports, border towns and pilgrimage networks, refusing lodging can be a serious act.

In crowded cities, hospitality may instead mean buying a meal or arranging a bed elsewhere.

---

# 7. Etiquette

Etiquette should reveal social organization rather than decorate scenes.

Potential axes:

- age;
- rank;
- guest status;
- kinship;
- guild standing;
- religious office;
- debt;
- gender where locally relevant;
- citizenship;
- outsider status;
- speaker / listener language.

Useful scene-level questions:

- Who speaks first?
- Who sits where?
- Who pours or serves?
- Is interruption rude?
- Is direct refusal acceptable?
- Is eye contact respectful or aggressive?
- Who touches whom?
- How close do people stand?
- When is bargaining expected?
- Is punctuality measured by clock, sun, tide, prayer, market opening, or task completion?
- What kinds of gifts create obligation?

No answer should be regional by default.

---

# 8. Conflict, apology and reputation

A society's everyday conflict rules matter more than abstract claims about “honor.”

Possible mechanisms:

- private apology;
- public apology;
- restitution;
- mediated settlement;
- guild discipline;
- family compensation;
- religious reconciliation;
- formal duel or challenge where historically appropriate;
- boycott;
- refusal of service;
- gossip;
- legal complaint.

## Reputation

Reputation can attach to:

- household;
- lineage;
- workshop;
- ship;
- guild branch;
- neighborhood;
- creditor;
- religious office.

This creates consequences short of criminal law.

---

# 9. Care, sickness and disability

Ordinary life should account for people who cannot work at full capacity.

Possible support comes from:

- household;
- guild;
- faith;
- neighborhood;
- employer;
- charity;
- polity;
- informal mutual aid.

The answer may differ by class.

A prestigious guild master and an injured casual laborer can experience the same injury very differently.

This also gives institutions real reasons to retain loyalty.

---

# 10. Aging and elderhood

Age should not automatically equal authority.

Possible elder roles include:

- household authority;
- craft memory;
- legal witness;
- religious office;
- childcare;
- mediation;
- archive / genealogy keeper.

But old age can also mean:

- dependency;
- poverty;
- reduced status;
- conflict with younger generations;
- exclusion from changing work.

The world should contain both respected and neglected elderly people.

---

# 11. Death, mourning and burial

The nine trans-regional religions already have interests in burial, memory, ancestry, confession, cycles and moral testimony.

Therefore burial customs should be shaped by **faith + local material conditions + class + law**, not region alone.

Questions:

- burial, cremation, exposure, tomb, ossuary, water burial, or multiple options?
- who prepares the body?
- who pays?
- what happens to tools / clothes / names / debts?
- how long is mourning visible?
- who is obligated to attend?
- are guilds or faiths responsible for funerals?
- what happens to migrants who die away from home?
- what happens to bodies after disaster or war?

Exact systems remain open.

---

# 12. Markets, streets and public life

Older notes correctly treated market days as social events.

Public life can include:

- market squares;
- wells;
- docks;
- bathhouses where locally appropriate;
- taverns / public houses;
- tea or infusion stalls;
- food stalls;
- guild courts;
- shrine courtyards;
- caravan yards;
- repair alleys;
- public scales;
- communal ovens;
- laundry / washing places;
- ferries;
- firewood queues.

These are places where culture becomes visible without exposition.

---

# 13. Privacy

Privacy is material.

A person in:

- a crowded Port tenement;
- a multigenerational farm compound;
- a caravan camp;
- a wealthy courtyard house;
- a guild boarding house;

has very different opportunities to be alone.

This should affect:

- romance;
- secrets;
- prayer;
- conflict;
- childcare;
- sleep;
- gossip.

Do not assume modern private bedrooms or private conversations are normal.

---

# 14. Generational change

Every community should contain customs that are:

- declining;
- newly fashionable;
- revived;
- commercialized;
- politically promoted;
- mocked by younger people;
- defended by older people;
- imported from Port or another region.

Culture should contain arguments about what counts as “traditional.”

Some traditions described as ancient may be post-Convergence standardizations.

# 15. Regional pressures without regional personality

Regional geography and history still matter.

### Stone Hills

Likely recurring pressures:
- dangerous craft / mine work;
- apprenticeship;
- mixed workshop-households;
- labor mutual aid;
- employer / guild / household overlap;
- imported food dependence in some districts.

This can shape customs around work injury, certification, household labor and apprenticeship without making everyone “blunt.”

### North Coast

Likely recurring pressures:
- seasonal absence at sea;
- rescue obligations;
- harbor rights;
- preservation work;
- winter crowding;
- kin dispersed across coasts and islands.

This can shape care, visiting, inheritance and guest obligations without making everyone “stoic.”

### Low Rivers

Likely recurring pressures:
- seasonal labor;
- land / tenancy;
- storage;
- harvest labor exchange;
- irrigation;
- village and estate obligations.

This can shape household cooperation and inheritance without making everyone “communal.”

### High Roads

Likely recurring pressures:
- travel;
- temporary lodging;
- multilingual work;
- caravan schedules;
- herding cycles;
- contracts.

This can create strong institutions of guesthood, boarding, sponsorship and translation without making everyone “rational.”

### Longwood

Likely recurring pressures:
- dispersed settlements;
- water routes;
- seasonal extraction;
- communal land-use rules;
- forest-edge conflict;
- restricted areas.

This can shape visiting, land permission and neighborhood obligation without making everyone “spiritual.”

### Old Cities

Likely recurring pressures:
- water access;
- dense city life;
- civic status;
- household / merchant networks;
- irrigation schedules;
- heat;
- patronage.

This can shape etiquette, public/private register and neighborhood identity without making everyone “refined.”

### Port

Likely recurring pressures:
- crowding;
- migration;
- mixed households;
- shift work;
- boarding;
- legal pluralism;
- neighborhood identity;
- translation;
- unstable employment.

Port should develop customs that are specifically **Port-born**, not merely blends.

---

# 16. Scene test

An ordinary scene should allow questions such as:

- Who is expected to help?
- Who is allowed to refuse?
- Who pays?
- Who is embarrassed?
- Who is responsible for the child?
- Who gets the best seat?
- Who sleeps where?
- Who has to introduce the stranger?
- Who can borrow tools without asking?
- Who is expected to visit the sick person?
- Who hears the argument through the wall?

If these answers come only from “what region are they from?”, the culture is still too shallow.
