# Weapons and Elite Troops

## Principle

Weapons should be reusable, maintainable, culturally plausible, and suitable for the environment.

Most people do not carry battlefield weapons all day.

Self-defense often uses tools already present in daily life.

See [Military and Weapons Legacy Reconciliation](../Reference/Military-Weapons-Legacy-Reconciliation.md) for the deliberate Volume 09 salvage pass. The old named regional armies and signature-weapon sets remain provisional or rejected; the retained material is institutional and logistical.

## Stone Hills

Common:
- stout knives;
- hammers;
- short spears;
- axes;
- clubs;
- crossbows in some districts.

Why:
metal access makes durable fittings common, but good steel remains expensive.

Elite direction:
disciplined heavy infantry or engineering troops accustomed to fortifications, mines, and confined terrain.

Visual difference:
better helmets, standardized armor pieces, reliable tools, insignia tied to unit rather than flamboyant fantasy armor.

## North Coast

Common:
- utility knives;
- boat hooks;
- axes;
- short spears;
- bows;
- boarding weapons.

Elite direction:
marines/coastal fighters skilled in shipboard combat, rough weather, landing operations, and cold endurance.

Elite distinction:
waterproofed gear, layered clothing, rope skills, compact shields, superior boots.

## Low Rivers

Common:
- staff;
- billhook;
- sickle;
- knife;
- hunting bow;
- spear.

Elite direction:
mobile local defense, scouts, or disciplined levy cadres whose strength is logistics and local terrain rather than exotic weaponry.

## High Roads

Common:
- staff;
- short spear;
- bow;
- sling;
- caravan knife.

Elite direction:
route guards and high-altitude scouts trained for ambush prevention, escort, signal systems, and fighting around narrow passes.

## Longwood

Common:
- bows;
- spears;
- machete/billhook-like cutting tools;
- knives;
- slings.

Elite direction:
rangers/wardens who know terrain, tracking, concealment, river crossings, and coordinated small-unit fighting.

No magical "silent bows" are required.

## Old Cities

Common:
- knives;
- staffs;
- short spears;
- slings;
- bows;
- city watch weapons.

Elite direction:
city-state guards, cavalry where terrain and horse culture support it, professional household troops, or disciplined spear formations.

## Port

Port's best forces are likely specialized:
- harbor watch;
- customs officers;
- fire brigades;
- marine patrols;
- riot/public-order units.

Their advantage is training for dense urban environments rather than superior weapon technology.

## The Underpass

Specialized troops use:
- short spears;
- axes/picks;
- compact shields;
- crossbows;
- helmets;
- ropes;
- lamps.

Long weapons are often impractical.

## Military organization

Military organization belongs to **polities and institutions**, not cultural regions.

A polity may rely on some mixture of:
- household / ruler-controlled troops;
- town watches;
- militias or levies;
- clan forces;
- guild specialists;
- mercenary companies;
- convoy guards;
- harbor patrols;
- professional cadres.

Militias trade permanent readiness for local knowledge and lower standing cost.

Professional forces trade cost and political risk for regular training, command structure and campaign endurance.

## Logistics

A force is constrained by:
- food;
- footwear;
- animal feed;
- ammunition;
- spare shafts / strings / leather;
- repair labor;
- transport;
- medical care;
- pay.

A force that cannot sustain those systems is not elite simply because its equipment is expensive.

## Mobilization often begins as maintenance

Legacy military notes are strongest when they treat military capacity as a **repair and supply system** rather than a parade of special units.

Before open mobilization, a polity can increase readiness through ordinary-looking actions:

- repairing old posts;
- replacing wagon wheels;
- buying spare bowstrings / leather / shafts;
- contracting smiths;
- counting pack animals;
- checking reserve boats;
- increasing preserved-food stocks;
- recalling maps / route records;
- inspecting bridges;
- confirming which craftspeople can be called on.

Most of those actions are also useful in civilian emergencies.

That ambiguity matters.

A neighboring polity may not know whether:

> "they are preparing for a difficult season"

or:

> "they are preparing for war."

This makes maintenance spending an important early security signal in the current escalation model.

## Civilian systems create military capacity

Legacy military notes repeatedly hide their best material inside non-military technology.

Examples include:

- repair workshops;
- waterproofing / preservation;
- irrigation works;
- carts;
- rope;
- portable smithing;
- communal workshops;
- route shelters.

The useful rule is:

> **military readiness grows out of civilian capacity before it grows out of special weapons.**

A polity with:

- many wheelwrights;
- reliable rope makers;
- boat repair yards;
- preserved-food infrastructure;
- trained smiths;
- good route records;
- animal handlers;

can mobilize more effectively than a polity with impressive weapons but weak support systems.

This also means civilian investment can be misread as military preparation.

A new repair yard may genuinely serve:

- fishermen;
- merchants;
- road crews;

while also increasing the number of vessels / wagons that could be maintained during war.

That ambiguity belongs in the current escalation model.

## Elite troop rule

Elite troops should stand out by:
- training;
- discipline;
- logistics;
- equipment quality;
- unit culture;
- communication;
- ability to operate in difficult terrain.

Not by enormous weapons, implausible armor, or supernatural competence.

## Cultural behavior

Each elite formation should have:
- recruitment tradition;
- social reputation;
- distinctive speech/register;
- initiation;
- practical uniform differences;
- rules of conduct;
- relationship with ordinary troops;
- political patrons.

A feared elite unit can be excellent at its specialty and still fail badly outside it.
