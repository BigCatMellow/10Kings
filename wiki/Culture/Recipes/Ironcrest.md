# Ironcrest Recipes

## Status

**Provisional examples consistent with [Food](../Food.md).**

Ironcrest food should not reduce the region to “forge cuisine.” These dishes are strongest in mining, smithing and upland working communities. Lower valleys, wealthier households, religious communities and trade towns can eat very differently.

## Method profile

Common working-kitchen methods include:

- heavy iron or ceramic pots set near hearths, kilns or forge-adjacent cooking fires;
- roasting beside embers rather than placing food in fuel meant for metalworking;
- smoking, drying and pickling for storage;
- dense breads baked on heated stones, griddles or in lidded pots;
- hard browning and rendered fat;
- long simmering that can continue while people work.

### 1. Miner's Gruel

**Cultural logic:** Cheap grain, scraps and leftovers become a hot meal that can be maintained with little attention.

**Typical ingredients**
- cracked barley, oats or another hardy grain;
- water, thin broth or whey;
- onion or leek;
- a little smoked cheese, cured pork, beans or yesterday's meat if available;
- salt and pepper-like local pungency;
- optional malt or dark fruit syrup in households that favor sweet-salty breakfasts.

**In-world method**
1. Grain is soaked when fuel is scarce so it softens faster.
2. It is simmered in a heavy pot near a steady work fire.
3. Onion and scraps go in early; cheese or cured meat goes in near the end.
4. The pot is thinned through the morning as needed.
5. Workers eat from bowls or carry a thickened portion wrapped in bread.

**Variation:** Poorer versions are grain and onion. Better-off crews add dairy, meat and pickles.

### 2. Smoked Ember Stew

**Cultural logic:** Tough preserved meat becomes tender during a workday without requiring a dedicated cook.

**Typical ingredients**
- salted or smoked goat, mutton, beef-like livestock or game;
- onion and garlic-like alliums;
- roots;
- dried mushrooms;
- hardy grain or beans;
- broth, small beer or water;
- thyme, juniper-like berries or other highland aromatics;
- vinegar or sour ale at the finish.

**In-world method**
1. Meat is browned in rendered fat in an iron cauldron.
2. Alliums are cooked in the same fat.
3. Liquid loosens the browned residue.
4. Dried mushrooms and grain are added first.
5. The pot is moved to low residual heat and left for several hours.
6. Roots are added later so they remain intact.
7. Sour vinegar or ale is stirred in before serving to cut the fat.

**Real-world inspiration:** working-class barley stews, goulash-like braises and northern European meat pots.

### 3. Ashen Stone Loaf

**Cultural logic:** A durable sour loaf suited to upland grain, irregular ovens and work travel.

**Typical ingredients**
- rye-like and barley flour;
- sourdough starter;
- water;
- salt;
- malted or toasted grain;
- lard, tallow or butter when available.

**In-world method**
1. A stiff dough is mixed before work and ferments in a warm corner.
2. Small households cook flattened rounds on a heated stone or iron plate.
3. Larger loaves go into a lidded pot set among cooling hearth embers.
4. Loaves are baked dark and dry enough to keep several days.
5. Stale bread is never wasted: it thickens soup, becomes fried scraps or is soaked in broth.

**Note:** “Ashen” describes color and cooking environment; edible ash or coal is not required.

### 4. Coal-Roasted Boar with Root Mash

**Cultural logic:** A feast dish using the region's love of intense browning without pretending industrial fuel is a seasoning.

**Typical ingredients**
- boar, goat or pork-like shoulder;
- salt;
- rock thyme;
- crushed aromatic seed;
- onions;
- potatoes, turnips, rutabaga or parsnips;
- rendered fat;
- dark ale or broth.

**In-world method**
1. Meat is salted the day before.
2. A roasting pit or masonry hearth is heated with clean charcoal or hardwood embers.
3. The meat is set above or beside the coals in a covered iron pan, not buried in raw coal.
4. It roasts slowly until tender.
5. The lid comes off near the end so the exterior darkens.
6. Roots are boiled or steamed separately and mashed with fat and roasting juices.

**Serving:** Large pieces are carved onto bread or root mash at communal tables.

### 5. Smoked Ash-Marrow Broth

**Cultural logic:** Bones, trimmings and stale bread become valuable calories.

**Typical ingredients**
- roasted marrow or joint bones;
- charred onion;
- dried mushroom;
- thyme or sage;
- water;
- salt;
- stale bread or grain for thickening.

**In-world method**
1. Bones are roasted in a hearth oven or on a tray beside embers.
2. They go into an iron pot with water, onion and mushrooms.
3. The broth barely simmers for much of the day.
4. Fat is not fully skimmed in laboring households.
5. Bread crumbs or cooked grain turn it into a meal.

---

### 6. Forge-Fired Sausages

**Cultural logic:** Sausage converts scraps into portable, preservable protein.

**Typical ingredients**
- chopped goat, pork-like livestock or game;
- fat;
- salt;
- dried allium;
- rock thyme;
- pepper-like pungent seed;
- cleaned casing.

**In-world method**
1. Meat and fat are chopped rather than finely ground in poorer districts.
2. The mixture is salted and seasoned.
3. Sausages are poached or gently heated first.
4. Some are eaten fresh; others hang in a smokehouse.
5. Work stalls split smoked sausages and sear them on iron plates.

---

### 7. Smoked Meat and Root Pie

**Cultural logic:** A portable meal for shifts, travel and winter storage.

**Typical ingredients**
- coarse grain pastry;
- smoked meat;
- cooked roots;
- onion;
- thick broth or gravy;
- hard cheese if available.

**In-world method**
1. Leftover stew meat is chopped with roots.
2. The filling is bound with reduced broth.
3. Thick pastry is shaped into hand pies.
4. They bake in a communal oven or lidded iron pan.
5. The crust is intentionally sturdy rather than delicate.

---

### 8. Ember Nuts

Nuts are roasted in a heavy pan near the hearth and tossed with salt, toasted grain dust and a little rendered fat. Imported spice makes this ordinary street food noticeably more expensive.
