# Food Diaspora and Adaptation

## Status

**Working design method, not a fixed menu of canon dishes.**

[Food](Food.md) remains the owner for the setting's broad culinary ecology. This page explains how cuisines change when people migrate, intermarry, trade or live for generations outside their region of origin.

Exact ingredients and example dishes below are **illustrative/provisional** unless established elsewhere.

## Core principle

Diaspora food is not simply “the original recipe with substitutions.”

Over generations, several things can separate:

- flavor;
- technique;
- ritual;
- name;
- occasion;
- social meaning;
- the memory of what the dish is supposed to represent.

A cuisine can therefore remain culturally recognizable even after its pantry changes substantially.

## Three-stage model

### Stage 1 — Faithful reconstruction

First-generation migrants usually try to reproduce familiar food with the ingredients and equipment available.

Common changes:

- one fat replaces another;
- a local souring agent replaces an unavailable vinegar or fruit;
- a host-region grain replaces the original staple;
- cooking time changes because ovens, fuel or altitude differ;
- an expensive imported spice becomes ceremonial rather than everyday.

The **intent** stays close to home even when the result does not.

### Stage 2 — Living double life

The next generation often knows two versions:

- the household/family version;
- the public or host-region version.

Food becomes a form of code-switching.

A person may reject an “inauthentic” restaurant version in front of family, then crave it when living somewhere else. A festival dish may become richer than the ordinary home version because the diaspora community uses abundance to express success.

### Stage 3 — Distinct cuisine

After several generations, the host-region pantry and the inherited toolkit create something that no longer needs to apologize for being different.

What often survives longest:

- a technique;
- a serving gesture;
- a dish name;
- a holiday association;
- a rule about who cooks or receives it;
- the memory that “this is what our people make when…”

Practice can outlive exact taste.

## Two common emotional directions

### Excess

A community from a culture of restraint or scarcity may view a richer diaspora version as missing the point.

The disagreement is not merely culinary. It can become an argument about humility, memory, class or assimilation.

### Aspiration

A community whose older cuisine was shaped by scarcity may see the richer diaspora version as fulfillment rather than corruption: “we finally make it the way our grandparents wished they could.”

Both reactions can exist in the same family.

## Functional flavor framework

When adapting a dish, substitute by **job**, not by one-to-one ingredient resemblance.

| Culinary job | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Fat | What carries aroma and richness locally? |
| Salt / savor | Is depth coming from salt, aged food, smoke, broth, fermentation or concentrated vegetables? |
| Acid | Vinegar, cultured dairy, fruit, pickles, wine, sour grain? |
| Sweetness | Honey, fruit, malt, reduced onions, imported sugar? |
| Heat / pungency | Pepper, mustard-like seeds, aromatic roots, smoke, fermented heat? |
| Fresh lift | Herbs, greens, citrus-like fruit, raw alliums? |
| Texture | Dense bread, dumpling, grain, crisp garnish, nuts/seeds, preserved vegetables? |
| Aroma | What immediately tells a diner where or when they are? |

This protects cultural logic better than copying a modern real-world recipe wholesale.

## Regional cooking biases

These are **design prompts**, not rules about every household.

| Region | Useful culinary bias |
| --- | --- |
| Western Uplands | work schedules, upland staples, imported River Country grain, preservation, workshop districts and class differences |
| North Country | seasonal fisheries, preservation infrastructure, cold storage where available, harbor markets and imported grain |
| River Country | broad agricultural base, mills, storage, dairying, river markets, estate / village / town differences |
| High Country | local upland staples versus traveler food, fuel constraints, caravan provisioning and imported market goods |
| Forest Country | cultivated food plus forest / river supplements, preservation, water access and strong town-to-town ecological variation |
| South Country | irrigation, water cost, pulses/grains/oils/fruit where supported, urban markets and city-specific variation |
| Port | imported food, small urban kitchens, shift work, boarding houses, ship provisioning, street food and diaspora adaptation |

The imported notes contained specific modern ingredient analogues. Treat those as research prompts, not automatic setting facts; [World Rules](../World-Rules.md) requires “draw globally, copy nowhere whole.”

## Naming caution

Example foods on this page are mechanisms, not proposed final dish names.

Do not name diaspora foods by combining regional themes such as “forge,” “frost,” “forest,” “sun,” or “caravan.”

Apply [Naming](Naming.md): real food names often come from a place, family, maker, ingredient, vessel, shape, method, market, or occasion.

## Two illustrative adaptation paths

### Example: a family stew carried into diaspora

Imagine a particular Forest Country family's stew whose identity comes from slow cooking, locally available aromatics and a remembered serving custom.

A diaspora version might preserve the **method and occasion** while changing its pantry:

- in River Country, cultivated roots, dairy or orchard acidity may enter;
- in High Country, dried aromatics and portable grains may become normal;
- in North Country, preserved fish or brined vegetables may alter the savor;
- in South Country, pulses, oil and fresh herbs may reshape it;
- in Western Uplands, smoke and dense grain may make it heavier;
- in Port, several of these descendants can coexist and eventually cross-pollinate.

None of these versions should be treated as automatically canonical dishes. The example demonstrates the mechanism.

### Example: a workday roast carried into another food system

A Western Uplands workday preparation identified by hard browning, smoke and a shared labor context might migrate into South Country.

The host version could use a different meat, oil, acid and herb set while preserving the expectation that the dish should be deeply browned and served after collective labor.

What survives is the **idea of the food**, not necessarily its original shopping list.

## Design checklist

When creating a diaspora dish, ask:

1. What migration or trade vector moved it?
2. Which generation/stage is this version?
3. What original ingredients were unavailable or expensive?
4. What host-region ingredients perform the same culinary jobs?
5. What did the host culture add because it genuinely liked it?
6. What name, technique, ritual or occasion still traces back?
7. What happens when home-region and diaspora versions meet?

If the answer is only “swap ingredient A for ingredient B,” the cultural history is probably too thin.
