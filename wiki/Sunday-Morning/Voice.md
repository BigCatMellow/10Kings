# Voice

## Status

**Writing reference — not setting canon.** Notes on James's own prose voice, taken 2026-09-27 from two short chapters of his (the opening chapters of a Pathwell and Elizabeth story) that he shared as examples. The chapters themselves are not stored here; this page records only the habits they show. James approved a sample passage written to these notes ("i like it").

Use this page when drafting or revising any [Sunday Morning](README.md) story. The [Framework](Framework.md) says *what* a Sunday Morning story does; this page says how James's prose sounds while it does it. Where the two pull apart, the voice wins on the sentence and the framework wins on the story's shape.

Evidence base: two chapters, about 2,300 words. Treat every item as a working observation, and revise it when James marks up a draft.

## The habits

| Habit | What it looks like | In practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Start mid-motion** | The first line is an event: a crash, a question shouted down a stairwell. | Open on something happening. Setting and backstory come in later, a line at a time, or not at all. |
| **Short paragraphs, white space as timing** | Many paragraphs are one line. Big beats stand alone. | Give the beat its own line: "The impact sang." "Then nothing." |
| **Dialogue carries the scene** | Quick exchanges, few tags, people cutting each other off ("I-"), little narration between lines. | Let the talk move the plot. Use action beats instead of "said" where possible. |
| **Humor from character contrast** | A breezy character underreacts; a stressed one overreacts; nobody winks. | Put the comedy in who says what, not in a narrator's irony. |
| **Mundane worries in the middle of big events** | A monster, and she's worried about dry cleaning and a nine o'clock meeting. | Let people keep caring about small things when big things happen. |
| **Petty, specific wants** | "I don't share." "Oh fine, you can have a dumpling." | Give characters small, concrete, slightly selfish wants. |
| **Fragments for impact** | "Wet. Heavy." "Three weeks. Twenty-one days…" | Use them at pressure points, not everywhere. |
| **Feeling through the senses, smell and taste first** | Low tide, rot, butter and sugar, rain through an open window. | Reach emotion through a smell or a taste before naming the feeling. |
| **Grief through objects** | A mixing spoon, a diary, a cat's paw print. | Let one object carry the weight. |
| **One long sentence when the dam breaks** | After many short lines, a single run-on sentence releases what the character has been holding. | Save it for the one moment that earns it. |
| **Callbacks as punchlines** | "Ready?" / "No." / "Perfect." "Perfect" returns changed. | Plant a line early and let it come back with a new meaning. |
| **End on a hook line** | "He still has it." | Close scenes and stories on a short line that turns, not a summary. |

## What to avoid

These are the habits of the first-pass drafts that James's voice does not share:

- a narrator who explains the world ("Ironcrest speech made you say which kind of done a thing was…") — let a character show the habit instead;
- slow scene-setting openings;
- long paragraphs and "and… and…" chains;
- the narrator making the jokes;
- summary where a short exchange would do.

Do not imitate typing slips in the samples (dropped apostrophes such as "Im" and "Wheres"). They are draft artifacts, not voice.

## The approved sample

The opening of [The Heavy Scale](Stories/The-Heavy-Scale.md), rewritten to these notes:

> Eleven stone.
>
> Anselm Quill looked at the beam. The beam looked back.
>
> He took the ten-stone weight out of the pan. Put it back.
>
> Eleven stone. And a little over.
>
> "Well?" The customs chief hadn't taken his hands out of his sleeves all morning.
>
> "It's heavy."
>
> "Heavy."
>
> "By a tenth."
>
> "A tenth of what?"
>
> "Everything."
