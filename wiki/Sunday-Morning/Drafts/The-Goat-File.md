## The Goat File

The gallery laughed the first time the administrator read the new rule, so she read it again.

"Every deferred claim on the hall's books shall be closed at this year's Ledger Closing. By ruling, settlement or withdrawal. None shall be carried forward."

She put the paper down.

"The lenders in Port have written to us. They'll renew the town's credit this year only if our books are clean. They are not interested in our traditions. They were very clear on that point." She looked around the hall. "I'm sorry. We need the credit."

Nobody laughed this time.

Seven Wells was a pass town. Pass towns ran on credit the way cisterns ran on rain. The caravans had been thin all year, with attacks up the route and houses losing whole loads, and half the town had borrowed through the summer against an autumn that never came.

Everyone in the gallery knew someone who needed that renewal.

Then the senior arbiter stood up with a file under his arm.

The laughter came right back.

The file was thick. Four different leathers, because it had outlived three bindings. Someone forty years ago had drawn a goat on the spine so they could find it fast.

"Clerk Aldash."

Pim Aldash had been a junior arbitration clerk for seven months. He had never once been called by name from the upper bench. He stood up so fast he knocked over his inkpot.

"The hall assigns you the matter of the Ledger Goat." The senior arbiter handed it back across three rows of benches. "Tarrow against Kesh. Kesh against Tarrow. Forty-three years deferred. You have until Ledger Closing." A glance at the calendar on the wall. "Ten days."

Then, to the gallery, like a man opening a festival:

"The book is open. Four to one against a ruling."

By the end of the afternoon it was six to one.

Pim hadn't opened the file yet.

---

"Another?"

Pim looked at his cup. Full.

"I haven't finished this one."

"You will." The tea seller poured a second one anyway and set it next to the first.

Her stall stood inside the arbitration hall, under the gallery stairs. It sold tea by the cup and nothing else, and she charged the same for every cup, and nobody had ever seen her write anything down. Everybody said she was the real center of information in Seven Wells.

Pim had assumed for seven months that this was a joke.

He had the goat file open on her counter.

It was, in its way, beautiful. Highridge law recorded testimony exactly. The witness's own words, the year, the speaker. Nobody could claim later that they'd only *meant* something.

Forty-three years of Ledger Closings. Two proud men.

*As recorded in the seventeenth year of the dispute, Ebbe Tarrow said: The goat was lent to the Keshes by my mother as a kindness, and I will not see a kindness turned into a theft.*

*As recorded in the seventeenth year of the dispute, Mardin Kesh said: The goat was lent to the Tarrows by my mother as a courtesy, and a courtesy is not a deed.*

Both of them said the goat had been lent.

Each of them said it went the other way.

"That's the goat file," said the tea seller.

"Yes."

"My mother poured tea for that file." She wiped the counter. "They're making you close it?"

"Everyone has to close everything. The lenders—"

"I know about the lenders." She nodded at the doors, at the yards and the road beyond them. "Not just here, you know. Had a man through last month from down the route. His town did the same. Closed every old claim in one season. Two families lost their houses over debts that had sat quiet for thirty years." She took his cold first cup and poured it back in the pot. "Town after that, the same. Closing a file isn't nothing, clerk."

Pim looked at the file.

He'd been treating it as a joke. Everyone did.

Now it looked like two old men, and two houses, and a ruling one of them would have to stand in the hall and hear.

"How much?" he said.

"Two cups."

"I drank one."

"You drank one of that cup and none of this one. Two."

---

"*As recorded in the first year of the dispute,*" said Ebbe Tarrow, "*I said the goat was the Tarrows' goat.* As recorded in the ninth, I said it again. As recorded in the seventeenth—"

"I've read the seventeenth, sir."

"Then you know."

The caravan yards sat on the downhill side of town, where the route came out of the pass and flattened enough to hold a caravan. In a good year they were full by late autumn. Camels, mules, oxen. Bales and cases. Guides and drovers arguing at the gates.

This year a quarter of the stalls were empty. The Tarrow stalls were emptier than most.

Ebbe Tarrow was seventy-one and had taken caravans over the pass for fifty years. He met Pim in a good coat. He poured tea. He asked after Pim's family. He talked about the weather and the thin year for a full quarter of an hour, with enormous dignity.

Then Pim said the word *goat*, and Ebbe Tarrow went red from the collar up.

"My mother lent that goat to Seraph Kesh when the Kesh herd failed. A kindness. My mother was kind." He folded his hands on his stick. "And the Keshes have kept it, and every kid it ever dropped, for forty-three years. Every Ledger Closing they file their claim like it's theirs. Every Ledger Closing the hall says *deferred*. And the whole town laughs."

He looked out at the empty stalls.

"I didn't mind the laughing. It was a tradition. But the lenders don't care for traditions, I'm told."

"No, sir."

"If you rule for the Keshes, I'm the Tarrow who lost the goat. In front of the hall. After forty-three years." Quietly. "In a year like this one, clerk, a house doesn't have much face to spare."

He paused.

"My mother told me herself, you know. I remember her words exactly." He drew himself up. "*She is only lent, Ebbe. She'll come home in time.*"

Pim wrote it down, exactly.

On the way out he passed an empty stall with a Tarrow mark on its post. A boy was sweeping it.

There was nothing in it to sweep.

---

The cisterns were Seven Wells. The town was named for the wells, but the wells had gone brackish generations ago. It was the cisterns that let a pass town exist at all: cut into the terraces above the town, lined with fitted stone, kept by House Kesh for as long as anyone remembered.

Mardin Kesh was sixty-eight and still walked every edge of them each morning with a lamp.

He walked Pim along the upper terrace and stopped, without being asked, at the third cistern.

A long crack in the lining. A dark stain where the water was seeping out.

"The renewal would mend that," Mardin said. "Town borrows. House gets paid to mend it. I hire masons in the spring. No renewal, and the crack grows. Two summers, the third cistern's dry. A bad summer, and the town's short."

He looked at it a long time.

"So I need your file closed, clerk. Understand? I *need* it closed."

"Yes, sir."

"And I cannot lose it."

Pim waited.

"The goat was lent to the Tarrows by my mother. As a courtesy. It was *their* herd that failed that year, whatever Ebbe says." Mardin straightened. "My mother told me so herself. I remember her words."

He used the careful formal register. The one people used when they meant to be held to every word.

"*She is only lent, Mardin. She'll come home in time.*"

Pim wrote it down.

Then stopped writing.

He looked at the page.

The back of his neck went cold.

He flipped back to yesterday. The caravan yards. Ebbe Tarrow, same register, same care.

*She is only lent, Ebbe. She'll come home in time.*

Two women. Two houses. Two sons, forty years on.

The same ten words.

Only the names were different.

In a Highridge town, where people quoted each other for a living, that did not happen by accident.

"Something wrong, clerk?"

"No, sir." Pim closed his book. "I think your mother and Master Tarrow's mother might have known each other rather well."

Mardin Kesh looked at the cracked cistern and said nothing at all.

---

The upland pasture sat above the last terrace, where the town ran out and the mountain started. It was home to what the town called, with no irony at all, the Ledger herd.

Nobody knew how many goats had come from the original. Some were obviously hers: small, white, long-eared. Some had been traded across the plateau and come back. Or not. Some had been counted in one house's claim one year and in the other's the next.

The file held eleven herd censuses from eleven different years. None of them agreed. None had ever been meant to prove anything except that the other house was wrong.

Pim counted anyway.

Forty-one.

A group came over the ridge. Fifty-three.

Several of the fifty-three went back over the ridge.

He found Lio Tarrow and Nessa Kesh behind the shepherd's hut, sitting closer together than people sit when they just happen to be sitting near each other.

They stood up very fast.

"I'm counting goats," said Pim.

That was true, and it made everything worse.

Ebbe's grandson and Mardin's granddaughter. Courting quietly since spring. Which in Seven Wells meant everyone under twenty-five knew about it and nobody over sixty did.

"Please don't rule," Nessa said.

"I have to rule. The lenders—"

"Then rule for nobody." Lio had his grandfather's height and none of his color. He was as white as the goats. "Rule for my house, and her grandfather never forgives mine. Rule for hers, and mine never sets foot in the cisterns again. As long as it's deferred, they can pretend it's a joke."

"Once it's ruled," Nessa said, "somebody lost."

"And then we'd have to decide," said Lio, "which one of us lost."

Pim looked at them. At the goats. At his slate, which said fifty-three, then forty-one, then *I don't know*.

"I'll try," he said.

It was the only true thing he could think of.

When he got back to the hall, the book was at nine to one.

---

"Anything deposited under Tarrow or Kesh?" Pim asked. "Forty-three, forty-four years back?"

"Not until the hours end."

The archive was cut into the rock under the arbitration hall. It was kept, as many old archives on the plateau were, by an observer of the Silent Whisper. A small woman in gray named Oriel, who'd kept it for thirty years and did not believe in hurrying anything.

She had a small brass bell on her desk. This was the fourth day of the Whisper's autumn silence hours. Seven days a year when the archive released nothing and its custodian spoke as little as she possibly could.

"When do they end?"

"The evening before Ledger Closing."

"That's the night before the ruling."

"Yes."

She saw his face. Hers softened.

"A record that has waited forty-four years can wait three more days. And you can wait with it. A question asked in a hurry hears only half its answer." She put one finger on the bell. Gently.

She was done.

Pim went back up the stairs.

He didn't go home. He went to the tea stall.

"The archive," the tea seller said, and poured.

"She won't open it till the night before."

"She never does." She slid the cup across. "My mother used to see them go down there, you know. The two of them."

"The two of—"

"Hanne Tarrow and Seraph Kesh. Ebbe's mother and Mardin's. When they were young. Walked down to the archive arm in arm, laughing at something. My mother said you never saw one without the other." She wiped the counter. "Then came the dry summer. A quarrel over the wells. Never saw them together again. Then the goat business started, with their boys." She shrugged. "Nobody asked me."

Pim stared at her.

"Two cups," said the tea seller.

---

The silence hours ended at sundown on the eve of Ledger Closing.

Pim was sitting on the bottom step of the archive stair when Oriel took the brass bell off her desk and put it in a drawer.

"Tarrow and Kesh," she said. Her ordinary voice. He'd never heard it before. "Forty-four years ago." She took a ring of keys from her belt. "Come."

The locked room at the back was small and cold and full of pigeonholes. Bundles in ribbon. Wax seals. Oriel ran a finger along one row, stopped, and drew out a single folded sheet.

Two seals side by side. A camel for the Tarrows. A cistern for the Keshes.

"The seal term ran out thirty years ago," she said. "Free to anyone who asked, since. Either house. The hall. Anyone at all."

"And nobody—"

"You are the first to ask."

He broke the seals under the reading-room lamp. Thin paper. Ink gone brown. A quick, confident hand. Two signatures.

*Recorded between Hanne of House Tarrow and Seraph of House Kesh, friends, in the spring before the dry summer:*

*The white goat called Ledger is not lent by either house to the other. She is wagered. She, and all that come of her, belong to whichever house hosts the next wedding between our two families. Until that day she is lent to both.*

*We each expect to win.*

*Our sons are not to be told, because they would argue about it.*

Pim read it three times.

Then he put his head down on the reading-room table and laughed until Oriel, very kindly, brought him a cup of water.

---

The hall was packed for Ledger Closing.

Caravan negotiators in the gallery, in town to settle the season's accounts. The senior arbiter on the upper bench, betting book open beside him. Eleven to one. Ebbe Tarrow on the west bench, Mardin Kesh on the east, both in their best coats, both studying the ceiling. Lio and Nessa at the very back of the gallery, as far apart as it was possible to sit, which fooled no one under twenty-five.

The tea seller had moved her stall six feet closer to the benches.

"The matter of the Ledger Goat," said the senior arbiter. "Clerk Aldash."

Pim stood with a single folded sheet.

"The hall has received testimony in this matter for forty-three years." His voice cracked on *forty*. He started over. "The hall has also received, last night, from the archive, a sealed private deposit. Recorded forty-four years ago, between the late Hanne Tarrow and the late Seraph Kesh."

The gallery went quiet.

Ebbe Tarrow stopped looking at the ceiling.

"It concerns the goat."

He read it exactly, the way he'd been trained.

He read *friends*. Mardin Kesh's hands closed on his knees.

He read *wagered*. Someone in the gallery started to laugh, and stopped.

He read *whichever house hosts the next wedding*. He read *until that day she is lent to both*. He read *we each expect to win*.

Then he read *our sons are not to be told, because they would argue about it*.

The whole hall laughed at once.

Ebbe Tarrow laughed too. Loud and helpless, one hand over his eyes. After a moment, so did Mardin Kesh.

"*She is only lent,*" Ebbe said, when he could. "*She'll come home in time.* That's what she told me. She meant—"

"She told me the same," Mardin said. "Word for word. The two of them. They must have—"

He stopped.

He looked across the hall at Ebbe Tarrow. The gallery agreed later it was the first time in forty-three years.

"They must have practiced."

At the back of the gallery, Lio Tarrow and Nessa Kesh stood up.

They didn't say anything. They didn't have to.

They stood up at opposite ends of the back row. Then Nessa walked the length of it and took Lio's hand.

The hall turned to look at them.

It kept looking for a while.

Pim picked up his pen. In the column for rulings, in the formal register, he wrote:

*Completed. Pending wedding.*

---

It was lawful. The senior arbiter confirmed it from the bench within the hour. Highridge law was entirely comfortable with a condition, provided it was stated exactly.

This one had been stated exactly for forty-four years.

The ownership of the Ledger Goat was settled. It just hadn't happened yet.

The senior arbiter paid out, at eleven to one, cheerfully, to the three people in the gallery who'd bet on a ruling.

One of them was the tea seller.

"You knew," said Pim.

"Everybody knew their mothers were friends." She poured. "Nobody asked me what it meant."

The administrator sent the hall's books to Port with every deferred claim closed. The lenders renewed within the month. Mardin Kesh hired masons for the spring.

The wedding was set for spring too. Both houses announced they would host it.

Both houses also announced, separately and on the same day, that they would provide the goat.

When it was pointed out to each house that the other had offered a goat too, each house said that of course the other house was welcome to provide *a* goat. But *the* goat would come from them.

The matter went to the tea stall. It was expected to stay there for some time.

---

The senior arbiter found Pim at the counter that evening and sat down next to him.

He'd never done that before.

"I got that file thirty years ago," he said. "My first year. Same as you. The senior arbiter then was taking bets, same as me. I read every page. Counted the goats." He put two coins on the counter. "I failed."

Pim looked at him.

"Been hoping someone would close it ever since. The book was the only way I could think of to make sure someone tried."

"You bet against me."

"I bet against the file. Different thing." He nodded to the tea seller. "His is on me."

As the gallery emptied, one of the caravan negotiators came down the stairs and stopped at the counter. Tall, quiet, traveling coat, a route book under one arm. Pim had noticed him during the reading. He'd been the only one in the gallery taking notes.

"Samir Tareh." A small nod. "I settle accounts here for three caravan houses. May I ask you one question, clerk?"

"Of course."

"The Tarrows run guides over the pass. The Keshes keep the water." He tapped the route book. "When those two marry, will a caravan stopping here get both? From one house? Under one roof?"

Pim thought about Ebbe's empty stalls. Mardin's cracked cistern. A thin year on the route, and caravans looking for somewhere they could count on.

"I think so," he said. "In the spring."

Samir Tareh opened the route book and wrote something down. He thanked Pim and the tea seller, paid for a cup he hadn't drunk, and went out into the cold.

Up on the pasture, fifty-three goats grazed in the dark.

Or forty-one.

None of them had any idea they were a dowry.
