## The Goat File

The administrator announced the new rule from the arbitration hall's upper bench, ten days before Ledger Closing, and read it twice, because the first time the gallery laughed.

"Every deferred claim on the hall's books," she read again, "shall be closed at this year's Ledger Closing, by ruling, settlement or withdrawal. None shall be carried forward." She lowered the paper. "The lenders in Port have written to us. They will renew the town's credit this year only if our books are clean. They are not interested in our traditions. They have been very clear on that point." She looked around the hall. "I am sorry. We need the credit."

Nobody laughed the second time. Seven Wells was a pass town, and pass towns ran on credit the way cisterns ran on rain. The caravans had been thin all year, with attacks up the route and houses losing loads, and half the town had borrowed through the summer against a better autumn that had not come. Everyone in the gallery knew someone who needed that renewal.

Then the senior arbiter stood up, with a file under his arm, and everyone in the gallery knew exactly which deferred claim he was holding, and the laughter came back.

The file was thick. It was bound in four different leathers, because it had outlasted three bindings, and it had a goat drawn on the spine in faded ink by some clerk forty years ago who had wanted to find it quickly.

"Clerk Aldash," said the senior arbiter.

Pim Aldash, who had been a junior arbitration clerk for seven months and had never once been called by name from the upper bench, stood up so fast he knocked his inkpot over.

"The hall assigns you the matter of the Ledger Goat," the senior arbiter said, and handed him the file across three rows of benches. "Tarrow against Kesh, Kesh against Tarrow. Forty-three years deferred. You have until Ledger Closing." And then, turning to the gallery with the air of a man opening a festival: "The book is open. I'm offering four to one against a ruling."

By the end of the afternoon it was six to one, and Pim had not yet opened the file.

* * *

The tea stall stood inside the arbitration hall, against the east wall under the gallery stairs, where it had stood longer than the gallery. It sold tea by the cup, and nothing else, and the tea seller charged the same for every cup and nobody had ever seen her write anything down. Everybody said she was the real center of information in Seven Wells. Pim had assumed for seven months that this was a joke.

He sat at her counter the next morning with the goat file open and a cup of black tea going cold.

The file was, in its way, beautiful. Highridge law recorded testimony exactly, in the witness's words, with the year and the speaker marked, so that no one could later say that a thing had been said when it had only been meant. Forty-three years of Ledger Closings were preserved here in the precise words of two proud men. As recorded in the seventeenth year of the dispute, Ebbe Tarrow said: *The goat was lent to the Keshes by my mother as a kindness, and I will not see a kindness turned into a theft.* As recorded in the seventeenth year of the dispute, Mardin Kesh said: *The goat was lent to the Tarrows by my mother as a courtesy, and a courtesy is not a deed.*

Both men claimed the goat had been lent, and each said it had been lent the other way.

"Another?" said the tea seller.

Pim looked at his cup, which was full. "I haven't finished this one."

"You will." She poured a second cup anyway and set it beside the first. "That's the goat file."

"Yes."

"My mother poured tea for that file." She wiped the counter. "They're making you close it?"

"Everyone has to close everything. The lenders—"

"I know about the lenders." She nodded toward the doors, toward the caravan yards and the roads beyond. "It's not just here. I had a man through last month from down the route. His town did the same thing. Closed every old claim in a season. Two families there lost their houses over debts that had sat quiet for thirty years, because somebody had to rule and the ruling went against them." She took his first cup, which had gone cold, and poured it back into the pot. "And a second town, further on, the same. Closing a file isn't nothing, clerk."

Pim looked at the file. He had been thinking of it as a joke, because everyone else did. He thought of it now as two old men, and two houses, and a ruling that one of them would have to stand in the hall and hear.

"How much for the tea?" he said.

"Two cups," said the tea seller.

"I drank one."

"You drank one of this cup and none of the other," she said. "Two."

* * *

Ebbe Tarrow received him in the caravan yards, because that was where the Tarrows had always received people.

The yards lay on the downhill side of Seven Wells, below the cistern terraces, where the route came out of the pass and flattened enough to hold a caravan. In a good year they were full by late autumn: camels and mules and oxen, bales and cases, guides and drovers and negotiators arguing at the gates. This year a quarter of the stalls were empty, and the Tarrow stalls were emptier than most.

Ebbe Tarrow was seventy-one and had taken caravans over the pass for fifty years, and he met Pim at the gate in a good coat, with the manner of a man who expected to be spoken to with respect and generally was. He gave Pim tea. He asked after Pim's family. He discussed the weather, the route and the thin year with great dignity for a quarter of an hour, and then Pim said the word *goat*, and Ebbe Tarrow's face went red from the collar up.

"As recorded in the first year of the dispute," Ebbe said, "I said that the goat was the Tarrows' goat. As recorded in the ninth, I said it again. As recorded in the seventeenth—"

"I've read the seventeenth, sir."

"Then you know." Ebbe folded his hands on the head of his stick. "My mother lent that goat to Seraph Kesh when the Kesh herd failed. It was a kindness. My mother was kind. And the Keshes have kept it and every kid it ever dropped for forty-three years, and every Ledger Closing they file their claim as though it were theirs, and every Ledger Closing the hall says *deferred*, and the whole town laughs." He looked out across the half-empty yard. "I did not mind the laughing. It was a tradition. But the lenders don't care for traditions, I am told."

"No, sir."

"If you rule for the Keshes," said Ebbe, "I will be the Tarrow who lost the goat. In front of the hall. After forty-three years." He said it quietly. "In a year like this one, clerk, a house doesn't have a great deal of face to spare."

On the way out Pim passed an empty stall with a Tarrow mark on its post. A boy was sweeping it, though there was nothing in it to sweep.

* * *

Mardin Kesh received him at the cisterns, because that was where the Keshes had always been.

The cisterns were Seven Wells. The town was named for the wells, but the wells had gone brackish long ago; it was the cisterns, cut into the terraces above the town and lined with fitted stone, that let a pass town exist at all. House Kesh had kept them for as long as anyone remembered. Mardin Kesh, at sixty-eight, still walked their edges every morning with a lamp, and he took Pim along the upper terrace and showed him, without being asked, the long crack in the third cistern's lining where the water was seeping away.

"The renewal would mend that," Mardin said. "The town borrows, the house is paid to mend it, I hire masons in the spring. If the lenders don't renew, the crack gets longer, and in two summers the third cistern is dry, and in a bad summer the town is short." He looked at the crack for a long time. "So I need your file closed, clerk. You understand. I need it closed."

"Yes, sir."

"And I cannot lose it."

Pim waited.

"As recorded in the seventeenth year," said Mardin Kesh, "I said that the goat was lent to the Tarrows by my mother as a courtesy. It was. The Tarrow herd failed that year, not ours, whatever Ebbe says. My mother lent them a goat. And my mother told me so herself, clerk. I remember her words."

"What were they?"

Mardin Kesh drew himself up and quoted, in the careful formal register Highridge people used when they meant to be held to every word: "*She is only lent, Mardin. She'll come home in time.*"

Pim wrote it down. He wrote it down exactly, as he had been trained to, with the speaker and the source marked. Then he stopped writing and looked at what he had written, and a small cold feeling started at the back of his neck.

He had written those exact words the afternoon before.

He turned back through his notes. There it was, from the caravan yards, in Ebbe Tarrow's formal register: "*My mother told me, She is only lent, Ebbe. She'll come home in time.*"

Two women, two houses, two sons forty years on, and the same ten words. Only the names were different.

In a Highridge town, where people quoted each other exactly for a living, that did not happen by chance.

"Is something the matter, clerk?"

"No, sir," said Pim. "I think your mother and Master Tarrow's mother may have known each other rather well."

Mardin Kesh looked at the cracked cistern and said nothing at all.

* * *

The upland pasture lay above the last terrace, where the town ran out and the mountain began, and on it lived what the town called, without any irony, the Ledger herd.

Nobody knew how many goats were descended from the original. Some were plainly hers: small and white, with the long ears the town's old breeders swore by. Some had been traded across the plateau and come back, or not come back. Some had been counted in one house's claim and then in the other's. The file contained eleven separate herd censuses, taken in eleven different years at the demand of one house or the other, and none of them agreed, and none of them, as far as Pim could tell, had ever been meant to prove anything except that the other house was wrong.

He counted anyway. He had been told to close the file, and the file had a census in it, so he climbed to the pasture with a slate and counted goats. He got forty-one. Then a group came over the ridge and he got fifty-three. Then several of the fifty-three went back over the ridge.

He found Lio Tarrow and Nessa Kesh behind the shepherd's hut, sitting closer together than people sat when they happened to be sitting near each other.

They stood up very fast. Pim, who was not good at this sort of thing, said, "I'm counting goats," which was true and made everything worse.

Lio Tarrow was Ebbe's grandson, Nessa Kesh was Mardin's granddaughter, and they had been courting quietly since the spring, which in Seven Wells meant that everyone under twenty-five knew about it and nobody over sixty did.

"Please don't rule," Nessa said.

"I have to rule. The lenders—"

"Then rule for nobody." Lio had his grandfather's height and none of his grandfather's color; he was white as the goats. "If you rule for my house, her grandfather will never forgive mine. If you rule for hers, mine will never set foot in the cisterns again. As long as it's deferred, they can pretend it's a joke. Once it's ruled, somebody lost."

"And then we'll have to choose," Nessa said, "which one of us lost."

Pim looked at them, and at the goats, and at his slate, which said fifty-three, then forty-one, then *I don't know*.

"I'll try," he said, which was the only true thing he could think of.

When he got back to the hall the senior arbiter's book was at nine to one.

* * *

The archive of Seven Wells was cut into the rock under the arbitration hall, and it was kept, as many old archives on the plateau were kept, by an observer of the Silent Whisper.

The custodian was a small woman in gray named Oriel, who had kept the archive for thirty years and did not believe in hurrying anything. The archive held the hall's records and the town's deeds and, in a locked room at the back, the sealed private deposits: papers left by families and individuals with instructions that they be kept, and not opened except on request, sometimes for a set term, sometimes forever. Pim knew this in the way a clerk knew most things about the archive, which was in outline and without ever having gone into the locked room.

He went down the stair four days before Ledger Closing and asked whether there was any deposit under the names Tarrow or Kesh, from forty-three or forty-four years ago.

Oriel looked at him, then at the small brass bell on her desk. It was the fourth day of the Whisper's autumn silence hours: seven days, every autumn, in which the archive released nothing and its custodian spoke as little as she could.

"Not until the hours end," she said.

"When do they end?"

"The evening before Ledger Closing."

"That's the night before the ruling."

"Yes." She saw his face, and her own softened. "A record that has waited forty-four years," she said, "can wait three more days. And you can wait with it. It will do you good. The Whisper holds that a question asked in a hurry hears only half its answer." She put a finger on the bell, gently, to show she was done speaking.

Pim went back up the stair. He did not go home. He went to the tea stall.

"The archive," said the tea seller, and poured.

"She won't open it until the night before."

"No, she won't. She never has." The tea seller slid the cup toward him. "My mother used to see them go down there, you know. The two of them."

"The two of them?"

"Hanne Tarrow and Seraph Kesh. Ebbe's mother and Mardin's. When they were young women. They used to walk down to the archive together, arm in arm, laughing at something. My mother always said you never saw one without the other." She wiped the counter. "Then came the dry summer, and a quarrel over the wells, and after that you never saw them together at all. And then the goat business started with their boys." She shrugged. "Nobody asked me."

Pim looked at her.

"Two cups," said the tea seller.

* * *

The silence hours ended at sundown on the eve of Ledger Closing. Pim was sitting on the bottom step of the archive stair when Oriel took the brass bell off her desk and put it in a drawer.

"Tarrow and Kesh," she said, in her ordinary voice, which he had never heard before. "Forty-four years ago." She took a ring of keys from her belt. "Come with me."

The locked room at the back was small and cold and lined with pigeonholes. Most of them held bundles tied in ribbon and sealed in wax. Oriel ran her finger along a row, and stopped, and drew out a single folded sheet, sealed with two seals side by side: a camel for the Tarrows, a cistern for the Keshes.

"The seal term ran out thirty years ago," she said. "It's been free to anyone who asked since then. Both houses. The hall. Anyone." She held it out to him.

"And nobody—"

"You are the first to ask," said Oriel.

He broke the seals in the reading room, under the lamp, with her watching. The paper was thin and good and the ink had gone brown. The hand was quick and confident, and there were two signatures at the bottom.

*Recorded between Hanne of House Tarrow and Seraph of House Kesh, friends, in the spring before the dry summer:*

*The white goat called Ledger is not lent by either house to the other. She is wagered. She and all that come of her are the property of whichever house hosts the next wedding between our two families. Until that day she is lent to both.*

*We each expect to win.*

*Our sons are not to be told, because they would argue about it.*

Pim read it three times. Then he put his head down on the reading-room table and laughed until Oriel, very kindly, brought him a cup of water.

* * *

The arbitration hall was full on the morning of Ledger Closing, because it was Ledger Closing, and because everybody knew that this year the goat file would close.

The caravan negotiators were in the gallery, in town to settle the season's accounts before the relief that followed. The senior arbiter sat on the upper bench with his betting book open beside him, at eleven to one. Ebbe Tarrow sat on the west bench and Mardin Kesh on the east, both in their best coats, both looking at the ceiling. Lio and Nessa sat at the very back of the gallery, as far apart as it was possible to sit, which fooled no one under twenty-five. The tea seller had moved her stall six feet closer to the benches, and was pouring.

The administrator read out the rule again. Then the senior arbiter said, "The matter of the Ledger Goat. Clerk Aldash," and Pim stood up with a single folded sheet in his hand.

"The hall has received testimony in this matter for forty-three years," Pim said. His voice cracked on *forty*, and he began again. "The hall has also received, last night, from the archive, a sealed private deposit, recorded forty-four years ago between the late Hanne Tarrow and the late Seraph Kesh. It concerns the goat."

The gallery went quiet. Ebbe Tarrow stopped looking at the ceiling.

Pim read it. He read it exactly, as he had been trained to, with the speakers named. He read *friends*, and saw Mardin Kesh's hands close on his knees. He read *wagered*, and heard somebody in the gallery start to laugh and stop. He read *whichever house hosts the next wedding between our two families*, and read *until that day she is lent to both*, and read *we each expect to win*. And then he read *our sons are not to be told, because they would argue about it*, and the whole hall laughed at once. Ebbe Tarrow laughed too, loudly and helplessly, with his hand over his eyes. After a moment, so did Mardin Kesh.

"She is only lent," Ebbe said, when he could. "She'll come home in time. That's what she told me. She meant—"

"She told me the same," Mardin said. "Word for word. The two of them. They must have—" He stopped. He looked across the hall at Ebbe Tarrow for what the gallery later agreed was the first time in forty-three years. "They must have practiced."

At the back of the gallery, Lio Tarrow and Nessa Kesh stood up.

They did not say anything. They didn't need to. They stood up together, at opposite ends of the back row, and then Nessa walked along the row and took Lio's hand, and the hall turned to look at them, and it went on looking for some time.

Pim wrote in the file, in the column for rulings, in the formal register: *Completed. Pending wedding.*

The ruling was lawful. The senior arbiter confirmed it from the upper bench within the hour. Highridge law was entirely comfortable with a condition, provided the condition was stated exactly, and this one had been stated exactly for forty-four years. The ownership of the Ledger Goat and all that came of her was settled. It simply hadn't happened yet.

The senior arbiter paid out. He paid out cheerfully, at eleven to one, to the three people in the gallery who had bet on a ruling, and one of them was the tea seller.

"You knew," said Pim.

"Everybody knew their mothers were friends," said the tea seller. "Nobody asked me what that meant."

* * *

Afterward, in the brief relief that always followed Ledger Closing, the town settled things.

The administrator sent the hall's books to Port with every deferred claim closed. The lenders renewed the town's credit within the month. Mardin Kesh hired masons for the spring, and on the first dry morning of the thaw, the third cistern would be mended.

The wedding was set for the spring. Both houses announced that they would host it, and both houses announced, separately and on the same day, that they would provide the goat. When it was pointed out to each house that the other had also offered a goat, each house said that of course the other house was welcome to provide *a* goat, but that *the* goat would come from them. The matter went to the tea stall, where it was expected to remain for some time.

The senior arbiter found Pim at the counter that evening and sat down beside him, which he had never done.

"I was given that file thirty years ago," he said. "My first year. Same as you. The senior arbiter then was taking bets, same as me. I read every page. I counted the goats. I failed." He put two coins on the counter. "I've been hoping someone would close it ever since. The book was the only way I could think of to make sure someone tried."

"You bet against me," said Pim.

"I bet against the file," said the senior arbiter. "Different thing." He nodded to the tea seller. "His is on me."

As the gallery emptied, one of the caravan negotiators came down the stair and stopped at the counter. He was a tall, quiet man in a traveling coat, with a route book under his arm, and Pim had noticed him in the gallery because he had been the only person there taking notes.

"Samir Tareh," the man said, and nodded. "I settle accounts for three caravan houses here. Clerk, may I ask you one question?"

"Of course."

"The Tarrows run guides over the pass. The Keshes keep the town's water." He tapped the route book with one finger. "When the grandchildren marry, will a caravan that stops here be able to get both, from one house, under one roof?"

Pim thought about it. He thought about Ebbe's empty stalls and Mardin's cracked cistern, and about a thin year on the route, with caravans looking for places they could rely on.

"I think so," he said. "In the spring."

Samir Tareh nodded, opened the route book, and wrote something down. Then he thanked Pim and the tea seller, paid for a cup he hadn't drunk, and went out into the cold.

On the upland pasture, fifty-three goats, or perhaps forty-one, went on grazing, unaware that they had become a dowry.
