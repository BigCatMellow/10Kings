## One Square, Two Harvests

The carts were still coming in on the tenth day before Harvest Home, which was the whole trouble, and Pell Anwick was up his quince tree, which was the whole point of being retired.

From the third branch he could see most of Harveston Vale: the grain lanes running north toward the wetter fields, the orchard terraces climbing south toward the dry country, the canal that fed both, and the square in the middle where the lanes and terraces met and pretended to get along. Carts moved along every lane. They had been moving for three weeks. Nobody in the Vale could remember a harvest that took three weeks to bring in, and several people had tried hard to.

"Pell!"

He did not look down. A man in a tree, he had decided, was not obliged to hear anything below the second branch.

"Pell Anwick, I know you can hear me, you've got the face you make in court."

Bettany Corlew had chaired the Harvest Home committee for eleven years and had never once shouted up a tree, so he looked down after all. She stood with her fists on her hips and a ledger under one arm, which for Bettany was the same as a sword.

"I'm retired," Pell said.

"I know you're retired. The whole Vale knows you're retired. You had a party."

"Then you know I'm busy." He showed her the quince in his hand. It had a small brown bruise on one side, and he had been deciding for some time whether the bruise was the tree's fault or the wasp's. "Forty years I waited to have time for this tree."

"The harvest's late."

"I'd noticed."

"It's late because it's big. It's the biggest harvest anyone's seen. And Harvest Home follows the harvest, it always has, you can't hold the accounting before the grain's in." She lifted the ledger as though he could read it from the tree. "So Harvest Home moves back a week. Into the Crush's week."

Pell said nothing. From the third branch he could also see the Salve house at the south end of the square, its courtyard gate already hung with the first vine garlands for the Wine Crush.

"The square belongs to each festival in its own week," Bettany said. "That's the clause. Our week is the week the harvest is in. This year that's their week. I'm going to tell Idris Salve this afternoon, and he's going to say it's their square, and someone has to tell him he's wrong."

"Someone," Pell said.

"You've been telling people they're wrong for forty years."

"I have been getting people to agree for forty years," Pell said. "It isn't the same thing. It isn't even close."

Below the tree, at the corner of the lane, the canal gatekeeper was walking past with his hands clasped behind him, reciting, as he always did when a conversation near him went badly: "North sluice opens at first bell, closes at third. South sluice at second bell, closes at fourth. Mill race on the half—"

"Good morning, Aurel," Pell called.

"Good morning, Arbiter." Aurel did not stop walking or reciting. "—mill race on the half, except market days, when the mill race—"

"I'm not an arbiter anymore," Pell called after him, and went back to the quince.

Bettany waited long enough to make sure he knew she was waiting, then went. Pell stayed up the tree until his granddaughter came to tell him lunch was ready. She was nine and called the square the Commons in the morning and the Press Yard after lunch, and had never noticed that she did.

* * *

The co-op granary was full to the rafters, and it was not supposed to be.

A granary full in early autumn was a fine thing. A granary full because nobody would buy from it was something else, and the whole Vale knew the difference. Bettany had brought Pell there the next day on the grounds that he was retired and therefore had nothing better to do, and he had come on the grounds that he did not want her shouting up his tree again.

His granddaughter came too. Lissa sat on a sack in the doorway with a bowl of orchard grain — barley cooked in last year's apricot syrup, with a spoon of sour cream on top — and ate it with the concentration of someone who did not intend to be sent home.

"Listen to this," Bettany said, and read from her ledger. At Harvest Home the committee stood on the square's steps and read out the year: what had come in, what was stored, what was owed, what would be given to the households that asked. The Vale had done it for longer than it had had a mill. "'The co-op holds four times its usual surplus. Of this surplus, none has been sold.'" She shut the ledger. "It sounds like we're announcing a funeral."

"It's a large harvest," Pell said. "It's good news."

"It's a large harvest in a year when everybody's got a large harvest and nobody's buying." She sat down beside Lissa, which Pell had not seen her do in eleven years of committees. "The downriver merchants won't even make an offer. Prices are falling, they say, wait a month. And then they say the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"That the new seed isn't safe." Bettany said it quietly, as if the grain could hear. "The co-op planted the new strain last year, the one from the Meadowcroft trials. That's why the harvest's so big. And now half the merchants on the river say they've heard that grain from it makes people sick. Nobody's been sick. Nobody knows anyone who's been sick. But they've heard."

Pell looked at the sacks. Lissa ate another spoonful of orchard grain, made from last year's barley, which was not the new seed, and Pell found himself relieved about that and was annoyed with himself for it.

"And there's the other other thing," Bettany said. "There's a man."

"There's always a man."

"A land agent. From downriver. Very polite. He's been to the Thistle farm and the Orrins and I think the Brays. Farms that took seed loans for the new strain and now can't sell the grain to pay them." She turned the ledger over in her lap. "He doesn't threaten anyone. He sits in their kitchens and says how sorry he is, and he offers to take the notes off their hands. And he waits."

"Has anyone sold?"

"Not yet." She looked at the sacks the way he looked at his quince. "Not yet."

* * *

Idris Salve received them in the courtyard of the Salve house, because the Salve house received everyone in the courtyard; that was what the courtyard was for. It was tiled in blue and ochre, shaded by a vine older than anyone in the Vale, and built, Pell had always thought, less to be sat in than to be seen sitting in. Idris sat in it badly. He was twenty-three and had been running the Wine Crush for three weeks, since his mother decided it was time, and he had the look of a young man who could feel her portrait watching from the hall.

"The barrels are late," he said, before anyone had said anything. "Did you know that? From Ironcrest. Forty new barrels, ordered in spring. Their forges are on short hours and their coopers are fighting with their carters, and I have a harvest of grapes and nothing to put it in."

"We came about the square," Bettany said.

"I know you came about the square." Idris spread his hands with the particular Sunplains grace that meant *I will now refuse you so politely that you will thank me for it.* "The honored committee must of course hold its accounting when the harvest permits. The Crush would never wish to inconvenience the honored committee. It is only that the Crush has kept its own week in that square since before either of our grandmothers, and the Crush would find it very difficult to explain to the city why it had been asked to press its grapes behind the mill."

"Nobody said behind the mill," Bettany said.

"Nobody needed to," said Idris. "Where else is there?"

There was nowhere else. Pell had known that before they came. The square was the only flat, paved space in the Vale large enough for either festival, and the mill yard behind the canal was mud from the first autumn rain until the last frost. Whichever festival lost the square would be held in the mud, and the Vale would discuss it at every wedding for twenty years.

"My mother's Crush," Idris said, more quietly, "fed four hundred people in the square. The year I was born she fed six hundred. I am not going to feed them in a mill yard." He hesitated, then added, in the plain register Sunplains people used at home, "And my own merchants won't touch your grain, Bettany. I asked. They say the new seed makes people ill. I told them that was nonsense. They said the price was falling anyway."

Bettany went very still. Pell watched her decide not to be offended by the only kind thing anyone had said to her all week.

On the way out, Pell stopped by the gate, where the gardener and the cook were arguing about whether a dog that slept in the courtyard belonged to the house or to the lane. He listened for a moment, asked when the dog had first come, asked who fed it in winter, and told them it belonged to whoever wanted to walk it on the mornings it rained. They both looked at him, then at each other, and the cook said she supposed that was fair.

"You're doing it again," Bettany said, outside.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You just ruled on a dog."

"I didn't rule," Pell said. "They agreed."

* * *

The miller billed them both for his patience.

It was on the walk to the mill that the trouble started, though at the time Pell thought it was the walk that was the trouble. Between the Salve gate and the canal there were three households, two orchards, a cart that had lost a wheel and a pair of sisters who did not agree about a hedge, and Pell had meant to pass all of them.

He did not pass the cart. The carter was blocking the lane and the man behind him wanted to go around through a garden, and the garden's owner was standing in it with a rake. It took Pell a minute to find out that the carter had a spare wheel, the man behind him had a strong back, and the garden's owner had been wanting the lane cleared for an hour. They changed the wheel together. The garden's owner held the horse.

He did not pass the sisters either. Their hedge had been planted by their father along a line he had drawn with a stick forty years ago, and neither of them had the stick. Pell asked which of them trimmed it. Both did, it turned out, from their own sides, to exactly the middle, and had for twenty years, without ever mentioning it to each other. Pell pointed this out. The sisters looked at the hedge as though seeing it for the first time and went inside, still arguing, but about something else.

The third dispute was about a pie. He would not afterward describe it.

The miller, Hobb, was waiting at the mill door with a slate. His wheel stood exactly halfway between the grain lanes and the orchard terraces, and his mill ground both barley and the crushed pips that went into the Salve cakes, and he had been caught between both festivals every year of his life.

"I've written it down," Hobb said, and showed them the slate. "Time spent listening to the committee. Time spent listening to the Salve house. Separate columns. I'll bill whichever of you loses."

"That's not how billing works," Bettany said.

"It is this year." Hobb looked at Pell. "So you've taken it, then."

"Taken what?"

"The case. Everyone says you've taken the case. Aurel says you've ruled on six things since breakfast."

"Three," Pell said. "And I didn't rule on any of them."

"Aurel counts the pie twice," Hobb said. "He says it was really two disputes."

Pell opened his mouth and found he could not in good conscience argue with that.

* * *

Six days before the festivals, both sides read the clause aloud in the square.

It had not been Pell's idea. Nothing that week had been Pell's idea. But the committee and the Salve house had each said the clause was on their side, and each had said the other side was misreading it, and half the Vale wanted to hear it read, so on a bright cold afternoon Bettany stood on the north steps with the Greenvale text and Idris stood on the south steps with the Sunplains text, and the town filled in between.

Aurel stood near the canal wall, reciting under his breath. Lissa sat on the well-head with a honey pastry. Pell stood at the back, near the lane, where a retired man could leave.

Bettany read first. The Greenvale was old and plain. *Each harvest kept the square in its own week.*

Idris read second. The Sunplains was later, and ornate in the way of documents written to be read aloud in courtyards. *Each festival shall hold the square in its appointed week, and neither shall trespass upon the other.*

"'Its appointed week,'" said somebody on the south side. "That's the Crush's week. It's appointed."

"'Its own week,'" said somebody on the north side. "Harvest Home's week is the week the harvest's in. That's its own."

It went on like that for a while. Pell was not listening to it. He was listening to the verb.

Greenvale speech did not let you say that something happened without saying what kind of happening it was: beginning, ongoing, recurring, finished, broken off, coming round with the season. It was the first thing children learned to get wrong. A thing that happened every year took one form. A thing that had happened once and was done took another. And the Greenvale clause, read aloud in Bettany's clear committee voice, did not say that each harvest *keeps* the square, the way the sun keeps rising. It said each harvest *kept* it, in the form for a thing done once, completely, and finished.

That was a description, not a rule. It was the kind of sentence you wrote in a record book to say what had happened in a particular year.

He looked across the square at Idris, reading his ornate translation, and thought that Sunplains had no neat way to carry that difference, and that some clerk long ago had made a finished thing into a standing law with one stroke of the pen, and had never known it.

He said nothing. Bettany saw his face anyway. She saw it from the north steps, across the whole square, and for a moment she looked hopeful, and then she saw him turn away, and looked furious instead.

"There's a ledger in the records room," Hobb said to him, at the edge of the crowd. "The old one. Nobody's opened it since my grandfather's time. It'll have the year in it, whatever year that clause came from."

"Then someone should open it," said Pell.

"You're someone."

"I'm retired," said Pell, and went home to his tree.

* * *

The bake-off was not officially part of either festival. It was an argument the Vale had every autumn and called a competition so nobody would have to admit it was an argument. Greenvale households brought orchard hand pies, crimped shut with the family pattern, filled with whatever fruit their trees had given; Sunplains households brought honey-drizzled nut pastries, layered thin as letters. A panel of three judges tasted everything and chose nothing, because choosing was not the point. The point was the conversation afterward about who had been robbed.

This year it was vicious. It was vicious in the Vale's way, which meant everyone was extremely polite. A Salve cousin said the hand pies were "very honest." A committee member said the honey pastries were "so ambitious." An old man said he preferred the pies of forty years ago, which had been made with lard, and three people turned on him at once.

Lissa ate one of each. She did it at the same time, one in each hand, a bite of pie and then a bite of pastry and then both together, and when she had finished she licked honey and fruit off her fingers and said to no one in particular, "They're better together. You should just make one with both."

Several adults heard her. None of them looked at each other.

Pell, who was supposed to be at home with his tree, stood by the judges' table and watched Bettany and Idris hear it. Bettany was across the table from Idris. For a moment each looked as though they would say something, and then each decided the other would say it first, and so nobody did.

That was when Pell decided. He had been an arbiter for forty years, and he knew the look of two people who had nearly reached the same thought and could not be the one to say it aloud.

* * *

The records room was at the top of the old weighhouse, up a stair that had been built for sacks rather than people. It smelled of dust and ink and the particular mildew of paper that has been kept dry for a hundred years by people who meant well. Pell had spent a good part of his working life there, and it had not missed him.

He asked Bettany and Idris to meet him there three days before the festivals. He did not say why. He let each of them believe the other had asked for the meeting, which was not quite a lie, and was the kind of thing he had done a hundred times on the water court and had never once felt entirely good about.

When they arrived he had the old ledger on the table. It was bound in board and leather, and two hands had written in it: Greenvale on the left page, Sunplains on the right.

"I'm not ruling," he said. "I want that clear. I'm retired. I'm going to go and look at my tree." He went to the door. "The ledger's from the right years. You'll want the section after the Convergence settlement. Aurel will bring you tea."

"Pell," said Bettany.

"Arbiter," said Idris.

He shut the door. He did not lock it; he had never locked a door on anyone in forty years. But he sat down on the top step outside it, which in a building with one stair came to much the same thing.

It took them two hours. He heard most of it through the door. First the stiff silence of two people each waiting for the other to read. Then pages. Then Bettany reading the Greenvale side aloud, slowly, and Idris reading the Sunplains, and both of them stopping at the same place.

"This is one festival," Idris said.

"It's one festival," said Bettany. "Look. 'The harvests of the Vale, grain and fruit, are brought to the square together, and accounted together, and eaten together.' That's every year. That's the recurring form. That's every year for — how far back does this go?"

Pages.

"And here," said Idris. "In the year after the settlement. An administrator's note. 'For the proper filing of the regional returns, the Greenvale harvest and the Sunplains harvest shall be separately recorded.' He needed two columns." A pause. "He split the festival so he could split the paperwork."

"And the clause—"

"Is from that year." Bettany's voice had gone strange. "'Each harvest kept the square in its own week.' That's what happened. That year. It's not a rule. It never was. It's a note in a ledger saying what they did."

"And in my language," said Idris slowly, "it became a law."

Through the door, Pell heard neither of them say anything for a long moment. He looked at his hands and thought about the quince.

Aurel came up the stair with a tray of tea. He saw Pell on the top step and stopped. "Is it going well?"

"I don't know," said Pell. "I'm not in there."

Aurel considered this. "North sluice at first bell," he said, very softly, and set the tray down beside Pell, and went back down.

When Bettany and Idris spoke again, they were no longer arguing about the square. That had been settled in the silence; Pell could hear it. They were arguing about the grain.

"If it's one festival, then it's one accounting," said Idris. "Your surplus is the Vale's surplus. Mine is too."

"My surplus is four granaries of grain nobody will buy."

"Then I will buy it."

"You can't afford it."

"No," said Idris. "Not easily. It will stretch the house. My mother will have things to say." A pause. "But she fed six hundred people. If the Crush and the Harvest are one festival, then this year the Salve house buys the Vale's grain at a fair price and gives it to every household in the square that asks. And the farms with seed loans pay them. And your land agent can go home."

The silence this time was different.

"No," Bettany said.

"No?"

"Not a gift. I won't have the Vale stand on its own steps and read out that Sunplains rescued it." Pell could hear her pacing, three steps each way, the length of the table. "A sale. At the price it would have fetched in a good year, not this one. Written in our ledger as a sale. The co-op keeps its own accounting. You do what you like with the grain once it's yours. If you want to give it away in the square, that's the Salve house's generosity, and the Vale will thank you for it. But it's bought."

"That is," said Idris, after a moment, "a great deal more expensive than a gift."

"Yes."

Another silence.

"My mother," said Idris, "would have loved you."

Pell stood up, knees complaining, picked up the tea tray, and knocked on the door as though he had just arrived.

* * *

On the day of the festival the square had both names, and so did the banners, because nobody had been able to agree which name went first and Idris had solved it by having them painted in a ring.

The first morning was awkward. People did not know where to stand. The Harvest Home families had always set up on the north side of the square and the Crush families on the south, and nobody wanted to be the first to move. The Salve cousins brought out the pressing troughs and found they had no barrels to press into. A committee member counted the benches twice and announced there were too few.

Then Bettany and Idris climbed the north steps together and read the surplus aloud.

They took turns. Bettany read what had come in, which was more than anyone living had seen. Idris read what had been bought, and by whom, and at what price, and Bettany added, in her committee voice, *sold* every time he said *bought*, and people laughed, because they could hear what was happening between the two of them even if they didn't understand it. And then Bettany read what would be given to every household that asked. For once, the public accounting of the Vale's surplus sounded like good news.

After that they ate it. That was the other thing Harvest Home had always been for. The grain the merchants had said was unsafe was boiled with apricots and honey and laid out on long tables down the middle of the square, and the whole Vale sat down and ate it in front of each other, and nobody was sick, and everybody knew nobody was sick, and by evening there were two downriver merchants at the edge of the crowd, looking thoughtful.

The barrels from Ironcrest arrived on the second day, forty of them on six carts with a carter who apologized all the way down the lane. The pressing began at noon.

Pell sat at the joint table, where people from both ends of town were learning to pass each other bowls. Beside him sat an old man he did not know, broad-shouldered, with a boatbuilder's hands and a Northwind accent that the Vale had not managed to soften.

"Too flat," the old man said, to nobody. "The whole country. You can't see anything coming."

"You're Mattie Scarth's father," said Pell, who knew everybody's son.

"I'm the Scarth from Kettle Cove," said the old man. "My son brought me here to rest. There's nothing to rest from." He looked around the square: the ring of names, the long tables, the barrels, the Salve cousins with their sleeves rolled up at the pressing troughs beside the committee's grain carts. "Two festivals at once," he said, "and they've eaten everything. I'm going to write to the cove about this. They won't believe it."

Late in the afternoon, the polite man from downriver found Pell.

He was exactly as Bettany had said: well dressed, soft-voiced, sorry about everything. He had visited the Thistle farm that morning and found that the Thistles had paid their seed loan in full the evening before, in coin, from the sale of their grain to the Salve house. He had visited the Orrins and found the same. He had not gone on to the Brays.

"I don't suppose," he said, "there's a seat."

There was a seat. Pell made one. The agent ate orchard grain and a hand pie and two honey pastries, and drank the first of the Crush's new wine, and after a while, without Pell asking, began to talk about his lenders.

They had tightened this year, the agent said; they were being pulled too. His house had borrowed to buy notes, and now it needed notes to pay what it had borrowed. He was not a bad man, he wanted Pell to understand. He was a link in a chain, and he was being pulled.

Pell listened. He did not want to. He had a quince tree. But after forty years he could not hear two parties in a mess without starting to look for the place where they might agree, and by the time the agent had finished his second glass Pell had asked him how many notes his house needed, and by when, and whether his lenders might extend terms against a smaller purchase at a fair price in a district that was actually selling.

"You should do this for a living," the agent said.

"I did," said Pell. "I stopped."

At dusk the grain went out. Every household that asked took home a sack, filled from the co-op's granary and marked with the Salve house's purchase stamp. Idris had had the stamp made that morning, in a hurry, and had not been able to decide which of the square's names went first. The sacks said COMMONS / PRESS YARD on one line, and PRESS YARD / COMMONS on the next, which the stamp-cutter considered a disgrace and everyone else considered correct. Sacks the households did not want went to the river barges, sold on for winter stores up the coast, where nobody would know what the stamp meant.

A cousin from the city, visiting for the Crush, stood at the joint table with a plate of orchard grain and hand pie and honey pastry and said, delighted, "Oh, this is that fusion food, isn't it? Greenvale and Sunplains together?"

Lissa, beside him, did not look up from her bowl. "We always eat it like this," she said.

* * *

Pell's quince tree got a visitor on the fourth morning after the festival. He saw her coming up the lane from the third branch: a young woman in the gray coat of the water court, carrying a folder, walking the way people walked when they had been told they would find someone in a tree.

"Arbiter Anwick?"

"Retired," said Pell.

"Yes, sir. Everyone said." She held up the folder. "It's the upper sluice and the Thistle orchard. It's a very small matter. They said to ask if you might give just one small opinion."

Pell looked at the quince in his hand. It had a small brown bruise on one side. He had decided, over four days, that it was the wasp's fault after all.

He sighed, and started to climb down.
