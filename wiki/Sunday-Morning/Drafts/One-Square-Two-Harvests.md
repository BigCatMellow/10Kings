## One Square, Two Harvests

"Pell!"

Pell Anwick didn't look down. Years ago he'd decided that a man up a tree didn't have to hear anything said below the second branch, and he'd waited forty years for the chance to find out if that was true.

"Pell Anwick, I know you can hear me. You've got your court face on."

He looked down. Bettany Corlew was planted under the quince tree with her fists on her hips and the committee ledger clamped under one arm. She'd chaired the Harvest Home committee for eleven years, and in all that time she'd never once had to shout up a tree.

"I'm retired," he said.

"I know you're retired. Everyone knows you're retired. You had a party."

"Then you know I'm busy." He held up the quince he'd been turning over for most of an hour. It had a small brown bruise on one side, and he was still working out whose fault that was, the tree's or a wasp's.

"The harvest's late."

"I'd noticed."

From the third branch he could see most of Harveston Vale: the grain lanes running north into the wet fields, the orchard terraces climbing south toward the dry country, the canal that fed both of them, and the square in the middle, where the lanes and the terraces met and pretended to get along. There were carts on every lane. There'd been carts on every lane for three weeks. Nobody in the Vale could remember a harvest taking three weeks to bring in, and Harvest Home was ten days off.

"It's late because it's big," Bettany said. "Biggest anyone's seen. So Harvest Home gets pushed back a week." She held the ledger up at him like he could read it from there. "Into the Crush's week."

At the far end of the square, the Salve house gate was already hung with vine garlands for the Wine Crush.

"Each festival gets the square in its own week," Bettany said. "That's the clause. Our week's whenever the harvest's in, and this year that's their week. I'm telling Idris Salve this afternoon, and he's going to say it's their square, and somebody's got to tell him he's wrong."

"Somebody."

"You've been telling people they're wrong for forty years."

"I've been getting people to agree for forty years," Pell said. "It's not the same thing. It's not even close."

Aurel the canal gatekeeper went by under the tree with his hands behind his back, reciting. Aurel recited the sluice schedule whenever a conversation anywhere near him went bad, and he'd obviously heard this one from the lane.

"North sluice opens at first bell, closes at third. South sluice at second, closes at fourth. Mill race on the half—"

"Morning, Aurel."

"Morning, Arbiter." He didn't slow down. "—on the half, except market days, when the mill race—"

"I'm not an arbiter anymore," Pell called after him, but Aurel was already round the corner.

Bettany waited just long enough to make sure he knew she was waiting, and then she left.

He stayed up the tree till his granddaughter came to tell him lunch was ready. Lissa was nine. She called the square the Commons in the morning and the Press Yard in the afternoon, and she'd never noticed she did it.

"Why do you always sit in that tree?" she asked on the way in.

"Because nobody can ask me anything up there."

Lissa thought about that. "I just did," she said, and went in to lunch.

---

The co-op granary was full to the rafters, and that was the problem.

"Listen to this," Bettany said, and read from the ledger. "'The co-op holds four times its usual surplus. Of this surplus, none has been sold.'" She shut the book. "Sounds like we're announcing a funeral."

Every Harvest Home, the committee stood on the steps of the square and read out the year: what had come in, what was stored, what was owed, and what would go to every household that asked. The Vale had been doing it longer than it had had a mill. Bettany had read it out for eleven years, and she was proud of it, the way people are proud of a thing they do well in front of everybody.

"A big harvest is good news," Pell said.

"When you can sell it. Everybody's got a big harvest this year and nobody's buying." She sat down on a sack of grain, which he'd never seen her do. "The river merchants won't even make an offer. Prices are falling, they say, wait a month. And then they say the other thing."

"What other thing?"

She dropped her voice as if the grain might be listening. "That the new seed isn't safe."

Lissa was sitting in the doorway with a bowl of orchard grain — barley cooked in last year's apricot syrup with a spoonful of sour cream on top — and eating it very slowly, like somebody who had no plans to be sent home.

"We planted the new strain last year," Bettany said. "From the Meadowcroft trials. That's why the harvest's so big. And now half the merchants on the river have heard it makes people sick."

"Has anyone been sick?"

"No."

"Does anyone know anyone who's been sick?"

"No. But they've heard."

Pell looked at the sacks, then at Lissa's bowl, which was last year's barley and not the new seed, and was relieved, and then annoyed at himself for it.

"And there's a man," Bettany said.

"There's always a man."

"Land agent from downriver. Very polite. He's been to the Thistles, and the Upcotts, and I think the Brays. The farms that took seed loans for the new strain and can't sell the grain to pay them back." She turned the ledger over in her lap. "He doesn't threaten anybody. He sits in their kitchens and tells them how sorry he is, and he offers to take the notes off their hands. And then he waits."

"Anyone sold?"

"Not yet," Bettany said, and then, after a while, like she was checking, "Not yet."

---

The Salve house saw everyone in its courtyard, because that was what the courtyard was for. It was tiled in blue and ochre, shaded by a vine older than anybody in the Vale, and built less for sitting in than for being seen sitting in. Idris Salve sat in it badly. He was twenty-three and had been running the Crush for three weeks, ever since his mother decided it was time, and he looked like a young man who could feel her watching him from an upstairs window.

"The barrels are late," he said, before anybody else had said a word. "Did you know that? Forty barrels from Ironcrest, ordered in the spring. Their coopers are fighting with their carters, and I've got a harvest of grapes and nothing to put it in."

"We came about the square," Bettany said.

"I know you came about the square." He spread his hands in that Sunplains way that meant he was about to turn her down so politely she'd thank him for it. "The honored committee must of course hold its accounting when the harvest permits. The Crush would never wish to inconvenience the honored committee. It's only that the Crush has kept its own week in that square since before either of our grandmothers, and the Crush would find it very difficult to explain to the city why it was pressing grapes behind the mill."

"Nobody said anything about the mill."

"Nobody had to. Where else is there?"

There wasn't anywhere else, and all three of them knew it. The mill yard was mud from the first autumn rain till the last frost. Whichever festival lost the square would end up in the mud, and the Vale would bring it up at every wedding for the next twenty years.

"My mother fed four hundred people in that square," Idris said. "The year I was born she fed six hundred. I am not feeding them in a mill yard."

Then he dropped the courtyard voice and talked the plain way Sunplains families talk at home, and he looked a lot younger doing it.

"And my merchants won't touch your grain, Bettany. I asked. They say the new seed makes people ill. I told them that was nonsense." He looked up at the vine. "They said the price was falling anyway."

Bettany went very still. Pell watched her decide not to take offense at the only kind thing anybody had said to her all week.

At the gate on the way out, the Salves' gardener and cook were arguing about a dog. It slept in the courtyard and ate in the lane, and they couldn't agree whose it was. Pell asked when it had first turned up, and who fed it in winter, and once they'd both admitted they each fed it in winter without telling the other, he suggested it belonged to whoever walked it on mornings when it rained. They looked at each other, and the cook said she supposed that was fair.

"You just ruled on a dog," Bettany said outside.

"I didn't rule. They agreed."

---

He meant to walk straight to the mill. He didn't get very far.

There was a cart with a broken wheel blocking the lane, a man behind it who wanted to cut around through somebody's garden, and the woman who owned the garden standing in it with a rake. It took Pell about a minute to find out that the carter had a spare wheel, the man behind him had shoulders like a door, and the woman with the rake had been wanting the lane clear for an hour. They changed the wheel together. The woman held the horse.

Then there were the sisters and the hedge. Their father had planted it along a line he'd drawn with a stick forty years ago, and neither of them had the stick. Pell asked who trimmed it. Turned out they both did, each from her own side, exactly to the middle, and had for twenty years without either one ever mentioning it. The sisters looked at the hedge like they'd never seen it before and went back inside, still arguing, but about something else.

The third one was about a pie. He never did tell anyone about the pie.

Hobb the miller was waiting in the mill doorway with a slate. His wheel sat exactly halfway between the grain lanes and the orchard terraces, he ground for both festivals, and he'd been stuck between them every autumn of his life.

"I've been keeping count," Hobb said. The slate had two columns, *Committee* and *Salve house*, and a lot of marks under both. "Time spent listening. I'll bill whichever of you loses."

"That's not how billing works," Bettany said.

"It is this year." Hobb looked at Pell. "So you've taken the case, then."

"I haven't taken anything."

"Aurel says you've ruled on six things since breakfast."

"Three," said Pell. "And I didn't rule on any of them."

"Aurel counts the pie twice. Says it was really two."

Pell opened his mouth, thought about the pie, and shut it again. He couldn't honestly say Aurel was wrong.

---

Six days out, both sides read the clause out loud in the square.

It wasn't Pell's idea. Nothing that week was Pell's idea. But half the Vale wanted to hear it, so on a bright, cold afternoon Bettany stood on the north steps with the Greenvale text and Idris stood on the south steps with the Sunplains text, and the town packed in between. Aurel stood by the canal wall, reciting under his breath. Lissa sat on the well-head eating a honey pastry. Pell stood at the back by the lane, where a retired man could leave whenever he liked.

Bettany read first, in plain old Greenvale. *Each harvest kept the square in its own week.*

Idris read second. The Sunplains version was newer, and fancy, the way things get when they're meant to be read out in courtyards. *Each festival shall hold the square in its appointed week, and neither shall trespass upon the other.*

"'Appointed week'!" somebody shouted from the south side. "That's the Crush's week!"

"'Its own week'!" somebody shouted back from the north. "Harvest Home's week is whenever the harvest's in!"

It went on like that for a while. Pell wasn't listening to any of it. He was listening to the verb.

*Kept.* Not *keeps*.

He didn't say a thing. Bettany saw his face anyway, all the way across the square, and for a second she looked hopeful. Then he turned away, and she looked furious instead.

"Both lots keep their old banners up in my loft," Hobb said at his elbow. "Always have. Two chests. Each lot swears theirs is the original, and neither's unrolled theirs in my lifetime, in case it turns out to be moth." He sniffed. "I charge them both for the space."

"Then somebody should unroll them."

"You're somebody."

"I'm retired," said Pell, and went home to his tree.

---

The bake-off wasn't officially part of either festival. "It's an argument," Lissa had explained to him that morning, very patiently, "but they call it a competition so nobody has to say it's an argument."

Orchard hand pies stood on one table, crimped with each family's pattern. Honey-drizzled nut pastries stood on the other, layered thin as letter paper. Three judges tasted everything and picked nothing, because picking wasn't the point. The point was deciding afterward who'd been robbed.

This year it got vicious, in a polite sort of way. A Salve cousin said the hand pies were "very honest." A committee member said the honey pastries were "so ambitious." An old man said he liked the pies from forty years ago better, back when they used lard, and three people turned on him at once.

Lissa ate one of each. She ate them at the same time, one in each hand — a bite of pie, a bite of pastry, then both together — and when she was done she licked the honey and fruit off her fingers and announced to the whole table, "You should just make one with both."

Several people heard her. None of them looked at each other.

Across the judges' table, Bettany and Idris had both heard it. They both opened their mouths, and each of them waited for the other to go first, so neither did. Pell had been watching that happen for forty years.

That was when he decided.

---

Three days out, he got them both up into Hobb's loft.

He did it the way he'd done it a hundred times on the water court. He let Bettany think Idris had asked to meet her at the mill and let Idris think it was Bettany, and neither of those was quite a lie, and in forty years he'd never felt completely good about it.

The loft ran the length of the mill under the roof beams, and it smelled of flour dust and old rope and the sweetish rot of apples somebody had forgotten in a corner. Two chests stood at the far end, a yard apart, as if whoever set them down hadn't wanted them touching. One had a sheaf of wheat burned into the lid. The other had a vine.

"I'm not ruling," Pell said from the top of the ladder. "I want that understood. I'm retired. Hobb says your old banners have been in those chests since before any of us were born, and somebody ought to look at them before the festival in case the moth's got at them." He started back down. "Aurel will bring you tea."

"Pell—" said Bettany.

"Arbiter—" said Idris.

He didn't take the ladder away. He'd never trapped anybody anywhere in forty years. He just sat down on the third rung from the top with his back to the hatch, which, in a loft with one ladder, came to about the same thing.

It took them two hours. He heard most of it through the hatch: the stiff quiet of two people each waiting for the other to go first, then a lid creaking, then another, then cloth being unrolled across the boards, a lot of cloth, and then nothing at all for a long time.

"Yours is cut," Idris said.

"So's yours."

More cloth. Somebody dragging something heavy across the boards. Then Bettany's voice, very quiet.

"Put it there. No, the other way. Line up the wheat."

Pell had never seen either banner, but he could picture this one without climbing up. Grain on one half and grapes on the other, a border running all the way round, and along the top, stitched in both languages, one sentence neither half could finish on its own.

"'The harvests of the Vale, grain and fruit,'" Bettany read, "'are brought to the square together—'"

"'—and accounted together, and eaten together,'" said Idris.

"That's the every-year form," said Bettany. "Not *were brought*. *Are*. Every year."

"There's a tag." Bettany again. "Sewn on the cut edge. Mine says *Greenvale return*. There's a year."

"Mine says *Sunplains return*," said Idris. "Same year." A pause. "That's the year after the settlement. When they started filing the regional returns." A longer pause. "Somebody needed two columns. So they cut the festival in half and gave us each a half to file."

"And the clause." Bettany's voice had gone strange. "The committee copy's got a year written at the top. I've read that clause out eleven times and never once looked at the year." A pause. "It's this one. 'Each harvest kept the square in its own week.' That's what they did, that one year. It isn't a rule. It was never a rule."

"And in my language," Idris said slowly, "it became one."

For a long time neither of them said anything.

Aurel came in at the mill door with a tray of tea and stopped at the bottom of the ladder when he saw Pell sitting on it.

"Is it going well?"

"Couldn't tell you. I'm not in there."

Aurel thought about that. "North sluice at first bell," he said, very quietly, and handed the tray up to Pell and went back out.

When the voices started up again, they weren't arguing about the square anymore. That had got settled somewhere in the quiet; Pell could hear it. They were arguing about the grain.

"If it's one festival, then it's one accounting," Idris said. "Your surplus is the Vale's surplus."

"My surplus is four granaries' worth of grain nobody will buy."

"Then I'll buy it."

"You can't afford it."

"No. Not easily. It'll stretch the house, and my mother's going to have things to say about it." A pause. "But she fed six hundred people. So this year the Salve house buys the Vale's grain at a fair price and gives it to every household in the square that asks. The farms pay off their seed loans. And your polite land agent can go home."

This time the silence was different.

"No," said Bettany.

"No?"

He could hear her pacing, three steps each way along the boards. "Not a gift. I'm not standing on my own steps and reading out to the whole Vale that Sunplains rescued us. A sale. At a good year's price, not this year's. Written in our ledger as a sale, and the co-op keeps its own books. What you do with the grain once it's yours is your business. If you want to give it away in the square, that's the Salve house being generous, and the Vale will thank you for it. But it's *bought*."

"That," Idris said after a moment, "is a great deal more expensive than a gift."

"Yes."

Another silence.

"My mother," said Idris, "is going to love you."

Pell climbed the last three rungs, knees complaining, and put his head up through the hatch with the tea tray as if he'd only just got there.

Bettany was kneeling on the boards with one hand flat on the seam where the two halves met. She looked at the tray, then at the ladder where he'd so obviously been sitting for two hours, then at him.

"You lied to get us up here," she said.

"I didn't lie."

"You arranged."

He held out the tray. After a moment she took a cup and drank from it, and made it very clear she'd be bringing this up again sometime in the next twenty years.

---

On festival day the square had both names. The old banner hung over the well-head in the middle, stitched back together along the cut by three committee women and two Salve cousins in one long evening. You could see the seam, and nobody minded.

The first morning was awkward. Harvest Home families had always set up on the north side and Crush families on the south, and nobody wanted to be the first to move. The Salve cousins wheeled out the pressing troughs and then remembered there weren't any barrels. Somebody counted the benches twice and announced there weren't enough.

Then Bettany and Idris climbed the north steps together and read out the surplus.

They took turns. Bettany read what had come in, which was more than anybody alive had ever seen. Idris read what had been bought, and by who, and for how much, and every time he said *bought*, Bettany said *sold* in her committee voice. People laughed and couldn't have told you why.

Then they ate it. The grain the river merchants said wasn't safe got boiled up with apricots and honey and laid out on long tables down the middle of the square, and the whole Vale sat down and ate it in front of each other. Nobody got sick. Everybody knew nobody got sick. By evening there were two downriver merchants standing at the edge of the crowd, looking thoughtful.

The barrels from Ironcrest showed up on the second day, forty of them on six carts, with a carter who apologized the whole way down the lane.

Pell sat at the joint table, where people from both ends of town were learning to pass each other bowls. Next to him sat an old man he didn't know, broad in the shoulders, with a boatbuilder's hands and a Northwind accent the Vale hadn't managed to wear down.

"Too flat," the old man said, to nobody. "The whole country."

"You're Mattie Scarth's father," said Pell, who knew everybody's son.

"I'm the Scarth from Kettle Cove. My boy brought me here to rest." He looked around the square: the stitched banner, the long tables, the Salve cousins with their sleeves rolled up at the troughs next to the committee's grain carts. "Two festivals at once, and they've eaten everything," he said. "I'm going to write to the cove about this. They won't believe it."

Late in the afternoon the polite man from downriver found Pell.

He was exactly how Bettany had described him: a good coat, a soft voice, sorry about everything. He'd gone out to the Thistle farm that morning and found out the Thistles had paid off their seed loan the night before, in coin, from selling their grain to the Salve house. He'd gone on to the Upcotts and found the same. He hadn't bothered with the Brays.

"I don't suppose," he said, "there's a seat?"

Pell made room. The agent ate orchard grain and a hand pie and two honey pastries, drank the first of the new Crush wine, and after a while, without anybody asking, started talking about his lenders, who'd tightened up this year and were leaning on him in turn. Two valleys over, he said, not unkindly, half the farms had sold to him since the harvest, and he'd bought them lunch too.

"I'm not a bad man," he said. "I'd like you to understand that."

Pell listened. He didn't want to. He had a quince tree. But after forty years he couldn't hear two sides of a mess without looking for where they might meet, and by the second glass he'd asked how many notes the agent's house actually needed, and by when, and whether his lenders might give him longer if he bought less, at a fair price, somewhere that was actually selling.

The agent put his glass down. "You should do this for a living."

"I did," said Pell. "I stopped."

At dusk the grain went out. Every household that asked got a sack filled from the co-op granary and marked with the Salve house's purchase stamp. Idris had had the stamp cut that morning in a hurry and couldn't decide which name went first, so the sacks read COMMONS / PRESS YARD on one line and PRESS YARD / COMMONS on the next. The stamp-cutter thought it was a disgrace. Aurel read one of the sacks, said it was correct, and went back to his sluice schedule. Whatever the households didn't want went down to the river barges to be sold on for winter stores up the coast, where nobody would know what the stamp meant.

On the second evening a young woman in the gray coat of the water court found him at the joint table with a folder under her arm, and asked if the arbiter might give just one small opinion on the upper sluice and the Thistle orchard. It was a very small matter, she said. Pell said he was retired. Then he asked her who'd dug the upper channel, and when, and whether anybody had asked the Thistles, and by the time the barrels were broached the matter was even smaller than she'd said, and she went off looking a bit cheated.

A cousin from the city, down for the Crush, stood at the joint table with a plate of orchard grain and hand pie and honey pastry and said, delighted, "Oh, this is that fusion food, isn't it? Greenvale and Sunplains together?"

Lissa didn't look up from her bowl. "We always eat it like this."

---

Four mornings after the festival, Pell was back up his quince tree.

The quince in his hand had a small brown bruise on one side. It had taken him four days, but he'd decided it was the wasp's fault after all.

Nobody came up the lane. Nobody came all morning. The water court had nothing for him, the committee had nothing for him, and Bettany, who was talking to him again, was busy counting sacks at the co-op with Idris Salve, who was apparently also counting sacks at the co-op. He had the whole tree and the whole morning to himself, which was exactly what he'd wanted for forty years.

He climbed down twice before noon to see if anyone was coming.

The second time, the Salves' dog was asleep at the bottom of the tree, and it had just started to rain, which by his own ruling made it his morning to walk it. He went to find a length of rope.
