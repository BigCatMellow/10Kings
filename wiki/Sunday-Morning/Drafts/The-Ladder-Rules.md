## The Ladder Rules

"Rung," said Yusa. "Rung. Rung. Left foot, you're on the edge. Rung."

Her father went up the practice ladder the way he went up every ladder, like it had said something rude about his mother. The ladder leaned on the lamp yard wall, where there wasn't a lamp, because it was the practice ladder and the wall was for practicing on. Yusa had both hands on the stiles and one foot on the bottom rung, and she could feel every step he took come down through the wood into her arms.

"I know where my left foot is," Dov said.

"It's on the edge."

He moved it. "It was fine."

"It's fine now." She leaned her weight in. Thirty-one rungs on the long ladders, twenty-six on the short. This was a short. "Rung. Top. Hold."

He held. He hung there at the top of the practice ladder with his arm up as if there was a lamp to light, and his knee made the noise it had been making since the start of the cold season, and he pretended it hadn't.

All round the yard the other lighters were doing the same. Forty-four lighters in Vasselin, eleven rounds, a long ladder and a short to each round, racked along the back wall with the round's mark burned into the side. Yusa knew them all. She knew the lamps too. Two hundred and six on the eastern round alone, if you counted the three at the bathhouse that hadn't been lit since the cistern dropped, which she did, because they were still lamps.

What nobody in the yard was doing was saying the word. Not today. Not on any day until the first lamp of the night had taken. You could say *lamp*, you could say *oil*, you could say *wick* and *flame* if you were careful how you said them, but you didn't say the other word before it was earned. Old Fesse on the market round had said it once, out loud, years ago, before the first lamp, and that very night he'd fallen off a ladder in the spice lane and broken his wrist. Everyone knew that story. Yusa had heard it about four hundred times.

"All right," said Qadir Lemm.

He'd come out of the guild room with a paper in his hand, and the yard went quiet. Qadir was master of the lighters' guild and looked it: tall, gray in the beard, with a way of standing that made people straighten up near him without knowing they were doing it. He wasn't a showy man. He just didn't do anything by halves.

"You've all heard," he said. "I'll say it once so it's said. The assembly's taken the cities' new standard. Two to a ladder. One climbs, one foots. After what happened in the south, I don't argue with that. A man died." He looked round the yard. "But the assembly pays one wage a ladder. It's always paid one. It's paying one this year, and it's cut the oil because the store's low, and it's asked us to keep the standard on top of that." He folded the paper. "So we've told the warden. Pay for the second man, or we stand down for the Lanterns."

The yard stayed quiet.

"Three days," Qadir said. "They've got three days."

"The Nazar rule," somebody muttered behind Yusa. "That diplomat's always on a barge somewhere, agreeing things."

"Fine thing to agree," somebody else said. "When you're not the one paying."

Yusa looked up at her father, still hanging at the top of the practice ladder with his arm in the air.

"You can come down now," she said.

---

The warden received them on the assembly steps in the middle of the afternoon. The steps were where the warden received people.

Rafiq Daunt was a narrow man in a beautiful coat. He stood two steps up, which was a rule, and Qadir stood at the bottom with six lighters behind him, which was also a rule, and Yusa was at the back of the six because her father had been told to come and she'd come because he had. Behind them the cistern square was doing its business: water-sellers, a woman with a cart of oranges, children doing nothing in particular as loudly as they could.

"The honored guild is welcome," Rafiq said. "The assembly holds the guild in the highest esteem. The assembly would never ask the guild to climb a ladder unsafely."

"Then pay for the second man," said Qadir.

"The assembly is deeply conscious of the second man." Rafiq inclined his head. "The assembly has every confidence that the guild, which has lit this city since before the walls, will find a way to honor the standard it so wisely supports."

"With what?"

"With the resourcefulness for which the guild is famous."

Qadir didn't move. "The guild is famous," he said, in a voice like a door shutting, "for not falling off ladders. It would like to stay famous for that. The guild has every confidence that the assembly, which agreed the standard so quickly, will find the money to keep it just as quickly."

"The assembly's resources are, regrettably, not what the guild's admiration might suppose."

"The guild's admiration is entirely at the assembly's disposal. The guild's ladders aren't."

They kept at it for a good quarter of an hour. Yusa had never heard anything like it. Nobody said no. Nobody said anything remotely like no. Every sentence was a compliment, and every compliment was a door being shut, very gently, in the other man's face. By the end of it Rafiq had thanked the guild four times and Qadir had praised the assembly's wisdom twice, and the lamps were exactly as dark as they'd been when they started.

On the way back to the yard her father said, "Well. That was the first day."

"Two more," said Yusa.

"Thank you. I can count."

"I know you can count. I'm just saying it."

---

They ate on the roof that night, because her mother had gone to her sister's for the festival and the house was too quiet with two.

Flatbread and oil, a bowl of the soft cheese, and a clay pot of chickpeas and lamb that Dov had put in the bread oven at the corner that morning and carried home under his coat. He was proud of it. It had too much cumin and he was proud of that too. They sat on the low wall with the pot between them and ate with their fingers and the bread, and below them the eastern round went dark, lane by lane, as the evening came down.

Not dark-dark. There were windows, a few. Somebody's household lantern already in a courtyard, practicing. But the lamps up on the walls on their iron arms stayed black. Two hundred and six of them on this round, give or take the bathhouse three, and she could have pointed to every one.

For a good while neither of them bothered to talk. The cumin was doing most of it.

"Your knee," she said eventually.

"My knee's fine."

"Your knee's been making that noise since the first frost."

"It's a knee. It makes noises." He tore a piece of bread in half and didn't eat either half. "It'll do the round."

"And the great lamp?"

He didn't answer. The great lamp was over the assembly steps, the highest lamp in Vasselin and the last one lit on Lantern night, and it had been his for longer than she'd been alive. It took the longest ladder in the yard, forty rungs, and it went up a wall with nothing to lean on at the top but the lamp arm.

"I've never climbed," Yusa said. "You know that."

"I know."

"I'm not saying it like it's a problem. Footing's the real job. Anybody can go up a ladder. It's keeping it on the ground that matters."

"I know," her father said again. He ate one of the halves of bread. "You say that a lot."

"Because it's true."

"I didn't say it wasn't." He looked out at the dark round. "I've never once asked you to go up, Yusa."

"I know."

"Well." He reached for the pot. "Just so it's said."

---

The guild's ledger was a big book with a leather strap that lived in the guild room on a stand, and nobody touched it but Qadir and the clerk. Yusa touched it the next morning anyway, while the clerk was at the privy, because she'd been awake half the night with a thought and the thought wouldn't leave.

There were the rounds. There were the lighters. Forty-four names, written in a good hand, with their round and their lamps and their wage. Her father's name was there. Fesse's name was there, with a little note in the margin about the wrist.

She went through it twice. Then she went across the cistern square to the assembly's lamp office, where a junior clerk who'd been at the market school with her let her look at the city's book, because he didn't think a footer could do any harm with it.

It was the same. Forty-four lighters. Forty-four wages. Lamps by round, oil by round.

Not one footer. Not in either book. Not a name, not a wage, not a mark. As far as the guild and the city of Vasselin were concerned, every lighter in the city had gone up every ladder every night for a hundred years with nobody holding the bottom.

She stood in the lamp office looking at the page, and for a moment she was angrier than she'd been in her life, and she didn't know who at.

Then she borrowed a pen from the clerk and a sheet of paper from his desk, and wrote *Eastern round* at the top of it, and under that, *Dov Rennet, lighter: Yusa Rennet, footer.* And she went out to count.

---

Some of it was easy. She knew the eastern round. She knew who footed for who: Mehri Ash's boy for his mother, the Sabbagh twins for each other, turn and turn about, old Wahid's wife for twenty-odd years before her hips went and his nephew after. She went door to door down the lanes with her sheet of paper, and people told her, and she put it on the sheet, and most of them looked at her as if she'd asked them their own name.

"You want to know who holds the ladder?" said Mehri Ash. "Why?"

"Because it's not written anywhere."

Mehri Ash thought about that. "It's not, is it," she said, and went and got her son out of bed to tell Yusa himself.

By noon the eastern round was done: eight lighters, eight footers, every one named. She took it back to the lamp yard, and Qadir Lemm read it twice over with his beard in his hand, and then he called four of the younger lighters over and gave each of them a sheet of paper and a round.

"Every lighter," he said. "Every footer. Last year. Names."

"Why?" said one of them.

Qadir looked at Yusa. "Because nobody ever asked," he said.

The other rounds took the rest of that day and most of the next. People were out, people were at work, people had moved and their footers had moved with them. The market round had a footer nobody could find for half a day, until she turned up at the lamp yard on her own to ask why everyone was looking for her. The harbor round had one lighter who swore he'd never had a footer in his life, and his wife came round the corner while he was saying it and gave him a look, and he remembered.

And then there was the oil.

Even with a deal, the store was short. Yusa had seen the city's book: the lamp oil was down by a third, because every household that could afford it had been buying oil all autumn and putting it away against the drought, and the merchants had sold the city what was left at a price the assembly wouldn't pay. Which meant that even if every footer in Vasselin were paid in gold, a third of the lamps would stay dark.

Unless somebody sold.

Zeyna had been an oil-seller's widow for nine years and an oil-seller for longer than that, and she had a cellar under her house in the oil lane that everybody on the eastern round knew about and nobody had been allowed into since the summer. People called her a hoarder, mostly not to her face. When Yusa knocked, she opened the door about a hand's width.

"If you're from the assembly," Zeyna said, "they can buy oil like everybody else."

"I'm not from the assembly."

"If you're from the guild, the same."

"I'm a footer," said Yusa. "Dov Rennet's girl."

Zeyna looked at her. "So you are." The door opened another hand. "What do you want, then?"

Yusa had come to tell her the city needed oil, and that it was her civic duty, and a lot of other things she'd been working up on the walk over. She didn't say any of them. She looked past Zeyna into the dark hall, and the jars along the wall, big ones, sealed, and she said, "How much have you got?"

"Why?"

"Because the store's short by about a third. And I don't think anybody's asked you to sell it. I think they've only called you names."

Zeyna stood in her doorway for a while.

"Nobody's asked," she said. "No."

"Would you?"

"At a fair price." Zeyna's mouth went sideways. "Not the price they're paying. Not the price they're charging in the market either, mind you. A fair one. I'm not a thief." She opened the door the whole way. "Come and count, then. You're the one who counts, aren't you? The whole lane knows. Dov's girl, going round with a bit of paper."

---

On the third day, the afternoon of the longest night, the deputation went up to the assembly steps again.

It was a bigger deputation this time. It had Qadir at the front and six lighters behind him, and behind them it had Yusa with a stack of paper in her hands, eleven sheets, one for every round. Behind her, for no reason anybody had organized, it had about fifty footers who'd heard what she was doing and wanted to see.

Rafiq Daunt came down to his step and looked at all of them.

"The honored guild has brought company," he said.

"The guild has brought its footers," said Qadir. "The assembly was concerned about the second man. Here he is." He turned and held his hand out, and after a moment Yusa understood he meant her, and came forward.

She hadn't planned to speak. She'd thought Qadir would. But Qadir was standing aside, and Rafiq was looking at her, and she held out the papers.

"Forty-four lighters," she said. "Forty-four footers. Every lighter in Vasselin had one last year, and the year before. Some of them for twenty years. They're all on here, by name. They're not in your book and they're not in the guild's." She swallowed. "You've had two to a ladder the whole time. You just never paid the second one."

Rafiq took the papers. He went through them, one sheet at a time, the whole eleven, while the square waited and the woman with the orange cart got bored and went home. When he got to the eastern round he stopped for a moment at the top of the sheet.

"Dov Rennet," he read. "Yusa Rennet."

"Yes."

He handed the papers back to her. Then he looked at Qadir, and there it was on his face: a man finding the way out of a corner and deciding to take it.

"The assembly," Rafiq said, "is delighted to discover that the honored guild has always kept the standard. The assembly could not, of course, find a second wage in three days without a vote, and a vote would take a season." He paused. "But the assembly's lamp store gives a gift at the Lanterns, by custom, to the households of the guild. It's always been a small gift. The warden may give it without a vote." He tilted his head at the paper in Yusa's hands. "The warden sees no reason why the households of the footers should not be counted among the households of the guild. A lantern filled from the city's store, for each one. This year and every year."

Qadir considered this. "For the households of the footers."

"By name."

"And the oil to fill them? The store's short."

"The store," said Rafiq, "is regrettably short."

"It isn't," said Yusa, before she could stop herself. "Not anymore. Zeyna in the oil lane will sell. At a fair price. I've got the count." She held up a twelfth sheet, the one with the jars on it.

There was a pause on the assembly steps.

"The honored widow Zeyna," said Rafiq Daunt slowly, "is to be thanked for her civic generosity."

"She'd rather be paid," said Yusa.

"She will be both," said the warden, and for the first time in three days he very nearly smiled.

---

The first lamp took at sundown, on the market round, and somebody shouted it across the rooftops, and the city could talk again.

"*Light holds!*"

"*Light holds!*"

It went round from round to round, lighter to lighter, a shout on every roof. Yusa footed her father down the eastern round with her count going under her breath. Lamp one, by the bathhouse gate. "*Light holds!*" Lamp two. Lamp three. The courtyards were full of household lanterns coming out, paper and brass and glass, children carrying them on sticks, grandmothers carrying them in both hands as if they might spill. Every time the ladder went up, people stopped to watch, and every time her father called it, they cheered, a bit, the way people cheer when they've had a cup of something.

Lamp sixty. Lamp sixty-one. She talked him up every ladder. *Rung. Rung. Left foot.* He let her. Lamp a hundred and twelve, outside the bakery, where the baker came out and gave them each a hot roll without saying a thing about it. Lamp a hundred and ninety. The three at the bathhouse, which she counted, and which had oil in them again, the first since the cistern dropped, because Zeyna had insisted on it.

Two hundred and six.

By the time they got to the cistern square the whole city was lit except the one lamp that mattered most, and everyone in Vasselin who could walk had come to the square to see it done.

The great lamp hung over the assembly steps on its iron arm, forty rungs up a bare wall. The long ladder was waiting for it, set and footed, by the lighters who'd brought it. Her father looked at the ladder, and then at his knee, and then at Yusa.

"No," he said. "Not this year. I'll be halfway up and it'll go."

The square went quiet, which in a square that full is a very particular sound.

Then, slowly, people started looking at Yusa. It came at her like heat off an oven. Dov Rennet's girl. Forty years of the great lamp, and the girl who'd held his ladder for the last twenty.

"No," she said.

Somebody laughed, uncertainly.

"No," she said again. Her face was hot. "I've never climbed. I'm not starting forty rungs up in front of everyone." She heard herself, and for once she didn't say the rest of it. She didn't say footing was the real job. She just stood there.

Nobody moved. The lamp hung dark.

Then Rafiq Daunt came down the assembly steps. He came all the way down, past his step, to the bottom where the deputations stood, and took off his beautiful coat and folded it over his arm and looked around for somewhere to put it. A water-seller took it from him, astonished.

"The warden," said Rafiq, "agreed the standard. The warden would be honored to be seen keeping it." He looked up at the ladder. He was a narrow man in a white shirt now, and he looked smaller without the coat. "The warden has never climbed a ladder in his life."

"It's forty rungs," said Qadir.

"So I understand."

He looked at Yusa.

She stepped up to the ladder and put both hands on the stiles and one foot on the bottom rung, and leaned her weight in, and felt the whole long ladder settle against the wall, true.

"Go on, then," she said.

He went. He went up the first ten rungs too fast, the way people do when they're trying not to think, and she said "Slower," and he went slower. At twenty he stopped.

She felt it in her arms. She'd felt it before, years ago, once or twice, with apprentices. The whole ladder went still in a different way.

"Don't look down," she called up. "Look at the wall. Look at the stone in front of you. Left hand, rung. That's it. Right foot. Rung."

He didn't move.

"Warden," she said. "You're halfway. It's the same up as down from here, and up's got a lamp at the end of it."

Somebody in the crowd laughed, but kindly.

"Rung," said Yusa. "Rung. I've got the bottom. I've always got the bottom. Rung."

He went. Twenty-five. Thirty. She talked him up each one, the way she'd talked her father up every lamp on the eastern round, and the square was so quiet she could hear the oil sloshing in the can on his belt. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine.

"Top," she said. "Hold."

He held. He got the can off his belt somehow, and the taper, and he reached out over the drop to the great lamp, and his hand shook, and the wick caught.

It caught, and took, and the great lamp of Vasselin came up gold over the assembly steps, and the whole square let its breath out at once.

By custom the lighter calls it. Rafiq Daunt was hanging on to the top of a forty-rung ladder with both arms and his eyes shut and wasn't calling anything.

Yusa looked up at the lamp, and the warden under it, and the ladder running down to her hands, where it had always been.

"*Light holds!*"
