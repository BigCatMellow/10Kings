## The Mushrooms From Nowhere

The last forty steps of the stair were the worst. That was where the light started.

Xenna Dree had been climbing since what would have been midnight up top. Down in Underwell there wasn't any midnight; there was the long lamp and the short lamp and the time the water in the spring went cold. But she'd counted, and it would be about midnight, and she'd been climbing since then: up the long gallery from the spring, through the dry passage, round the long way where the deep road used to be before the shake brought it down, and then the stair. Two mules behind her on the lead, loaded with crates, grumbling. Her lamp in her hand. Her hat on her head.

And then, forty steps from the top, the dark started going gray.

She'd known it would. She'd come up this stair as a girl, three or four times, holding her father's coat. She remembered the gray. She'd forgotten what came after it.

At the top there was a door. It was the stair-hut door, heavy wood on iron, and it was shut, and when she put her shoulder to it and it groaned open, the light came in like somebody had thrown a bucket of it in her face.

She shut her eyes. It didn't help. It came in round the edges, red, through her eyelids. She pulled her hat brim down as far as it would go and stood in the doorway of the stair-hut with her eyes screwed shut and the tears running out of them, and behind her the mules stamped and blew, and out in front of her somebody laughed.

"First time up?" said a voice.

"No," said Xenna.

"Could have fooled me."

She didn't answer. She stood there and let the light hurt, and waited for it to stop, and it didn't, quite. It just turned into an ache behind her eyes that she'd be carrying round for days.

Udo should have been doing this. Udo had done it for fifteen years: brought Underwell's crop up the deep road and the stair every few weeks, sold it in the Ninewell market and at the fairs, bought oil and grain and salt and lamp-glass, and gone back down. Udo knew everyone up here. Udo knew what things cost and what people meant when they said them. Udo didn't mind the light. And when anybody up top asked Udo where his mushrooms came from, Udo said *below*, the same as anybody from Underwell would.

But the shake in the spring had brought the deep road down, a long stretch of it, somewhere under the saddle. Udo had been on the far side of it at the time, which was lucky, and he'd stayed there, working the other routes round, and sent word back hand to hand that he was alive and fine and couldn't get through. Which meant that if Underwell's mushrooms were going to be sold at the late-summer fair, and Underwell's oil bought for the winter, then somebody else had to bring them up.

And nobody else had wanted to. And Xenna had the biggest beds.

---

The fair was along the saddle road outside the west gate, and it was the loudest thing Xenna had ever been near.

There were stalls all along both sides of the road, a mile of them. Cloth and pots and knives and rope. Goats. Spices in heaps. Caravan people in long coats, plateau people in short ones, people from places she'd never heard of, all shouting. And over all of it the sky, which was so big and so blue and so high up that every time she looked at it her stomach dropped and she had to look at the ground again. It didn't seem right that it should just stay up there with nothing holding it.

She'd found a corner at the end of the stalls, between a man selling rope and a woman selling nothing at all that Xenna could see, and she'd put her crates down there on a cloth and taken the lids off.

People came to look straight away. That was the mushrooms. There weren't any other mushrooms at the fair, not one. She'd walked the whole mile to check. In a blight year the forest ones were short and dear, and the cellar ones hadn't come up because Udo hadn't come up. And here were hers, three crates fresh, packed in damp moss, and four of dried: brown caps and white, the long thin pale ones, the big flat oyster-gray ones that grew on the old timbers in the far beds.

"Deepwood?" said a woman, peering.

"No."

"Where, then?"

"Below," said Xenna.

The woman looked at her, and at the hat, and at the mushrooms, and went away.

It happened again. It kept happening. Everybody wanted to know where, and Xenna said below, because that was what you said. You didn't tell people up top your roads. That was the first thing anybody in Underwell learned, before walking, nearly. You didn't say where the passages went, or which junctions were open, or which springs were good, or how you got from one place to another. Up top was up top. Up top had its own business. If up top wanted to know where things came from, up top could go down and look.

"Below *what*?" said a man, with a laugh.

"Just below."

He laughed again and went away too.

And everyone kept looking at the hat. She could feel them do it. It was a grower's hat, wide-brimmed, made of plaited rush with a waxed crown, because in the beds you were working under the lamps all day and the glare off the wet walls would blind you. Everybody in Underwell had one. But up here, apparently, it was what people wore in Deepwood. Somebody said so, behind her. *Look at the hat. She's from the forest. Forest mushrooms, in a blight year.*

And after that it started being said a different way. *Where'd she get them, in a blight year? How's she got forest mushrooms when nobody's got forest mushrooms?*

That was when the warden came.

---

He was a short, square, sunburned man called Galt, with a heavy book under his arm and a pencil behind his ear, and he looked at her mushrooms for a long while before he looked at her.

"These are good," he said.

"They're mine."

"Where are they from?"

"Below."

Galt didn't laugh. He just nodded, as if *below* was an answer he could do something with, and opened his book.

"Here's the trouble," he said. "This is a blight year. You've heard about the blight?"

"Everybody's heard about the blight."

"Then you'll know the forest mushrooms are short. Very short. Which means there's money in them, and where there's money in a thing there's people who'll steal it, or sell you last year's rotten ones dressed up as this year's, or sell you whatever they found growing on a dead sheep and call it Deepwood black cap." He tapped the book. "So this fair has a rule this year. Mushrooms carry a mark. A Deepwood house's mark, from a house I know, that'll answer for them. No mark, no sale."

"They're not Deepwood."

"I can see that, now I'm close." He looked at the pale long ones. "These are cellar stuff. Udo's sort, from below. But Udo answered for Udo's, and Udo isn't here. And I've got a rule for forest goods and nothing at all for anything grown below."

Xenna stood there with her hat pulled down, and the sky pressing on the back of her neck.

"There's one other thing," Galt said. "Old rule. Older than me. A grower who sells her own goods, in person, at her own stall, under her own name, answers for them herself. Her name's the mark." He shrugged. "You'd put your name up on a board. And where you're from. And you'd stand behind them. Anybody gets sick, anybody's cheated, it's you they come to."

"Where I'm from," said Xenna.

"That's how a mark works."

"No," said Xenna.

Galt looked at her a while longer. Then he shut his book.

"Then you can't sell them," he said. "I'm sorry. They're very good."

---

Fitch found her an hour later, sitting on her crates in her corner with the lids back on.

He was a cheerful sort of man. Everything about him was cheerful: his coat, which was yellow; his face, which was round and pink and creased at the eyes from smiling; his voice, which sounded like somebody had just told him a joke he was going to pass on.

"Heard you had a bit of bother with old Galt," he said, sitting down on the next crate without being asked. "Mark rule. Shame. Terrible shame. Lovely-looking stock, too." He lifted a lid and peered in. "Lovely."

"They're not for sale."

"Not by you, they're not. Not under Galt's nose. But here's the thing." He leaned in. He smelled of something sweet. "I deal in forest goods. Have done for years. I've got a house's mark. A proper one, Deepwood, all above board, and old Galt knows it. I put my mark on your crates, they go on my stall, they sell as Deepwood. Galt doesn't blink. Everybody's happy."

"They're not Deepwood."

"Well, nobody's going to know that, are they?" He laughed. "Mushroom's a mushroom. Half of what I sell as Deepwood never saw a tree. Half the price to you, and you don't have to stand here all day in that hat getting looked at."

Xenna looked at her crates.

The oil. That was what was in them, really. Not mushrooms. Underwell's lamp oil for the winter, and the grain, and the salt, and the lamp-glass for the new beds, and a dozen other things a junction settlement couldn't make for itself down there in the dark. Half the price was half of all of that. But the full price was nothing at all, if she couldn't sell.

"Maybe," she said.

"You do that." Fitch stood up and dusted his yellow coat. "I'm at the far end, by the spice heaps. Come by before the fair closes tomorrow. After that I'm full."

---

She needed a mule for the return. One of hers had come up lame on the stair, and the lame one couldn't carry oil back down. So in the evening she went to the animal yards inside the gate.

The man who ran them was a tall, quiet sort with straw in his hair, and he didn't say anything when she came in, which suited her. He gave her one look, and then looked at her lame mule, and then knelt down in the dirt and picked up the mule's foot and looked at that for a good long while.

"Stone bruise," he said eventually. "She'll be right in a week."

"I've got two days."

He nodded. He went down the row of stalls, and came back with a dark brown mule with a white nose, and handed Xenna the rope.

"How much?" she said.

"Bring her back."

That seemed to be all. His name was Zan, somebody had said at the gate. There was an old gray pony in the end stall who watched the whole thing, and when Xenna looked at her she looked back, steadily, like somebody's grandmother.

There was a fire going in the yard, in a ring of stones, with a flat iron pan on it, and Zan was making his supper: plateau flatbread, slapped thin and cooked on the iron, with butter and spice on it. He looked at Xenna, and at the fire, and at the pan, and then made a second flatbread, and held it out.

She sat down on the fence rail and took it. Then she got up again and went to her crates, and came back with a handful of the big flat oyster-grays, and held them out to him the same way.

He looked at them. Then he put them in the pan with a lump of butter.

She'd eaten those mushrooms her whole life. She'd grown them, picked them, dried them, fried them, cooked them in broth and in oil and raw in the beds with the dirt still on. She'd never eaten one with the sky over her.

The sun was going down behind the gate, red, the whole sky turning colors she didn't have names for. The two of them sat on the fence rail with the flatbread and the mushrooms and didn't say a word. The pony in the end stall shifted and sighed. Somewhere out on the fair road somebody was singing badly. The mushrooms were very good.

"Better than forest," Zan said, after a long time.

"Yes," said Xenna.

That was the whole conversation.

---

In the morning an old woman came to her corner.

She was tiny and brown and ancient, with a basket on her arm full of bundles of dried herbs tied with string, and she smelled of about forty different things at once. She didn't ask where the mushrooms were from. She just leaned over the crates, lifted a lid, put her face nearly into the moss and sniffed, long and slow.

"Not forest," she said.

"No."

"No." The old woman took out one of the pale long ones, broke it, and sniffed the broken end. Then she bit it, chewed, and stood there with her eyes half shut. "Cellar," she said. "Timber-grown, some of them. These pale ones in the dark, never seen a lamp. Good clean beds." She opened her eyes. "Better than anything forest I've had through my hands this year. Better than most I've had any year."

Her name was Ondine. She was up from the Twilighthollow side for the fair, the way she had been every late summer for longer than most people at the fair had been alive, and she sold herbs, and she knew mushrooms the way Xenna knew damp. She said so, loudly, to the people who had stopped to watch, and to the people walking by, and to the rope-seller next door, who had heard it already.

"Not forest," she told everybody. "Cellar. And good."

People started stopping again. Asking again.

"What about the blight?" said a woman. "Can it get in a cellar?"

Xenna looked at her.

"My beds are clean," she said. "This year. I've looked at every one."

"And next year?"

"I don't know. Nobody does." That was the truth. Nobody in Underwell did. Nobody up here did either. She'd asked Ondine, quietly, and Ondine had shaken her head. "I'll look at every one next year too."

The woman nodded slowly.

But Galt walked by about noon, and nodded to Ondine, and looked at the lids still on the crates, and walked on. Ondine's word wasn't a mark. Xenna couldn't sell a single mushroom.

---

At the end of the second day she walked down to the spice heaps.

Fitch's stall was bigger than she'd thought it would be. It had an awning, striped, and a long table, and baskets on it of forest goods: dried berries, nuts, bark, a few small bunches of mushrooms that Xenna looked at once and knew had never seen a tree. On a hook behind the table was a little wooden board with a mark burned into it, a leaf in a ring, which she supposed was the Deepwood house's.

"There she is," said Fitch, delighted. "Thought you'd come."

She stood in front of his table.

It would be so easy. She'd go back to her corner and get the crates, and bring them here, and Fitch would hang his little board over them, and in the morning they'd sell, and she'd get half, and buy half of everything Underwell needed, and go back down the stair, and nobody would ever have to know where anything came from. Nobody would look at her. Nobody would ask. Nobody would laugh at *below*. It was what she'd been taught. Up top was up top.

But they weren't Deepwood mushrooms. They were hers.

She'd grown them. She'd been growing them since she was eight years old, in the far beds by the spring, on timbers she'd carried in herself and soaked herself and seeded herself, in the long dark, with her hat on against the glare. Ondine had put her face in the moss and said *good clean beds*, and they were.

"No," said Xenna.

Fitch's face did something, and then went cheerful again. "No?"

"No. Thank you." She meant it. He'd been kind enough, in his way. "They're not forest mushrooms. I'm not selling them as forest mushrooms."

"Well, you're not selling them as anything else," said Fitch, still cheerfully. "Galt won't let you."

"He will," said Xenna. "If I do it his way."

---

She found a stall. It had belonged to a man selling pots who'd sold out of pots, and he let her have the end of his table for the last day.

She pried the lid from one of her crates, tipped the moss and mushrooms into the next one along, and turned the lid over, and got out her knife.

Then she sat there with the knife in her hand, not cutting.

The fair was going on around her: the noise, the goats, the shouting, the sky. Galt was at his table by the gate with his book. Ondine was three stalls down with her herbs. Somebody was frying something that smelled of onions. Nobody was looking at her, particularly.

You didn't tell up top where you were from. You didn't tell up top anything.

She cut *XENNA DREE* into the lid in big square letters, the way growers at home cut their marks into the bed-timbers so everybody knew whose was whose. Then, under it, after another long moment:

*UNDERWELL. GROWN BELOW.*

She didn't cut anything about the roads. She'd have died first. But that much she cut, and propped the lid up against the crates, and stood back from it, and pushed her hat back off her forehead so people could see her face, and waited.

Galt came over in the evening, when the fair was closing. He read the lid twice, and looked at her, and at the crates.

"Underwell," he said.

"It's a junction. By a spring. Below."

"I've never heard of it."

"No," said Xenna. "Nobody has."

He took his pencil from behind his ear and wrote something in his book. "Your name's the mark, then," he said. "You answer for them."

"I answer for them."

"Then sell them," said Galt.

---

She sold out by noon on the third day.

It wasn't hard, after that. People came because Ondine had told them to, and because the lid said *GROWN BELOW* and that was interesting, and because there were no other mushrooms at the fair. They asked where Underwell was, and she said below, and some of them laughed, but they bought anyway, and some of them didn't laugh at all. An innkeeper from up the saddle bought a whole crate of the dried and said he'd been looking all summer. A woman from the plateau bought the pale long ones for her mother, who was sick and could keep nothing else down. By noon there was nothing left in the crates but moss.

She took the money to the market and bought oil, and grain, and salt, and lamp-glass, and a bolt of good cloth that wasn't on the list but that she'd decided Underwell needed anyway. Ondine haggled for her, ferociously, without being asked, and seemed to be having the time of her life.

In the evening she went back to the yards. Zan had the brown mule ready, and her own two, and he loaded them with her, sack by sack, without either of them saying much. The old gray pony watched from the end stall.

"Coming up again?" Zan said, when the last sack was on.

"Maybe. If Udo's not through by then." She thought about it. "Maybe anyway."

He nodded, and handed her the lead rope.

At the stair-hut by the east gate she stopped.

The door stood open on the dark. Cool air came up out of it, damp and mineral and smelling of home, and she could hear, very faintly, the spring running somewhere far below. The mules pulled at the lead, wanting to go down out of the light.

She took her hat off.

The sun was nearly gone. The sky over the saddle was going the color of the inside of a shell, dark blue at the top, and there were the first stars, three of them, very small, a long way up. She'd forgotten stars. She'd had them as a girl, holding her father's coat, and she'd forgotten them completely.

She stood there with the hat in her hand and looked up for as long as she could stand it, and then a little longer.
