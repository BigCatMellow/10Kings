## The Same Paper

The great bellows at the Upper Works were the size of a cart shed, and there were two of them, and every week of the frost somebody had to climb inside and grease them.

Rhona Gwynne did it on her knees, in the folds. The leather went up over her head in long pleats, gray-brown and stiff with cold, and where it creased it went pale, and where it went pale it would crack if you let it. She worked the dripping in with her palm, one crease at a time, as her grandad had shown her when she could still stand up in there. It smelled of mutton and old smoke. She couldn't remember a winter when the inside of the bellows hadn't smelled of somebody's supper.

The pot had been on the step again that morning, wrapped in a cloth and still a bit warm. It mostly was. Grandad said it was somebody who owed him, and she wasn't to ask, so she didn't.

That was the near one done. The far one wanted doing before the shortest day.

When she climbed out, Jupp was at the side gate with his hands in his armpits.

"Owners say nobody in," he said.

"I know."

"Nobody, they said."

He looked at her, and at the pot, and up at the Works behind her, which hadn't had a fire in it since the summer, when the crews walked out on the morning of the pour. "Well," he said. "I never saw you."

He said it every week. The owners down in Coalglen paid him to keep the gate shut and the crews out, and he'd kept it shut since the walkout, and he let her in every week, and that was all either of them had ever said about it.

"Will they light her in the spring?" said Rhona.

"Owners say that's up to the crews." Jupp sniffed. "Crews say it's up to the owners. Same as they've said since the walkout." He nodded up the valley. "He's lit, anyway."

At the head of the valley, past the last farms, one chimney was smoking. It was the only one in the valley that was. That was the Slatehallow workshop, and everybody knew whose it was, and nobody knew who paid for it.

"Carrier's due the morning after the shortest day," said Jupp, to the weather.

"I know."

"Only your grandad said."

"My grandad says a lot of things."

---

There was a man in her kitchen with one boot off.

He was sitting at her bench with the boot in his lap, and the sole was hanging off it like a tongue. He was big through the shoulders, and he had the look of somebody who'd been out in a lot of weather and had stopped minding. A sword in a plain scabbard leaned against the dresser.

"This is Wurdren," Grandad shouted from the other room. Iolo Gwynne had been in bed since the summer with his chest, and he kept his door open so he could join in. "He was limping. I had him in."

"You can't have people in. You're ill."

"I'm resting." He'd be up by the thaw. The healer from Coalglen said so, and so did he, and Rhona believed the healer. "He's walking down to Coalglen, and I told him the road's bad below the bridge, so he's stopping till the carrier comes. He can go down with Druce." A pause. "I told him you're off."

"Grandad."

"Well, you are."

He'd told everybody she was leaving. He'd told the coal man. He'd told Verity next door, and Cadoc from the crews, and Jupp, and the boy who brought the milk, through the window, as they went past.

The man had got up when she came in, which nobody on the Row ever did, and was standing with his boot in his hand looking sorry about it.

"I can pay," he said. "Not much. Or I can do something." He nodded at the bench. "That's split."

It was her bellows. Her own, the little double one she'd made for the forge out the back: three boards and two chambers, so it blew on the push and on the pull and the fire never dropped between strokes. She'd made four of them now. The side seam had gone in the cold, a long split down the stitching, and she'd tried to sew it herself and the thread had pulled straight through the leather. The saddler's boy used to do her leathers. He'd gone up the valley in the autumn, to the Slatehallow workshop, where it was full pay and a dry roof, and he hadn't been down since.

"I was going to leave it," she said.

He bent over the seam and ran his thumb along it.

"I've sewn worse," he said. "Boots, mostly. A scabbard. Have you got two needles?"

She had two needles. She found him waxed thread and a lump of beeswax, and he sat down at the bench with his own boot forgotten under it and started picking out her stitches. By the look of him he'd rather get it right than get it done.

The letter sat behind the jug on the shelf, where she'd put it so she'd stop looking at it.

---

Supper was Smoked Ember Stew, because it was winter and there was mutton. She browned the meat in its own fat and left it at the back of the fire all afternoon with barley and roots, and when it was done she skimmed the top off into the crock by the door.

"What's that for?" said Wurdren.

"The Works."

"She greases the bellows with it," Grandad shouted. "Mine, really. Forty years I did them. Now she does them, and I lie here and tell her she's doing it wrong."

"You can't see me doing it."

"I can tell."

They ate at the bench, with the door open so Grandad could hear. Rhona took him his bowl, and he said it was too hot and ate all of it. Wurdren said it was very good. A bit later he said it again.

After supper he lit the lamp and took up the seam again. Two needles through the same hole from either side, then pull, and pull, and the thread sat down into the leather instead of cutting it. He was slow, and he didn't make a single stitch she'd have to do again.

When he reached up to the shelf for more wax, the jug was in the way. He moved it, and the letter was behind it.

"Somebody's written to you," he said.

"About my bellows. I sold one in the autumn to a man buying for a house in Port. That's how, I suppose." It was the furthest any work of hers had ever gone. "You can read it if you like. Everybody else has."

"Read him the good bit," Grandad shouted.

Wurdren unfolded it. It was the only thing in the house that wasn't brown or black: heavy cream paper, folded small, in a clear, educated hand. It said that her bellows had come to the attention of one who valued useful work. It said that should the valley fail to recognize them, a sum sufficient to set her up in a workshop of her own was available, wherever she liked, with no condition but that she continue. A reply left with the carrier would find its way. It wasn't signed.

"*Should the valley fail to recognize them,*" said Grandad, through the wall. "Recognize! The valley recognized her when she was four and set the hen house on fire."

"It was smoking. It wasn't on fire."

"The hens recognized her."

Wurdren smiled, but he was holding the letter up to the lamp. He tilted it slowly, one way and then the other, until something came up in the grain of the paper: a little lantern, the kind that hangs off the back of a ship.

Rhona had read that letter every night for a week. She'd never known there was anything in the paper.

"What is it?"

"A watermark. The papermaker's mark." He was still looking at it. "I've seen this paper."

"Where?"

He thought. He thought for so long that Grandad shouted "Well?" through the wall.

"I can't think where," said Wurdren. "I've been a lot of places this year."

He folded the letter along its folds and set it on the shelf again, and put the jug back in front of it. Then he sat and went on with the needles, and when the seam was done he did his own boot, quickly, as if it didn't count.

---

In the morning there was a pot on the step.

"Somebody Grandad knows," said Rhona, when Wurdren looked at it.

He carried it up to the Works for her, with the crock from the door, the brazier and the bag of rags, and at the side gate Jupp gave the two of them a long look.

"I never saw either of you," he said.

Inside it was colder than out. The water wheel stood in its trough with icicles off every paddle, and the casting floor was still crusted where the sand had been laid for the pour and never used. The bellows took up the whole back wall. Wurdren stood and looked up at them with his mouth a little open.

"They're lungs," he said.

"They're bellows," said Rhona.

She showed him how. You set the pot on the brazier till the dripping ran, and you dipped your rag, and you got into the folds on your knees and worked it in, crease by crease, till the pale went brown again. He was clumsy at it, and then he wasn't. He didn't talk, which she hadn't expected. He looked like a man with a lot of stories.

When the first pot was done they sat on the bellows beam to wait for the second to warm. The brazier ticked. Up in the roof was the vent where the smoke went out when the Works was lit, and the snow was coming in through it, slowly, down a long slant of gray light, and settling on the casting floor on top of the sand. They sat a while. It came down and came down. There wasn't a sound in the whole Works but the brazier.

"Should I take it?" said Rhona.

He turned to her and opened his mouth, and she could see he had the answer ready. Then he shut it again.

"I don't know," he said.

She'd thought he'd say yes.

"I usually say yes," he said. "To people." He looked at the snow coming down. "I don't know."

The dripping was running. She got up and dipped her rag.

"Who'll do this," he said, "after you've gone?"

"Nobody."

"The crews?"

"The crews won't come in the owners' Works, and the owners won't pay anybody to." She got down on her knees in the fold. "So nobody."

"Have you asked?"

She worked a crease. It went from pale to brown under her hand.

"If I asked," she said, "they'd have to say. One way or the other."

He seemed to think about that. Then he got down beside her in the next fold, and they did the rest without talking.

---

That night she sat with Grandad.

His room was the warmest in the house, because the chimney went up the wall behind the bed, and he sat propped up on three pillows with his hands on the blanket.

"You've done the last of them," he said.

"Before the shortest day. Like you said."

"Then tomorrow you give her a breath."

"Grandad."

"One full stroke. The shortest day, every year, so she knows she's not forgotten. Forty years I did it." He pointed at her, more or less. "And the Row knows."

"It takes two on the beam. More."

"There's a big fellow in the kitchen."

Down the Row a dog barked and stopped.

"I heard the bell, you know," Grandad said. "In the summer. Coalglen's, for their pour. I lay here and listened for ours." He looked at the window. "There wasn't one."

Then he said she was letting the cold in, sitting there, and to go to bed.

---

The shortest day hardly bothered getting light.

By mid-afternoon the Works was full of blue dusk, and Rhona and Wurdren were hanging off the end of the bellows beam like two coats on a peg. The beam was a whole tree, squared, with an iron shoe at the end where the wheel's cam took it when the Works was running. It went down about the width of a hand, and stopped.

"Again," said Rhona.

They let it up and pulled it down. A hand's width. The leathers creaked and didn't fill.

"Your grandad did this?" said Wurdren, a bit short of breath.

"Grandad and two of the crews. Every year."

There was a noise behind them. Jupp had come in from the gate. He stood on the casting floor in what light there was and looked at the beam, and at the two of them on the end of it, and then he took his coat off and folded it and put it down on the sand.

"I never saw this," he said, and got on.

With three of them the beam came down. It came down slowly, and over their heads the leathers filled and rose, a cart shed's worth of cold air going in, and then the beam was all the way down and there was nowhere left for the air to go but out.

It went out through the cold furnace and up the stack. It went with a long, low sound, like somebody very big breathing out at the end of a day's work, and the whole Works breathed out with it, and on top of the stack a puff of old soot went up black against the snow: the first thing out of that chimney since the summer.

They let the beam up. The leathers sank. Nothing cracked and nothing wheezed. Rhona listened for a leak with her whole head, and there wasn't one.

When they came out of the side gate, the Row was standing in its doors.

Grandad was sitting up when she got in, without the pillows.

"She's sound," he said.

---

She was up before it was light, because the carrier came early and she hadn't written anything yet.

She lit the lamp and opened the door to look at the weather, and Cadoc was on the step.

He was bent over with a pot in both hands, in the act of putting it down. He was a ladle-man, a broad, quiet man who'd walked out with the rest of the crews in the summer and hadn't been near the Works since, and he stayed exactly where he was, bent over, as if she mightn't see him if he didn't move.

"Morning," said Rhona.

"Morning." He put the pot down and straightened up. "Heard her yesterday," he said, and looked up the Row at the Works. "And your grandad says you're off." Then he looked at his boots.

Somebody else was coming down the Row in the dark with a pot in a cloth. It was Jupp. He saw Cadoc and stopped, and Cadoc saw him. For a moment the two of them stood and looked at each other over her step.

Then Verity came out from next door in her shawl with a pot, and put it down beside Cadoc's, and said "Oh," and went back in. Then the widow from the end house, and then a boy Rhona hadn't known was old enough to be up at that hour.

Nobody said anything about it. Jupp put his pot down at the end of the line of them and said "I never—" and stopped, and went back up to his gate.

Five pots on the step. All winter. She'd thought it was Grandad's doing.

Wurdren came through behind her with both boots on, the loose sole sewn. He looked at the step for a while. Then he started carrying the pots in, two at a time, without asking, and setting them along the shelf under the jug.

She sat down at the bench with a leaf from Grandad's old order book and wrote her answer. *After the pour.* She looked at it, and didn't add anything.

Druce came up the Row with the mule just after it got light, stamping his feet, with the bag of letters for Coalglen across the mule's back. She gave him the paper folded small.

"Reply?" he said.

"Reply."

He put it in the bag with the rest. Wurdren was going down with him. He had his pack on and his sword at his hip, and he stood in the road a moment as if he were going to say something, and then said thank you for the bed, and for the stew, which he said was very good.

"Thank you for the seam," said Rhona.

He went off down the Row with Druce and the mule and the bag. At the bridge he stopped. He turned round and looked back up the valley, not at her and not at the Works exactly, at all of it, like a man who's left something behind and can't think what. Then he turned round again and went on down after the mule.

She went in and shut the door. The fire was nearly out. She knelt at the hearth with her bellows and worked it, and it blew on the push and blew on the pull, steady, and his stitches held all the way along the seam, a bit crooked. The fire came up.

"Well?" Grandad shouted. "What did you tell it?"

"After the pour."

He didn't say anything to that. She went and stood in his door.

"It's been the Row," she said. "The dripping. All winter. Cadoc, and Jupp, and Verity, and all of them."

Grandad looked at her as if she'd told him the snow was cold.

"Who did you think?" he said.
