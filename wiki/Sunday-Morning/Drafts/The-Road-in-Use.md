## The Road in Use

The lot came out of the bag with Zan Coyle's name on it, and Zan, who was at the far edge of everybody minding his own business, heard it read out and thought, *Oh, no.*

"Zan Coyle," said the customs clerk again, louder, because nobody had moved.

People turned round. There was a lot of that in Ninewell Saddle on a lot day: people turning round to look at whoever had come up, as though they'd never seen them before, when they'd been buying that person's mule-hire and walking past that person's yard every day of their lives. Zan stood there and let them look. It was how people look at a horse at a fair.

"Well, go on, then," said someone.

He went on. He went up to the customs-house step, where the clerk held out the blessing halter, which was red and had bells on it and was older than the clerk, and Zan took it. Everybody clapped. Zan nodded at them, which was what you did, and went straight back to the yards.

The blessing was in three days. The lot picked whoever led the string, the first animals of the season's first caravan out, along the saddle road from the west gate to the Compass shrine at the far end, where the keeper blessed them for the roads. It was an honor. People wanted it. Zan had put his name in the bag every year for ten years because you had to, and every year he'd stood at the back hoping it would be someone else, and every year it had been.

Vinegar was in the end stall, eating.

She was the oldest pony in the yards, gray in the face, broad as a door and about as easy to argue with, and she'd headed the blessing string for longer than Zan had worked there. She looked up when he came in with the red halter, and looked at the halter, and went back to her hay.

"Don't start," Zan told her.

He took her out to the yard to try it on her. Between the stalls and the water trough there was a plank bridge over the drain, two good boards nailed side by side, and Vinegar walked up to it, and stopped, and blew at it through her nose.

"It's a plank," said Zan.

She looked at him.

"It's been a plank every day this week."

She went on looking at him. So he walked across it himself, first, both ways, and stamped on it in the middle, and then came back and stood on the far side, and Vinegar considered all this, and then stepped over the drain as if the plank had never been there at all.

"Thank you," said Zan.

That was how it went with Vinegar. You didn't argue. You showed her.

---

The letter was read out in the customs house that afternoon, because the clerk had been told to and because half the town had already heard it was coming.

It was from the route authority, up on the plateau. It said a number of things about weights and tolls and the condition of roads, and then it said the thing everybody had come to hear: that this season, the town's share of the pass tolls would be *reckoned on the road in use at the blessing.*

The clerk read it twice, because somebody at the back asked him to, and then he put it down on the table and looked as though he wished he'd been born somewhere else.

The trouble was the shake.

It had come in the spring, between Pass Opening and now, a long low rumble in the night that rattled every pot in Ninewell and brought a slice of the hill down across the blessing road about halfway along the saddle. Nobody had been on the road, thank the roads. In the morning there'd just been a slope of new stone where the road had been, raw and gray and still settling, and the old road disappearing under it on one side and coming out again on the other.

The way round was a goat track, up and along the hillside above the fall. And on the goat track, where the slip had torn away a skin of earth and scrub, there was a tower.

Nobody had known it was there. It was a stump of a thing, a ring of dressed stone about twice a man's height, with a doorway and over the doorway a name, cut into the lintel, worn so nearly smooth you could only see it with the light coming across it sideways. It had been a toll tower. You could tell from where it stood: right where the old track would have narrowed, where anyone going along the saddle would have had to pass it single file.

"Ours," said Granny Jaske, from the front.

"Ours," said Arn Merrow, from the side, at exactly the same moment.

That was the other trouble.

The Jaskes and the Merrows had been in Ninewell Saddle as long as anyone, and they'd been not getting on for most of it. Each family had a great-grandmother who'd held a toll on this saddle, back before the route authority, when anybody who could hold a bit of road could charge for it. Each family had a story about her. And each family had gone up the goat track the morning after the shake, and stood in front of that worn lintel, and read their own great-grandmother's name in it.

If the blessing went by the goat track, then the goat track was the road of record, and the toll point was at the tower, and whoever held the tower held the town's share. If the blessing went by the old road, the toll stayed at the town gate, same as ever, and everybody shared.

But the old road was under a hill.

"It'll take a month to clear," said the clerk.

"Then we'll go round," said Granny Jaske.

"Then we'll go round," said Arn Merrow.

They both said it at once again, and then glared at each other for saying it, and the whole customs house turned round, as one, and looked at Zan.

---

He went up the goat track that afternoon with Vinegar, to see.

It was a bad bit of track. It went up steep from the end of the old road, and along the face of the hill above the fall, and at the tower it squeezed between the tower's wall and the edge, where the slip had taken away everything down to the bare stone. There was perhaps a stride and a half of track there, and then nothing, and then a long way down, the new scree, and the old road under it.

Vinegar walked it. She walked it slowly, with her head down and her ears going, and at the narrow place by the tower she stopped and blew at the edge, once, long and loud, the way she'd blown at the plank, and then she went on.

Zan stood by the tower afterward and looked at the name over the door. He looked at it for a while with the light going across it. He could make out a shape near the start that might have been a letter and might have been a crack. That was all.

He didn't think it said Jaske. He didn't think it said Merrow either. He didn't think it said anything anymore. But nobody had asked him.

---

The next day both families came to the yards.

They didn't come together. Granny Jaske came in the morning with a pie, a big one, mutton, still warm, and stood at the yard gate and told Zan how much his own mother would have liked to see him lead the blessing, and how the Jaskes had always thought very well of his family, and how her great-grandmother's name was perfectly plain on that lintel to anyone with eyes. Zan said yes. He said it several times. It was easier than anything else he could think of, and she went away pleased.

Arn Merrow came in the afternoon with a bottle, and stood at the same yard gate and told Zan what a good hand he'd always been with a string, and how the Merrows had always said so, and how anyone with eyes could see their great-grandmother's name on that lintel, plain as day. Zan said yes. He said it several times. Arn went away pleased.

Zan put the pie and the bottle on the shelf in the tack room, side by side, and looked at them.

"You agree with everybody," said a voice from the gate.

It was Lio Tarrow. He was a guide up from Seven Wells, a long friendly young man with a sunburned nose and a coat with too many pockets, and he'd been hired to take the first caravan of the season over the passes. He'd come up the day before to look at the roads. He'd married in the spring, and he mentioned it about every third sentence, as if he couldn't quite believe it himself.

"I don't agree," Zan said. "I just say yes."

"That's what agreeing is."

"No. Agreeing's when you mean it." Zan looked at the pie. "Saying yes is when you want them to go away."

Lio laughed. "My wife says I'm the opposite. I mean everything and say it all." He came in and leaned on the stall door next to Vinegar, who allowed it. "The caravan's coming in early, by the way. A day. They'll be at the west gate tomorrow night. They want to go out the morning after."

Zan did the sum in his head.

"That's the blessing," he said.

"That's the blessing." Lio scratched Vinegar under the chin. "It picked you, you know. The lot."

"It's a bag of names."

"It's a bag of names that picked you." Lio said it lightly, but he didn't look like he was joking. "Somebody's got to walk in front. Might as well be the one it picked."

---

They ate at the stair-hut, at midday, because the market was too full.

The stair-hut was by the east gate, a little stone place with a heavy wooden door and a hole in the floor where the stair went down into the dark. In an ordinary spring there'd have been a broker in it by now, a quiet man called Udo who came up from below every few weeks with baskets of cellar mushrooms and bundles of dried ones, and sold them in the market, and went back down with oil and salt. But the shake had brought down the deep road too, somewhere under the saddle, and Udo hadn't come up.

He was all right. Word had come up the stair a month back, passed hand to hand from somebody to somebody: he was alive, and working some other way round on the far side, and he'd be back when he could. But the door of the stair-hut was shut, and there were no cellar mushrooms in the market, and nobody knew when there would be.

Zan and Lio sat on the step of it in the sun with their backs to the door. Lio had bought caravan skewers off a stall, lamb and onion, black at the edges, and a flatbread each, hot from the clay oven, with spiced butter running into it. They ate out of their hands and watched the market.

There wasn't anything to watch, really. It was a market. People bought things. A goat got loose and was caught. Somebody's child dropped a honey cake in the dust and looked at it and decided not to cry about it after all. The sun came round onto the step and warmed their knees. Lio talked about his wife, and Seven Wells, and a goat that his grandfather had once owned half of, and Zan listened, and didn't have to say anything at all, and found that he liked it.

"There ought to be mushrooms," Lio said, when the skewers were gone. He nodded at the shut door behind them. "This time of year. Everybody says."

"Udo'll be back."

"And if he's not?"

Zan shrugged. "Then somebody else'll come up. They've got to sell somewhere."

---

It rained in the night, hard, the first real rain of the season, and in the morning the whole saddle smelled of wet stone.

The caravan had come in at the west gate after dark, forty animals and a dozen people and a great deal of noise. In the morning, with half of Ninewell up on the walls, Zan took the blessing string out to walk the way: a rehearsal, the day before, the way it was always done. Vinegar in front in the red halter with the bells. Six of the caravan's lead mules behind her. Zan at Vinegar's head with one hand on the halter rope, and Lio walking behind, and both families walking behind Lio, keeping well apart.

They went out of the west gate and along the old road to the fall, and turned up onto the goat track.

It was worse after the rain. You could see it. The earth at the edge had gone dark and soft, and in places it had slumped, just a little, a thumb's width, as though the hill were thinking about something. Vinegar went up the steep part with her head low and her ears turning, and along the face of the hill, slower and slower, and at the narrow place by the tower she stopped.

She didn't blow at it this time. She just stopped, with all four feet planted, and stood.

Zan stood with her.

Behind him the mules stopped, one after another, the way mules do when the one in front stops: not because they've seen anything but because they trust the one in front more than they trust you. The bells on the red halter went quiet.

"Come on, old girl," said Arn Merrow, from behind.

"Get her on," said Granny Jaske.

Zan didn't move. He stood by Vinegar's head and looked at the edge, the dark slumped earth, the long drop to the scree. He looked at Vinegar's ears, and her nose, and the way she had her weight back on her hind legs.

He could have walked it first, the way he'd walked the plank. He thought about it. He looked at the edge again and decided he wasn't going to.

"She won't," he said.

"Make her."

"No."

It wasn't loud. But both families heard it, and so did Lio, and so did the six mules, and so, possibly, did Vinegar, who turned her head and looked at Zan as if she'd been waiting for him to say it.

He turned her round. It took a while on a track that narrow, and the mules had to be backed down one at a time, and nobody spoke the whole way.

---

By nightfall the whole town knew. The blessing couldn't go by the goat track. The blessing was the next morning. And the old road was under a hill.

"Then we don't bless," said Granny Jaske, in the customs house, with half the town packed in. "Postpone it."

"You can't postpone a blessing," said the shrine keeper, who'd come down specially. "The caravan's going out tomorrow, blessed or not. They won't wait for you."

"Then they go out unblessed," said Arn Merrow.

The clerk cleared his throat. He'd been reading the letter again. He'd clearly been reading it all day.

"If there's no blessing," he said, "there's no road in use at the blessing. And if there's no road in use at the blessing, there's no road of record." He swallowed. "And then there's no toll share for anybody. Not the gate. Not the tower. Not this season."

That went round the room very slowly.

For a moment the room just took it in. Then somebody at the back said, "So we dig," and somebody else said, "In one night?", and everybody started talking at once.

Zan had his back to the wall by the door, where he always stood when he couldn't avoid being somewhere. He could feel the room starting to look at him again. He was the one the lot had picked. He was the one whose pony had stopped. He was, apparently, the one who'd said no.

Lio, next to him, said quietly, "Go on."

Zan opened his mouth. He'd never said anything in the customs house in his life. He'd never said anything in front of more than about three people at once, and two of those had usually been mules.

"The pony won't go by the tower," he said. "Not after the rain, and she's right. I won't make her, and I won't take the string past without her. So it's the old road or nothing."

That was four sentences. He counted them afterward. It felt like he'd been talking for an hour.

The customs house was very quiet.

Then Granny Jaske got up, slowly, because her knees weren't what they'd been, and pulled her shawl tight, and said, "Well, the Jaskes have shovels," in a voice that suggested the Merrows very likely didn't.

"The Merrows," said Arn Merrow, standing up so fast his bench went over, "have *more* shovels."

---

They dug all night.

They dug by lantern, the whole town, at the bottom edge of the fall where the old road went under the scree. They weren't trying to shift the hill. They didn't need the whole road, just a way through: a cut a mule wide, along the line of the old road, through the loose stuff on top. Lio walked the line first with a lantern and showed them where it ran, because he'd been guiding roads since he was a boy and could see a buried one the way Zan could see a lame leg. The caravan's mules were brought down and set to hauling, with drags and ropes, and Zan worked them, and that part of the night suited him fine.

The Jaskes dug on the left side of the cut and the Merrows dug on the right. They didn't speak to each other. They didn't need to. Every time a Jaske stopped to straighten their back, a Merrow saw it and dug faster, and every time a Merrow stopped, a Jaske did the same, and by about the middle of the night the two families were going at the scree like it had personally insulted their great-grandmothers, and the cut was going forward faster than anybody had thought it could.

Nobody got hurt. It was slow, careful work, and Lio made them stop and shore up twice where the loose stuff wanted to come down again, and once Granny Jaske had to be sat on an upturned bucket and given tea because she wouldn't stop on her own.

Just before dawn, Arn Merrow drove his shovel into the scree and it rang on stone, flat stone, laid stone, the old road's own paving coming up gray under the lantern. And everybody stopped to stare at it, and then Granny Jaske stood up off her bucket and walked over and stood on it.

"Well," she said. "There it is."

---

In the morning the blessing string walked the old road to the shrine.

Vinegar went in front in the red halter with the bells. Zan went at her head. The six caravan mules followed, and then the caravan, and then most of the town, filthy, wrapped in blankets, holding mugs, very proud of themselves. The string went out of the west gate and along the saddle and into the cut, a mule wide, raw stone walls on either side, and Vinegar walked through it without so much as a blow, and out the far side, and on to the shrine.

The keeper blessed them. She blessed the animals and the people and the road, and then, because it was there and she was a thorough woman, she blessed the scree as well.

The tower stayed where it was, up on the goat track, with its worn lintel and its doorway and whoever's name it was. The road of record was the old road. The toll stayed at the gate. The Jaskes and the Merrows announced, separately and at great length, that they would be taking the matter of the tower to the arbiters on the plateau, and then they spent the whole walk back to town arguing about which family should pay the clerk to write it up.

Zan put Vinegar in her stall and gave her an apple, which she took as no more than her due. He hung the red halter on its peg. He looked at the pie and the bottle on the shelf in the tack room, still side by side.

From down the street, in the inn, he could hear both families. They'd reached the inn and stopped there, all of them, because they were tired and filthy and it was a blessing morning and the inn was open. They were still arguing. They'd be arguing about that tower for years.

Zan stood in the yard for a minute.

Then he took the pie under one arm and the bottle in his other hand, and went out of the yard gate, and down the street to the inn, and in. He found a place on the bench between a Jaske and a Merrow, and he set the pie and the bottle down between them, and sat.
