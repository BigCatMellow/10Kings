## Three Pots at Three Moon

"I'm done," said Mother Seral. "After this year. The permit goes to whichever of you makes the right one."

She said it the way she said *the stew's ready*. Between the lunch crowd and the dinner crowd. A week before the Three Moon Festival.

Her three grandchildren were at the stall because she'd told them to be.

Tobiah, the eldest, straight from the docks in his work clothes, eating a bowl of yesterday's stew standing up. He ate everything standing up. Ines, from the merchant quarter, in a good coat, not eating, because she'd been told it was yesterday's. And Jory, from the courts, ink on his cuff, who'd been hoping to be told nothing in particular so he could go home.

"The right *what*?" said Ines.

"Stew."

"Which stew?"

Mother Seral looked at her. "What else do I make?"

The stall stood on festival street. A long, crooked lane of stalls running down from the old Charter Steps to the fish market, in a neighborhood that had been Port for as long as there'd been a Port. A narrow wooden counter. A striped awning. A charcoal hearth at the back. And on the hearth, one enormous dented copper pot.

Three generations on this spot. Mother Seral's mother had held it before her. She'd come to Port as a girl, from somewhere in the Deepwood uplands, with a clay pot and a recipe. Nobody in the family could find that village on a map anymore. The copper pot had replaced the clay one before anyone living was born.

The dent had been there just as long.

"Nana," Jory said carefully. "What makes it right?"

Mother Seral ladled a bowl of yesterday's stew and handed it to him.

"Did you eat?"

"I ate at the court."

"What did you eat at the court?"

"Bread."

"*Bread*," said Mother Seral, with enormous contempt.

She didn't answer his question. She never answered questions. She answered them with other questions, always about food, and she'd done it their whole lives. So steadily that the three of them had long ago stopped noticing it was a way of not answering.

Jory stood there with the bowl in his hands.

And for no reason at all, he felt very small. The bowl felt very heavy. He was carrying it somewhere, carefully, both hands, across a crowded street.

Then it was gone.

"And Jory does the paperwork," said Mother Seral. "Obviously."

"Why obviously?"

"You don't cook," said Tobiah, through a mouthful.

"I don't cook," said Jory, "because I've never needed to. There is a great deal of food in Port. It is sold on every street. It seems to me an unnecessary vanity to reproduce at home, badly, what the city already provides, well, at a very reasonable—"

"He doesn't cook," Ines agreed, and patted his arm.

---

"A transfer," said Amaranth Doss. "A family stall. Festival street." She clasped her hands. "Oh, *Jory*."

Five days to the festival.

The Market Regulation office took up three floors of an old customs building on the harbor. It was, as everyone in Port knew, the place where the stamp you needed always belonged to another office.

Jory knew the building professionally. The courts sent him once a month. The permit clerk, tall and dry and deeply unhelpful, knew him professionally too.

She'd never looked this happy to see him.

"It's a simple transfer," he said.

"It is not." She slid a form out of a drawer. "A festival-street permit passes to *one* named holder. You'll need the current holder's release. Green stamp, this office. The new holder's standing. Blue stamp, the ward office across the square. And the festival committee's acknowledgment. Red stamp." She smiled. "The committee only sits during the festival."

She laid the form in front of him. Tenderly.

"Filed complete by the close of the festival's second night. Or the spot reverts to the ward and goes to the next name on the list." She checked. "There are forty-one names on the list."

"One name," said Jory.

"One name."

"There are three of us."

"Then," said Amaranth Doss, "I'm going to enjoy this festival *very* much."

---

Four days out.

The dockside eating house where Tobiah cooked the night shift was a long, low room full of smoke and salt. Dock workers coming off the tide ate the way Tobiah ate. Standing. Fast. Straight from the pot.

"You can't hurry it," Tobiah said, stirring. "That's the thing. Everybody hurries. Nana had it on before the gulls were up. Before anybody was up. You'd come down in the morning and the whole house already smelled of it." He tasted. "On at dawn. Every time."

It was a good stew. Dark. Rich. Hot with pepper.

And where Jory remembered forest mushrooms, soft and black and earthy, there was salt fish.

"It's fish."

"It's fish," Tobiah agreed. "You seen what they want for mushrooms this year? Forest mushrooms? Blight up in the uplands. Two summers now. Half the villages lost their whole crop. You *can* get them. You'd pay more than for the meat." He added more pepper. "Down here, we've got fish. The lads like it."

The lads did like it. The man at Jory's elbow was on his third bowl.

An older worker from somewhere up the coast said something to Tobiah in harbor creole. Jory caught about half. Tobiah laughed and answered in the same. And without anyone asking, Jory turned to the man at his elbow and put the joke into plain Port speech.

The man laughed.

So did Tobiah.

Jory had always done that. At every family dinner he could remember. Turning Tobiah's dock talk and Ines's merchant talk into something the other one could hear. Even though all three of them spoke the same language. Had since birth. Could, in theory, understand each other perfectly.

"Dawn," Tobiah called after him as he left. "Write that down. Nana always had it on at dawn."

Jory wrote it down.

---

Three days out. Ines didn't put hers on at dawn. She put it on after breakfast, like a civilized person.

Her kitchen was white and blue tile, with a window full of light and a shelf of glass spice jars from half the ports on the coast.

The stew was magnificent.

Deep red. Fragrant. A dozen spices he couldn't name. Meat falling apart. And mushrooms. Real forest mushrooms.

He did not want to know what she'd paid for them.

"The way great-grandmother would have made it," said Ines, ladling. "If she could have afforded to."

"That's not the same as the way she made it."

"No. It's better. That's the point." She set the bowl in front of him with a small, fierce look. "She came here with a clay pot and nothing, Jory. She cooked what she could get. Roots. Scraps. Whatever the forest had. And we're *allowed* to have more than that. We're allowed to cook it the way she would have if she'd had a spice shelf and a tiled kitchen. That's what she came here for. So we could."

Jory ate it.

Magnificent.

It didn't taste like Nana's.

"Tobiah says you're doing it all wrong," he said. A translation. A gentle one. Of what Tobiah had actually said.

"Tobiah's cooking it the way she did because she was poor, and calling that tradition." Ines sat down across from him. "I'm cooking it the way she would have if she weren't, and calling it respect. We're both right. We're both being ridiculous."

She turned her own bowl on the table.

"You know what I remember about Nana's stew? It isn't the taste."

"What is it?"

"The bowls. That mismatched set from the back room. Eleven of them. Or twelve, I never knew. She'd serve out of the pot, and she'd always keep one back. One bowl. Empty. Turned over on the side of the counter." She smiled. "I used to ask her who it was for."

"What did she say?"

"She asked if I'd eaten."

Jory wrote it down.

*One bowl, always kept back.*

---

The Three Moon Festival opened at dusk, and by full dark festival street was one long, loud, smoky room with the sky for a roof.

Every stall lit. Every hearth going. Stews and soups and fried things and sweet things and a hundred kinds of bread. Families ten generations in Port and families ten weeks in Port. Harbor creole, heritage tongues, guild jargon, court Port. All at full volume. All at once.

That was the point of Three Moon, the committee said every year. Once a year, the whole of Port, which couldn't agree on anything, stood in one street and ate each other's food.

Nobody was entirely sure why it was called Three Moon. People argued about it every year.

Jory suspected that was the point too.

The family stall ran three pots that night. Nobody had said not to. Tobiah's fish and pepper at one end of the counter. Ines's spiced red at the other. Mother Seral's dented copper in the middle, and Mother Seral stirring it, refusing to taste either of the others.

"On the second night," she said when they asked. "Did you eat?"

The spot next door had been empty for two years.

Tonight, a family was cooking in it.

A borrowed brazier. One iron pot. No awning. Four of them: a woman of about fifty with work-worn hands, a man who seemed to be her husband, two half-grown kids. Thick barley porridge with apples and cream, the kind of thing a Greenvale farm kitchen made in autumn. A copper a cup.

And no permit on their post. Jory noticed. Noticing was his job.

By the middle of the evening he'd heard three different people call them smugglers.

He heard it the way you heard everything on festival street: over his shoulder, while doing something else. *It is said the new lot are running goods through the fish market. Nobody knows them. Turned up with nothing and now they've got a stall. Where'd the money come from?*

Smuggling was up all over Port that year. Everybody knew. The harbor watch was doubled. And the Market Regulation office had sent its inspectors onto the festival streets with orders to fine every stall that couldn't show a permit. Because, the rumor went, the people upstairs had started counting fines to prove they were doing something.

The street's inspector came down the lane at second bell with his book and his stamp.

Halloran. Tired. Round-shouldered. Jory knew him from the office.

He looked at the new family's bare post. At the family. At the three people who'd followed him down the lane to see what he'd do.

He looked at his book like it had personally disappointed him.

"You've no permit," he said to the woman.

"We applied. They said it needed a stamp from another office."

"It always does." He wrote something down. "I'll be back tomorrow night. If there's no permit—"

He didn't finish.

He looked at the family stall. The three pots. Jory.

"And you, clerk. Your nana's is on one name. I've seen the notice. You've got till close tomorrow."

When he'd gone, the woman next door caught Jory's eye across the gap and shrugged.

Dry. Tired. Funny. The shrug of someone who'd been through worse than an inspector.

"We're the Ardens," she said. "Greenvale. We had a farm, up the valleys." She stirred. "Bad year. Couldn't pay the seed loan. So a very polite man came round and bought our notes."

A smile with not much in it.

"*Ever* so polite. He bought us lunch."

Tobiah laughed out loud.

After a second, so did Mrs. Arden.

---

That was when the caravan cook came down the lane.

Jory didn't know he was a caravan cook until he said so. Short. Broad. Gray-bearded. A Deepwood accent worn thin by the road. He walked festival street the way a man walks a market in his own trade. Stopping at every stall. Looking into every pot.

At the family stall he stopped at all three. Then at the sign Ines had painted in her best lettering.

*Guardian Stew.*

"Guardian Stew," he said. "Is it?"

"Taste it," said Mother Seral.

He tasted all three. Slowly. Professionally. Three separate spoons, a sip of water between each.

He was a cook, he said. Ilo Sedgewater, from the uplands. Cooking for the caravans since the blight took his village's mushrooms the summer before last. Came over the pass at Icestep with the first caravan of spring — "honest scale there, if you're ever through" — and worked his way down the routes to Port, one job at a time.

Forty years he'd cooked. He knew Guardian Stew.

He put down the third spoon.

"None of these is Guardian Stew."

Silence at the family stall. So complete the Ardens looked over.

"That one." Tobiah's. "Fish stew with pepper. Good one. That one." Ines's. "A Sunplains stew with our name on it. Also good. Too much cinnamon." He looked at the dented pot. "And that one's very nice, and it's got mushrooms in it, which I'd like to know where you got. And it's a Port stew. Been a Port stew a hundred years."

He shrugged.

"Guardian Stew's not a recipe. Not where I come from. Every house makes it different. It's what you do with the pot."

"What do you do with the pot?" said Jory.

Ilo Sedgewater looked at him with the faint surprise of a man asked something everyone knows.

"You open it at the table. Not in the kitchen. Bring it out with the lid on. Open it where everyone can see. And the first bowl goes to a guest. Somebody who isn't family." He picked up his pack. "In my village, if there's no guest at the table, you keep a bowl back. And you wait."

He started off down the lane.

"That's Guardian Stew. The rest is just stew."

Behind the middle pot, Mother Seral had stopped stirring.

---

They argued about it that night in the back room.

Mother Seral's little room behind the stall. The mismatched bowls on the shelf. The dented pot cooling on the hearth. Nothing about the stew written down anywhere.

"The man's a crank," said Tobiah.

"The man liked *my* stew," said Ines. "Whatever he said about cinnamon."

"He said all three were wrong."

"He said all three were *stews*."

Jory sat between them, like always, turning each one's words into something the other could hear. He was halfway through a sentence when he stopped.

"You open it at the table," he said.

They both looked at him.

"And the first bowl goes to a guest." He looked at the shelf. "And if there's no guest, you keep one back."

"The kept-back bowl," Ines said slowly.

"On at dawn," said Tobiah. "Never hurried."

Jory stood up.

He didn't know why.

He crossed the room and took a bowl off the shelf. Chipped. Blue. A crack in the glaze. He held it in both hands.

It was very heavy. He was very small.

He was carrying it somewhere. Carefully. Both hands. Across a crowded street.

"I carried it," he said. "The first bowl. When I was a boy. Nana would fill it, the first one, when she took the lid off. She'd give it to me and tell me to take it to someone. Someone at the stall she didn't know. A sailor. A stranger. Somebody standing at the back who didn't look like they had a copper." He swallowed. "I'd carry it over. Both hands. I'd give it to them. Then I'd come back and she'd give me mine. Every festival, till I was eleven. Then I went to school, and I stopped, and I never—"

He looked at the bowl.

"I don't cook. I never learned. I was the one who *carried*."

Tobiah was staring at him. Ines had a hand over her mouth.

"The pot on at dawn," Tobiah said. "That's mine."

"The bowl kept back," said Ines. "That's mine."

"The first bowl to a stranger," said Jory. "That's mine."

None of them had heard Mother Seral come in.

She stood in the doorway, shawl around her shoulders, looking at the three of them.

She didn't say anything.

After a long moment she came across the room, took the chipped blue bowl out of Jory's hands, turned it over in her own, and put it back on the shelf.

"Did you eat?" she said.

Her voice was not quite steady.

And for the first time in his life, Jory understood that she'd been answering all along.

---

Morning of the second day. Jory went back to the Market Regulation office with his form and a problem that was, by any reasonable standard, unsolvable.

One name. Three of them. And the thing that made the stew *right* turned out to belong to all three of them. And to none.

Amaranth Doss was thrilled to see him.

"One name," she said.

"I know."

He didn't leave.

He was a clerk. Eleven years of forms. And he knew that most offices, if you went back far enough, had a form for everything, including the things nobody asked for anymore.

He asked for the old stall registers.

Amaranth Doss, who'd been hoping for exactly this, took him down to the basement herself.

Three hours.

He found it in a register from Mother Seral's mother's time. A section headed *Family Stalls (Mixed Holding)*, between a form for transferring a fishing weir and one for registering a performing bear.

A family stall permit. A festival-street spot held jointly by up to three named members of one household. All three to sign. All three to cook on at least one festival night.

Never repealed.

Nobody had used it in sixty years.

He filled it in on the basement table. Three names. Took it upstairs.

Amaranth Doss read it through twice. Then, with an expression of deep professional satisfaction, she stamped it green.

He had the blue stamp from the ward office across the square before noon.

Then, because he was a clerk and couldn't help himself, he went back up to her counter and asked for a second form.

New stall application. Festival street. The empty spot next to his family's.

The applicants had no permanent address yet and a farm's worth of cooking. He filled in everything he could. Wrote down what he couldn't, so the Ardens could fill it in that night.

He was still at the counter when Inspector Halloran came in off his rounds, gray with the morning, his book fat with fines.

Halloran read the form over Jory's shoulder. Read the name. Read it again.

"That's the new lot. The Greenvale family. Next to yours."

"Yes."

"You're filing it for them?"

"Starting it. They'll need the committee stamp tonight. So will we."

Halloran didn't say anything for a moment.

Then he let out a long breath. Like a man setting down something heavy he'd carried all the way up a hill.

"Thank the tides." He rubbed his eyes. "You know how many fines I've written this week? Every one of them to somebody who couldn't read the form, or couldn't find the office, or couldn't pay the stamp. Every one goes in a column. Somebody upstairs adds up the column and says, look how many smugglers." He shook his head. "A filed permit's the best thing I've seen all week."

He took out his own stamp. The inspector's gray one.

Stamped the Ardens' form where it said *Inspected on site*.

Then stamped the family stall form for good measure, though it didn't need it, and went home to sleep.

---

The second night of Three Moon, the family stall was the only stall in Port with three pots and three names on its post.

Tobiah had knocked on both their doors before the gulls were up. All three pots went on at dawn, in the stall's own hearth, because that was how it was done. Over each pot hung a little board Ines had painted with the neighborhood it came from.

*The docks.* *The merchant quarter.* *Festival street.*

At second bell the committee came down the lane with its red stamp. It stamped the family stall's form.

Then it stamped the Ardens' form next door.

The Ardens looked at the stamp on their post like it might fly away.

At third bell the pots came out to the counter with their lids on.

And were opened there. In front of everyone.

Jory took the kept-back bowl off the side of the counter, where Ines had turned it over at dusk. The chipped blue one. He filled it from the middle pot.

He looked across the gap between the stalls, at Mrs. Arden, who'd been watching the whole business with her dry, tired face.

He carried it over.

Both hands.

"We don't—" she started.

"It's the first bowl," Jory said. "It goes to a guest. It always has."

Mrs. Arden looked at the bowl. At him. At the three pots, the three painted boards, and Mother Seral behind them with her arms folded.

She took it in both hands.

She ate it standing up, the way Tobiah ate. When she was done she said, quietly, "That's very good." She handed him back the bowl, and a cup of her apple barley with it.

Jory, who did not cook, stood between the two stalls and ate it.

Up the lane, on the next street over, somebody was telling somebody that the new lot on festival street were running goods through the fish market.

Jory heard it over his shoulder while he was doing something else.

On this street, nobody was listening.

---

Halloran came by at fourth bell, book closed under his arm. He looked at both posts. Both stamps. Three pots.

He bought a bowl of Tobiah's fish and pepper and ate it standing up at the end of the counter without a word.

Ilo Sedgewater came back at fifth bell and tried Ines's again, like a man checking his own judgment.

He winced.

"Too much cinnamon."

"You said," said Ines.

"I'm saying it again." He held out the bowl for a second one.

He ate that one to the bottom.

Last of all, with the street emptying and the charcoal going gray, Mother Seral came out from behind the counter with a spoon.

She tasted all three at last.

The docks. The merchant quarter. Festival street.

One after another. Slowly. No water between.

Her three grandchildren watched her do it and didn't breathe.

She put the spoon down.

"Again next year," she said.

And went in to bed.
