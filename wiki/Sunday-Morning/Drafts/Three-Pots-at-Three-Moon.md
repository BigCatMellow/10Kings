## Three Pots at Three Moon

"I'm done," said Mother Seral. "After this year. The permit goes to whichever of you makes the right one."

She said it the way she said *the stew's ready*, between the lunch crowd and the dinner crowd, a week before the Three Moon Festival. Her three grandchildren were at the stall because she had told them to be. Tobiah, the eldest, had come straight from the docks in his work clothes and was eating a bowl of yesterday's stew standing up, as he ate everything. Ines had come from the merchant quarter in a good coat and was not eating, because she had been told it was yesterday's. Jory had come from the courts in his interpreter's collar, hoping to be told nothing in particular so that he could go home.

"The right what?" said Ines.

"Stew."

"Which stew?"

Mother Seral looked at her. "What else do I make?"

The stall stood on festival street, a long, crooked lane of stalls that ran down from the old Charter Steps to the fish market, in a neighborhood that had been Port for as long as there had been a Port. It was a narrow wooden counter under a striped awning, with a charcoal hearth at the back and one enormous dented copper pot on the hearth. The family had held the spot for three generations. Mother Seral's mother had held it before her, and had come to Port as a girl from somewhere in the Deepwood uplands with a clay pot and a recipe. Nobody in the family could have found that village on a map anymore. The copper pot had replaced the clay one before anyone living was born, and the dent had been in it just as long.

"Nana," Jory said carefully, "what makes it right?"

Mother Seral ladled a bowl of yesterday's stew and handed it to him. "Did you eat?"

"I ate at the court."

"What did you eat at the court?"

"Bread."

"Bread," said Mother Seral, with enormous contempt, and didn't answer his question. She never answered questions. She answered them with other questions, always about food, and she had done it so steadily all their lives that the three of them had long ago stopped noticing it was a way of not answering.

Jory stood with the bowl in his hands, and for no reason he could name he felt very small, and the bowl felt very heavy, and he was carrying it somewhere, carefully, in both hands, across a crowded street. Then the feeling was gone.

"And Jory does the paperwork," said Mother Seral. "Obviously."

"Why obviously?"

"Because you don't cook," said Tobiah, through a mouthful.

"I don't cook," said Jory, "because I have never needed to. There is a great deal of food in Port, and it's sold on every street. It seems to me an unnecessary vanity to reproduce at home, badly, what the city already provides, well, at a perfectly reasonable—"

"He doesn't cook," Ines agreed, and patted his arm.

---

Five days before the festival, Jory went to the Market Regulation office, which took up three floors of an old customs building on the harbor and was, as everyone in Port knew, the place where the stamp you needed always belonged to another office. The courts sent him there once a month, so he knew the building professionally, and the permit clerk, Amaranth Doss, knew him professionally too. She was a tall, dry woman who had never once in his experience been pleased to see anybody. When he told her what he had come for, her whole face lit up.

"A transfer," she said. "A family stall on festival street." She clasped her hands. "Oh, Jory."

"It's a simple transfer."

"It is not." She slid a form out of a drawer. "A festival-street permit passes to one named holder. You'll need the current holder's release, which is a green stamp from this office. The new holder's standing, which is a blue stamp from the ward office across the square. And the festival committee's acknowledgment, which is a red stamp, and the committee only sits during the festival." She laid the form in front of him with great tenderness. "It has to be filed complete by the close of the festival's second night, or the spot reverts to the ward and goes to the next name on the list. There are forty-one names on the list."

"One name," said Jory.

"One name."

"There are three of us."

"Then I'm going to enjoy this festival very much," said Amaranth Doss.

---

Four days out, Tobiah made his version in the kitchen of the dockside eating house where he cooked the night shift. It was a long, low room full of smoke and salt, and the dock workers coming off the tide ate the way Tobiah ate: standing up, fast, straight from the pot.

"You can't hurry it," Tobiah said, stirring. "That's the thing. Everybody hurries it. Nana had it on before the gulls were up, before anybody was up. You'd come downstairs in the morning and the whole house already smelled of it." He tasted from the spoon. "On at dawn. Every time."

It was a good stew, dark and rich and hot with pepper. And where Jory remembered forest mushrooms, soft and black and earthy, there was salt fish.

"It's fish," he said.

"It's fish," Tobiah agreed. "You priced mushrooms lately? Forest mushrooms? There's a blight up in the uplands — two summers now. Half the villages lost their whole crop. You can get them, but you'd pay more than you would for the meat." He added more pepper. "Down here we've got fish. The lads like it."

The lads did like it. The man at Jory's elbow was on his third bowl. An older worker from somewhere up the coast said something to Tobiah in harbor creole that Jory only half caught, and Tobiah laughed and answered in the same, and without anyone asking, Jory turned to the man at his elbow and put the joke into ordinary Port speech. The man laughed, and so did Tobiah.

Jory had always done that. At every family dinner he could remember he had sat between his cousins, turning Tobiah's dock talk and Ines's merchant talk into something the other one could hear, even though all three of them spoke the same language and had since birth and could, in theory, understand each other perfectly well.

"Dawn," Tobiah called after him as he left. "Write that down. Nana always had it on at dawn."

---

Three days out, Ines made hers. She didn't put it on at dawn; she put it on after breakfast, like a civilized person. Her kitchen was tiled in white and blue, with a window full of light and a shelf of glass spice jars from half the ports on the coast, and the stew she made in it was magnificent. It was deep red and fragrant with a dozen spices Jory couldn't name, the meat was falling apart, and there were mushrooms in it — real forest mushrooms. He did not want to know what she had paid for them.

"The way great-grandmother would have made it," Ines said, ladling, "if she could have afforded to."

"That isn't the same as the way she made it."

"No. It's better. That's the whole point." She put the bowl in front of him with a small, fierce look. "She came here with a clay pot and nothing, Jory. She cooked what she could get — roots, scraps, whatever the forest gave her. We're allowed to have more than that. We're allowed to cook it the way she would have if she'd had a spice shelf and a tiled kitchen. That's what she came here for, so that we could."

Jory ate it. It was magnificent, and it didn't taste like Nana's.

"Tobiah says you're doing it all wrong," he said, which was a translation, and a gentle one, of what Tobiah had actually said.

"Tobiah is cooking it the way she did because she was poor, and calling that tradition." Ines took the stool opposite. "I'm cooking it the way she would have if she weren't, and calling that respect. We're both right, and we're both being ridiculous." She turned her own bowl slowly on the table. "Do you know what I remember about Nana's stew? It isn't the taste."

"What is it?"

"The bowls. That mismatched set from the back room — eleven of them, or twelve, I never knew which. She'd serve straight out of the pot, and she always kept one back. One bowl, empty, turned over on the side of the counter." She smiled. "I used to ask her who it was for."

"What did she say?"

"She asked if I'd eaten."

---

The Three Moon Festival opened at dusk, and by full dark festival street was one long, loud, smoky room with the sky for a ceiling. Every stall was lit and every hearth was going. There were stews and soups and fried things and sweet things and a hundred kinds of bread; there were families who had been in Port ten generations and families who had been there ten weeks; there was harbor creole and court Port and guild jargon and half a dozen heritage tongues, all at full volume, all at once. That was the point of Three Moon, the festival committee said every year: once a year, the whole of Port, which could not agree on anything, stood in one street and ate each other's food. Nobody was entirely sure why it was called Three Moon. People argued about it every year, and Jory suspected that was the point as well.

The family stall ran three pots that night, because nobody had said not to. Tobiah's fish and pepper stood at one end of the counter and Ines's spiced red at the other, and in the middle was Mother Seral's dented copper pot, with Mother Seral stirring it and refusing to taste either of the others.

"On the second night," she said, when they asked. "Did you eat?"

The spot next door had been empty for two years. Tonight a family was cooking in it, with a borrowed brazier, a single iron pot and no awning: a woman of about fifty with work-roughened hands, a man who seemed to be her husband, and two half-grown children. They were making a thick barley porridge with apples and cream, the kind of thing a Greenvale farm kitchen made in the autumn, and selling it at a copper a cup. There was no permit on their post. Jory noticed that at once, because noticing things like that was his job.

By the middle of the evening he had heard three different people call them smugglers. He heard it the way you heard everything on festival street, over his shoulder while he was doing something else. *It is said the new lot are running goods through the fish market. Nobody knows them. They turned up with nothing and now they've got a stall — where did the money come from?* Smuggling was up all over Port that year; everybody knew it. The harbor watch had been doubled, and the Market Regulation office had sent its inspectors out onto the festival streets with orders to fine every stall that couldn't show a permit, because, the rumor went, the people upstairs had started counting fines to prove they were doing something.

The street's inspector came down the lane at the second bell with his book and his stamp. His name was Halloran, a tired, round-shouldered man Jory knew from the office. He looked at the new family's bare post, and at the family, and at the three people who had followed him down the lane to see what he would do, and then down at his book, unhappily.

"You've no permit," he said to the woman.

"We applied. They told us it needed a stamp from another office."

"It always does." He wrote something. "I'll be back tomorrow night. If there's no permit by then—" He didn't finish. He looked across at the family stall and its three pots, and at Jory. "And you, clerk. Your nana's permit is in one name. I've seen the notice. You've got until close tomorrow."

When he had gone, the woman next door caught Jory's eye across the gap between the stalls and shrugged. It was dry and tired and a little funny, the shrug of someone who had been through worse than an inspector.

"We're the Ardens," she said. "From Greenvale. We had a farm, up the valleys." She stirred her pot. "Bad year. Couldn't pay the seed loan. So a very polite man came round and bought our notes." She smiled without much in it. "Ever so polite. He bought us lunch."

Tobiah laughed out loud, and Mrs. Arden, a beat later, laughed with him.

---

It was a little after that when the caravan cook came down the lane. Jory didn't know he was a caravan cook until he said so. He was short and broad and gray-bearded, with a Deepwood accent worn thin by the road, and he walked festival street the way a man walks a market in his own trade, stopping at every stall and looking into every pot. At the family stall he stopped for longer. He looked at all three pots, and then at the sign Ines had painted in her best lettering, which said *Guardian Stew*.

"Guardian Stew," he said. "Is it?"

"Taste it," said Mother Seral.

He tasted all three, slowly and professionally, from three separate spoons, with a sip of water between each. He was a cook, he told them — Garro Sedgewater, from the uplands. He had cooked for the caravans since the blight took his village's mushrooms the summer before last, and had come over the pass at Icestep with the first caravan of the spring ("Honest scale there, if you're ever through") and worked his way down the routes to Port one job at a time. He had been cooking for forty years, and he knew Guardian Stew.

He put down the third spoon. "None of these is Guardian Stew."

The silence at the family stall was so complete that the Ardens looked over.

"That one," he said, nodding at Tobiah's, "is a fish stew with pepper. A good one. That one," at Ines's, "is a Sunplains stew with our name on it. Also good. Too much cinnamon." He looked at the dented pot. "And that one is very nice, and it has mushrooms in it, which I'd like to know where you got, and it's a Port stew. It's been a Port stew for a hundred years." He shrugged. "Guardian Stew isn't a recipe. Not where I come from. Every house makes it differently. It's what you do with the pot."

"What do you do with the pot?" asked Jory.

Garro Sedgewater looked at him with the faint surprise of a man who has been asked something everybody knows.

"You open it at the table, not in the kitchen. You bring it out with the lid on and open it where everyone can see. And the first bowl goes to a guest, somebody who isn't family." He picked up his pack. "In my village, if there's no guest at the table, you keep a bowl back, and you wait." He started off down the lane. "That's Guardian Stew. The rest is just stew."

Behind the middle pot, Mother Seral had stopped stirring.

---

They argued about it late that night in Mother Seral's little back room behind the stall, with the mismatched bowls on the shelf and the dented pot cooling on the hearth and nothing about the stew written down anywhere.

"The man's a crank," said Tobiah.

"The man liked my stew," said Ines, "whatever he said about cinnamon."

"He said all three were wrong."

"He said all three were stews."

Jory sat between them, as he always had, turning each one's words into something the other could hear, and he was halfway through a sentence when he stopped.

"You open it at the table," he said.

They both looked at him.

"And the first bowl goes to a guest. And if there's no guest, you keep one back."

"The kept-back bowl," said Ines slowly.

"On at dawn," said Tobiah. "Never hurried."

Jory stood up without quite knowing why and crossed the room and took a bowl down from the shelf. It was a chipped blue one with a crack in the glaze, and when he held it in both hands it was very heavy, and he was very small, and he was carrying it somewhere, carefully, across a crowded street.

"I carried it," he said. "The first bowl. When I was a boy. Nana would fill it when she took the lid off, the first one, and give it to me and tell me to take it to someone at the stall she didn't know. A sailor, or a stranger, or someone standing at the back who didn't look like they had a copper. I'd carry it over with both hands and give it to them, and then I'd come back and she'd give me mine." He swallowed. "I did it every festival until I was eleven. Then I went to school, and I stopped, and I never—" He looked down at the bowl. "I don't cook. I never learned. I was the one who carried."

Tobiah was staring at him. Ines had a hand over her mouth.

"The pot on at dawn," Tobiah said. "That's mine."

"The bowl kept back," said Ines. "That's mine."

"And the first bowl to a stranger," said Jory. "That's mine."

None of them had heard Mother Seral come in. She was standing in the doorway with her shawl round her shoulders, looking at the three of them, and she didn't say anything for a long time. Then she came across the room, took the chipped blue bowl out of Jory's hands, turned it over once in her own, and put it back on the shelf.

"Did you eat?" she said, and her voice was not quite steady.

---

On the morning of the second day Jory went back to the Market Regulation office with his form and a problem that was, by any reasonable standard, unsolvable. The permit could go to one name. There were three of them. And the thing that made the stew right had turned out to belong to all three of them, and to none of them on their own.

Amaranth Doss was delighted to see him. "One name," she said.

"I know."

He didn't leave. He had spent most of the night awake, and somewhere around the third hour he had remembered a probate case at the courts, years ago, in which two brothers had fought over a fish stall and the judge had thrown out the whole quarrel by pointing to a form neither of them had heard of.

"Family Stalls, Mixed Holding," he said.

Amaranth Doss's eyebrows went up by a very small, very satisfied amount. Then she opened the drawer of her own desk and took out a single printed sheet and put it on the counter in front of him, with something very close to respect.

It was a family stall permit. It allowed a festival-street spot to be held jointly by up to three named members of one household, provided all three signed it and all three served at the stall on at least one festival night.

He filled it in on the counter with three names, and Amaranth Doss read it through twice and then, with an expression of profound professional satisfaction, stamped it green. He had the blue stamp from the ward office across the square before noon.

Then, because he was a clerk and couldn't help it, he went back up to her counter and asked for a second form: a new stall application for festival street, for the empty spot beside his family's. The applicants had no permanent address yet and a farm's worth of cooking. He filled in everything he could, and wrote out a list of what he couldn't so that the Ardens could finish it that night.

He was still at the counter when Inspector Halloran came in off his rounds, gray with the morning, his book fat with fines. Halloran read the form over Jory's shoulder, and read the name, and read it again.

"That's the new lot. The Greenvale family next to yours."

"Yes."

"You're filing it for them?"

"Starting it. They'll need the committee stamp tonight. So will we."

Halloran said nothing for a moment. Then he let out a long breath, like a man setting down something heavy that he had carried all the way up a hill.

"Thank the tides," he said. "Do you know how many fines I've written this week? Every one of them to somebody who couldn't read the form, or couldn't find the office, or couldn't pay for the stamp. Every one of them goes in a column, and somebody upstairs adds up the column and says, look how many smugglers." He shook his head. "A filed permit is the best thing I've seen all week."

He took out his own stamp, the inspector's gray one, and stamped the Ardens' form where it said *Inspected on site*. Then he stamped the family stall form as well, for good measure, although it didn't need it, and went home to sleep.

---

On the second night of Three Moon, the family stall was the only stall in Port with three pots and three names on its post.

Tobiah had knocked on both their doors before the gulls were up, so all three pots had gone on at dawn in the stall's own hearth, because that was how it was done. Over each hung a small board Ines had painted with the name of the neighborhood it came from: the docks, the merchant quarter, festival street. At the second bell the festival committee came down the lane with its red stamp and stamped the family's form, and then stamped the Ardens' form next door, and the Ardens looked at the stamp on their post as though it might fly away.

At the third bell the pots were carried out to the counter with their lids on and opened there, in front of everyone.

Jory took the kept-back bowl from the side of the counter, where Ines had turned it over at dusk. It was the chipped blue one. He filled it from the middle pot and looked across the gap between the stalls at Mrs. Arden, who had been watching the whole business with her dry, tired face, and then he carried it over to her in both hands.

"We don't—" she began.

"It's the first bowl," said Jory. "It goes to a guest. It always has."

Mrs. Arden looked at the bowl, and at him, and at the three pots with their three painted boards, and at Mother Seral standing behind them with her arms folded. Then she took the bowl in both hands and ate it standing up, the way Tobiah ate. When she had finished she said quietly, "That's very good," and gave him back the bowl with a cup of her apple barley in it. Jory, who did not cook, stood between the two stalls and ate it.

Up the lane, on the next street over, someone was telling someone else that the new lot on festival street were running goods through the fish market. Jory heard it over his shoulder while he was doing something else. On this street, nobody was listening.

Halloran came by at the fourth bell with his book closed under his arm. He looked at both posts and both stamps and the three pots, bought a bowl of Tobiah's fish and pepper, and ate it standing up at the end of the counter without a word.

Garro Sedgewater came back at the fifth bell and tried Ines's again, like a man checking his own judgment. He winced, and held out the bowl for a second one, and ate that to the bottom too.

Last of all, when the street was emptying and the charcoal was going gray, Mother Seral came out from behind the counter with a spoon and tasted all three pots at last — the docks, the merchant quarter and festival street — slowly, one after another, with no water between. Her three grandchildren watched her do it and didn't breathe.

She put the spoon down.

"Again next year," she said, and went in to bed.
