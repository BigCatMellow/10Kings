## Three Pots at Three Moon

Mother Seral announced her retirement on a Tuesday, at the stall, a week before the Three Moon Festival, between the lunch crowd and the dinner crowd, in the voice she used to tell people the stew was ready.

"I'm done," she said. "After this year. The permit goes to whichever of you makes the right one."

Her three grandchildren were at the stall because she had told them to be. Tobiah, the eldest, had come from the docks in his work clothes and was eating a bowl of yesterday's stew standing up, as he ate everything. Ines had come from the merchant quarter in a good coat and was not eating anything, because she had been told the stew was yesterday's. Jory had come from the courts, where he was a clerk, with ink on his cuff. He had been hoping to be told nothing in particular so he could go home.

"The right what?" said Ines.

"Stew," said Mother Seral.

"Which stew?"

Mother Seral looked at her. "What else do I make?"

The stall stood on festival street, a long, crooked lane of stalls that ran down from the old Charter Steps toward the fish market, in a neighborhood that had been Port for as long as there had been a Port. It was a narrow wooden counter under a striped awning, with a charcoal hearth at the back and a single enormous dented copper pot on the hearth. The family had held the spot for three generations. Mother Seral's mother had held it before her, and had come to Port as a girl from somewhere in the Deepwood uplands with a clay pot and a recipe. By now nobody in the family could have found that village on a map. The copper pot had replaced the clay one before anyone living was born. The dent had been there as long.

"Nana," said Jory, carefully, "what makes it right?"

Mother Seral ladled a bowl of yesterday's stew and handed it to him. "Did you eat?"

"I ate at the court."

"What did you eat at the court?"

"Bread," said Jory.

"Bread," said Mother Seral, with deep contempt, and did not answer his question. She never answered questions. She answered them with other questions, always about food, and she had done it all their lives. She did it so steadily that the three of them had long ago stopped noticing it was a way of not answering.

Jory stood with the bowl in his hands and, for no reason he could name, felt as if he were very small, and the bowl was very heavy, and he was carrying it somewhere, carefully, with both hands, across a crowded street. The feeling lasted a moment and was gone.

"And Jory does the paperwork," said Mother Seral. "Obviously."

"Why obviously?"

"You don't cook," said Tobiah, through a mouthful.

"I don't cook," said Jory, "because I have never needed to. There is a great deal of food in Port. It is sold on every street. It seems to me," and he said this at the length he always said it, "an unnecessary vanity to reproduce at home, badly, what the city already provides, well, at a reasonable—"

"He doesn't cook," Ines agreed, and patted his arm.

* * *

The stall permit lived at the Market Regulation office, which occupied three floors of an old customs building on the harbor and was, as everyone in Port knew, the place where the stamp you needed always belonged to another office.

Jory went on the fifth day before the festival. He knew the building professionally; the courts sent him there once a month. The permit clerk, a tall, dry woman named Amaranth Doss, knew him professionally too, and when he told her what he had come for, her face lit up with a joy he found deeply unsettling.

"A transfer," she said. "A family stall. Festival street. Oh, Jory."

"It's a simple transfer."

"It is not." She pulled a form from a drawer. "A stall permit on festival street passes to one named holder. One. You'll need the current holder's release, which is a green stamp from this office; the new holder's standing, which is a blue stamp from the ward office across the square; and the festival committee's acknowledgment, which is a red stamp from the committee, which only sits during the festival." She laid the form in front of him with great tenderness. "And it has to be filed complete by the close of the festival's second night, or the spot reverts to the ward and goes to the next name on the list. There are," she checked, "forty-one names on the list."

"One name," said Jory.

"One name."

"There are three of us."

"Then," said Amaranth Doss, "I'm going to enjoy this festival very much."

* * *

Tobiah made his version on the fourth day, in the kitchen of the dockside eating house where he cooked for the night shift. It was a long, low room full of smoke and salt and dock workers coming off the tide, and they ate the way Tobiah ate, standing up, fast, from the pot.

He cooked it the way he said Nana had always cooked it. He put it on at dawn and did not hurry it. "That's the thing," he told Jory, who had come to taste it because Nana had said somebody had to. "Everybody hurries. You can't hurry it. She used to have it on before the gulls were up. Before anybody was up. You'd come down in the morning and the whole house smelled of it already." He stirred. "Dawn. Every time."

It was a good stew. It was dark and rich and hot with pepper. And where Jory remembered forest mushrooms, soft and black and earthy, there was salt fish.

"It's fish," said Jory.

"It's fish," Tobiah agreed. "You seen what they want for mushrooms this year? Forest mushrooms? There's a blight up in the uplands. Two summers now. Half the villages lost their whole crop. You can't get them. You can get them, but you'd pay more than for the meat." He tasted from the spoon and added more pepper. "Fish is what we've got down here. Fish and pepper. The lads like it."

The lads did like it. A dock worker at Jory's elbow had eaten three bowls. Another, older man, from somewhere up the coast by his accent, said something to Tobiah in a harbor creole that Jory caught only half of, and Tobiah laughed and answered in the same, and Jory, without being asked, turned to the dock worker at his elbow and put the joke into ordinary Port speech. The dock worker laughed. So did Tobiah. It was a thing Jory had always done. He had done it at family dinners for as long as he could remember, turning Tobiah's dock speech and Ines's merchant speech into something the other could hear, though all three of them spoke the same language, had spoken it since birth, and could in theory understand each other perfectly.

"Dawn," Tobiah said again, as Jory left. "Write that down. Nana always had it on at dawn."

Jory wrote it down.

* * *

Ines made her version on the third day, in her own kitchen in the merchant quarter, which was tiled in white and blue and had a window full of light and a shelf of glass spice jars from half the ports on the coast. She did not put it on at dawn. She put it on after breakfast, like a civilized person, and it was ready by the time Jory arrived, and it was, he had to admit, magnificent.

It was deep red and fragrant, with a dozen spices he could not name. The meat was falling apart. There were mushrooms in it, real forest mushrooms, and he did not want to ask what she had paid for them.

"The way great-grandmother would have made it," said Ines, ladling, "if she could have afforded to."

"That's not the same as the way she made it."

"No," said Ines. "It's better. That's the point." She set the bowl in front of him with a small, fierce look. "She came here with a clay pot and nothing, Jory. She cooked what she could get. Roots and scraps and whatever the forest had. And we — we're allowed to have more than that. We're allowed to cook it the way she'd have cooked it if she'd had a shelf of spices and a tiled kitchen. That's what she came here for. So we could."

Jory ate the stew. It was magnificent. It did not taste like Nana's.

"Tobiah says you're doing it all wrong," he said, which was a translation, gently done, of what Tobiah had actually said.

"Tobiah's cooking it the way she did it because she was poor," said Ines, "and calling that tradition." She sat down across from him. "I'm cooking it the way she'd have done it if she weren't, and calling that respect. We're both right. We're both being ridiculous." She was quiet for a moment. "Do you know what I remember? About Nana's stew? It isn't the taste."

"What is it?"

"The bowls." Ines turned her own bowl on the table. "She had that set — the mismatched ones, from the back room. Eleven of them, or twelve, I never knew which. And she'd serve out of the pot, and she'd always, always keep one back. One bowl. Empty. On the side of the counter, turned over. I used to ask her who it was for." She smiled. "She'd ask me if I'd eaten."

Jory wrote it down. *One bowl, always kept back.*

* * *

The Three Moon Festival opened at dusk on the second day before the deadline, and by full dark festival street was one long, loud, smoke-filled room with the sky for a roof.

Every stall was lit. Every hearth was going. There were stews and soups and fried things and sweet things and a hundred kinds of bread. There were families who had been in Port for ten generations and families who had been there ten weeks, and harbor creoles and heritage tongues and guild jargons and the high court Port that Jory spoke at work, all at full volume, all at once. That was the point of Three Moon, the festival committee said every year: once a year, the whole of Port, which could not agree on anything, stood in one street and ate each other's food. Nobody was entirely sure why it was called Three Moon. People argued about it every year, which was also, Jory suspected, the point.

The family stall ran three pots that night, because Mother Seral had said nothing to stop them. Tobiah's fish and pepper stood at one end of the counter, Ines's spiced red at the other, and Mother Seral's dented copper pot in the middle. She was stirring it, and still refused to taste either of the others.

"On the second night," she said, when they asked. "Did you eat?"

The spot next door had been empty for two years. Tonight a family was cooking in it.

They had a borrowed brazier and a single iron pot and no awning, and four people: a woman of about fifty with work-roughened hands, a man who seemed to be her husband, and two half-grown children. They were cooking a thick barley porridge with apples and cream, the kind of thing a Greenvale farm kitchen made in autumn, and they were selling it in cups for a copper a cup, and they had, Jory noticed at once, because noticing was his job, no permit on their post.

By the middle of the evening he had heard three different people say they were smugglers.

He heard it the way everyone on festival street heard everything, over his shoulder while he was doing something else. *It is said*, people said, *that the new lot are running goods through the fish market. Nobody knows them. They turned up with nothing and now they've got a stall. Where'd the money come from?* Smuggling was up all over Port that year. Everybody knew it. The harbor watch was doubled, and the Market Regulation office had sent its inspectors out onto the festival streets with orders to fine every stall that couldn't show a permit, because, the rumor went, the office's masters had started counting fines to prove they were doing something.

The street's inspector came down the lane at the second bell with his book and his stamp. He was a tired, round-shouldered man named Halloran, whom Jory had met in the office, and he looked at the new family's post and at the family, and at the three people who had followed him down the lane to see what he would do. Then he looked at his book, visibly unhappy.

"You've no permit," he said to the woman.

"We applied," said the woman. "They said it had to have a stamp from another office."

"It always does," said Halloran, and wrote something down. "I'll be back tomorrow night. If there's no permit—" He didn't finish. He looked at the family stall, at the three pots, and at Jory. "And you, clerk. Your nana's is on one name. I've seen the notice. You've till the close of tomorrow."

When he had gone, the woman next door caught Jory's eye across the gap between the stalls and shrugged. It was a dry, weary, humorous shrug, the shrug of a person who had been through worse than an inspector.

"We're the Ardens," she said. "Greenvale. We had a farm, up the valleys." She stirred her pot. "Had a bad year. Couldn't pay the seed loan. And a very polite man came round and bought our notes." She smiled without much in it. "Ever so polite. He bought us lunch."

Tobiah, who had heard, laughed out loud, and after a moment so did Mrs. Arden.

It was then, in the middle of the second bell, with the street at its loudest, that the caravan cook came down the lane.

Jory did not know he was a caravan cook until he said so. He was a short, broad, gray-bearded man with a Deepwood accent worn thin by travel, and he walked down festival street the way a man walks through a market in his own trade, stopping at every stall, looking into every pot. At the family stall he stopped and looked at the three pots in a row, and at the sign Ines had painted that said, in her best lettering, *Guardian Stew*.

"Guardian Stew," he said. "Is it?"

"Taste it," said Mother Seral.

He tasted all three. He did it slowly and professionally, from three separate spoons, with a sip of water between. He was, he told them, a cook. Ilo Sedgewater, from the uplands. He had cooked for caravans since the blight took his village's mushrooms the summer before last. He had come over the pass at Icestep with the first caravan in the spring — "honest scale there, if you're ever through" — and worked his way down the routes to Port a job at a time. He had cooked forty years. He knew Guardian Stew.

He put down the third spoon.

"None of these," he said, "is Guardian Stew."

There was a silence at the family stall so complete that the Ardens looked over.

"That one," said Ilo Sedgewater, pointing at Tobiah's, "is a fish stew with pepper. Good one. That one," at Ines's, "is a Sunplains stew with our name on it. Also good. Too much cinnamon. And that one," at Mother Seral's dented pot, "is very nice, and it has mushrooms, which I'd like to know where you got, and it's a Port stew. It's been a Port stew for a hundred years." He shrugged. "Guardian Stew's not a recipe. Not where I come from. Every house makes it different. It's what you do with the pot."

"What do you do with the pot?" said Jory.

Ilo Sedgewater looked at him with the faint surprise of a man asked something everyone knew.

"You open it at the table," he said. "Not in the kitchen. You bring it out with the lid on, and you open it where everyone can see. And the first bowl goes to a guest. Someone who isn't family. In my village, if there's no guest at the table, you keep a bowl back, and you wait." He picked up his pack. "That's Guardian Stew. The rest is just stew."

Mother Seral, at the middle pot, had stopped stirring.

* * *

They argued in the back room that night, after the festival street had gone quiet, in Mother Seral's little room behind the stall. The mismatched bowls were stacked on the shelf, and the dented pot stood cooling on the hearth, and nothing about the stew had ever been written down.

Tobiah said the cook was a crank. Ines said the cook had liked her stew, whatever he said about cinnamon. Tobiah said the cook had said all three were wrong. Ines said the cook had said all three were stews. Jory, as he always had, sat between them and turned each one's words into something the other could hear. He found himself doing it without meaning to, and then, halfway through a sentence, stopped.

"You open it at the table," he said.

They both looked at him.

"And the first bowl goes to a guest." Jory looked at the shelf of mismatched bowls. "And if there's no guest, you keep one back."

"The kept-back bowl," said Ines slowly.

"On at dawn," said Tobiah. "Never hurried."

Jory stood up. He did not know why. He crossed the little room and took a bowl down from the shelf, a chipped blue one with a crack in the glaze, and held it in both hands, and it was very heavy and he was very small, and he was carrying it somewhere, carefully, across a crowded street.

"I carried it," he said. "The first bowl. When I was a boy. Nana would fill it, the first one, when she took the lid off, and give it to me, and tell me to take it to someone. Someone at the stall she didn't know. A sailor, or a stranger, or somebody standing at the back who didn't look like they had a copper. And I'd carry it over, both hands, and I'd give it to them, and I'd—" he stopped. "And then I'd come back and she'd give me mine. I did it every festival until I was eleven. And then I went to school and I stopped, and I never—" He looked at the bowl in his hands. "I don't cook. I never learned. I was the one who carried."

Tobiah was staring at him. Ines had her hand over her mouth.

"The pot on at dawn," said Tobiah. "That's mine."

"The bowl kept back," said Ines. "That's mine."

"The first bowl to a stranger," said Jory. "That's mine."

In the doorway, where none of them had heard her come in, Mother Seral stood with her shawl around her shoulders, looking at the three of them. She did not say anything. After a long moment she came into the room, took the chipped blue bowl from Jory's hands, turned it over in hers, and put it back on the shelf.

"Did you eat?" she said, and her voice was not quite steady.

For the first time in his life, Jory understood that she had been answering all along.

* * *

On the morning of the second day, Jory went to the Market Regulation office with his form and a problem that was, by any reasonable standard, unsolvable. The stall could go to one name. There were three of them. And the thing that made the stew right had turned out to belong to all three and to none.

Amaranth Doss was delighted to see him.

"One name," she said.

"I know," said Jory.

He did not leave. He was a clerk. He had spent eleven years among forms, and he knew that most offices, if you went back far enough, had a form for everything, including the things nobody asked for anymore. He asked to see the old stall registers. Amaranth Doss, who had been hoping for exactly this, took him down to the basement.

It took him three hours. He found it in a register from the time of Mother Seral's mother, in a section headed *Family Stalls (Mixed Holding)*, between a form for the transfer of a fishing weir and one for the registration of a performing bear. It was a family stall permit. It allowed a festival-street spot to be held by up to three named members of one household jointly, provided all three signed and all three were present to cook on at least one festival night. It had never been repealed. Nobody had used it in sixty years.

He filled it in on the basement table, three names, and took it upstairs, and Amaranth Doss read it through twice, and then, with an expression of profound professional satisfaction, stamped it green.

He got the blue stamp at the ward office across the square before noon. Then, because he was a clerk and could not help himself, he went back up to Amaranth Doss's counter and asked for a second form: a new stall application, festival street, the empty spot beside his family's. The applicants had no permanent address yet and a farm's worth of cooking. He sat at the counter and filled in everything he could, and wrote down what he couldn't so the Ardens could fill it in that night.

He was at the counter when Inspector Halloran came in off his rounds, gray with the morning, his book full of fines.

Halloran looked at the form over Jory's shoulder. He read the name. He read it again.

"That's the new lot," he said. "The Greenvale family. Next to yours."

"Yes."

"You're filing it for them?"

"I'm starting it," said Jory. "They'll need the committee stamp tonight. So will we."

Halloran did not say anything for a moment. Then he let out a long breath, like a man putting down something heavy he had been carrying up a hill.

"Thank the tides," he said. "Do you know how many fines I've written this week? Every one of them to somebody who couldn't read the form, or couldn't find the office, or couldn't pay the stamp. And every one of them goes in a column, and somebody upstairs adds up the column, and says, look how many smugglers." He shook his head. "A filed permit's the best thing I've seen all week."

He took out his own stamp, the inspector's gray one, and stamped the Ardens' application where it said *Inspected on site*. Then he stamped the family stall form for good measure, though it didn't need it, and went home to sleep.

* * *

On the second night of the Three Moon Festival, the family stall on festival street was the only stall in Port with three pots and three names on its post.

Tobiah's pot had gone on at dawn, in the stall's own hearth, and so had Ines's, and so had Mother Seral's, because Tobiah had knocked on both their doors before the gulls were up and said that was how it was done. Each pot had a small board over it that Ines had painted, giving the neighborhood it came from: the docks, the merchant quarter and festival street. At the second bell the festival committee came down the lane with its red stamp, and stamped the family stall's form, and stamped the Ardens' form next door. The Ardens looked at the stamp on their post as if it might fly away.

At the third bell the pots were carried out to the counter with their lids on, and opened there, in front of everyone.

Jory filled the first bowl. It was the chipped blue one. He took it from the kept-back place on the side of the counter, where Ines had set it turned over at dusk, and filled it from the middle pot, and looked across the gap between the stalls at Mrs. Arden, who had been watching the whole business with her dry, tired face.

He carried it over with both hands, and gave it to her.

"We don't—" she started.

"It's the first bowl," said Jory. "It goes to a guest. It always has."

Mrs. Arden looked at the bowl, and at him, and at the three pots and the three painted boards, and at Mother Seral standing behind them with her arms folded. Then she took the bowl in both hands and ate it standing up, the way Tobiah ate, and when she had finished she said, quietly, "That's very good," and gave him back the bowl, and a cup of her apple barley in exchange. Jory, who did not cook, stood between the two stalls and ate it.

Up the lane, on the next street over, somebody was telling somebody else that the new lot on festival street were running goods through the fish market. Jory heard it over his shoulder while he was doing something else. On this street nobody was listening.

Inspector Halloran came by at the fourth bell with his book closed under his arm. He looked at both posts, at the stamps, and at the three pots, and he bought a bowl of Tobiah's fish and pepper and ate it standing up at the end of the counter without saying a word.

Ilo Sedgewater came back at the fifth bell. He tried Ines's version again, as if checking his own judgment. He winced at the cinnamon, and held out his bowl for a second.

"Too much cinnamon," he said to Ines.

"You said," said Ines.

"I'm saying it again," said Ilo Sedgewater, and ate the second bowl to the bottom.

Last of all, when the street was emptying and the charcoal was going gray, Mother Seral came out from behind the counter with a spoon and tasted all three pots at last: the docks, the merchant quarter, festival street. She tasted them slowly, one after another, with no water between, and her three grandchildren watched her do it and did not breathe.

She put the spoon down.

"Again next year," she said, and went in to bed.
