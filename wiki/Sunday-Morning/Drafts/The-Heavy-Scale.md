## The Heavy Scale at Icestep Summit

The public scale of Icestep Summit stood under its own slate roof in the middle of the customs yard, and on the first morning of the pre-season check, Anselm Quill put his ten-stone reference weight in its pan and watched the beam tell him it weighed eleven.

He did not react. He had been trained not to react to instruments; an instrument that saw you react would never trust you again. He wrote down *eleven stone and a little over* in his book, in the column for readings, and took the reference weight out, and put it back in, and read the beam again.

Eleven stone and a little over.

It was a beautiful scale. It was a steelyard of the old plateau pattern, a single great beam of black iron on a knife-edge, with the goods pan hanging from the short arm and the long arm notched and numbered in brass. The counterweight, the town's one great stone, rode in a lidded iron cradle that hooked into the notches, latched shut against the ice; nobody lifted that lid from one season's service to the next. It had been built a hundred years ago by people who expected it to be used by a thousand caravans and trusted by all of them. The beam was polished where hands had nudged it for a century. The brass numbers were worn smooth. It was, as the customs chief had told Quill three times since his arrival the afternoon before, the pride of the town.

It was reading heavy by about a tenth.

Quill tried his five-stone weight. It read heavy by a tenth. He tried the two-stone, the one-stone, the half. Every one of them read heavy, in proportion, as if the whole world had quietly put on weight over the winter while the pass was closed.

He looked up from his book. Across the customs yard, past the stable and the water trough, the back of the Second Gate inn rose three stories into the thin cold air, with a long fresh patch of tarred sailcloth on its roof, weighted at the edges with stones. Snow lay in a deep drift below the eaves on the shady side. It was the kind of detail Quill noticed without meaning to, the way he noticed the angle of a beam: something had been mended there, and not long ago.

"Well?" said the customs chief.

Chief Dorran Pike was a large, soft-spoken man in a heavy coat, who had been the pass authority's man at Icestep for eleven years and had never, so far as Quill could tell, enjoyed a single one of them. He had stood beside the scale all morning with his hands in his sleeves, watching Quill work with the expression of a man watching a physician examine a family member.

"It reads heavy," said Quill.

"Heavy."

"By about a tenth. At every weight."

The chief closed his eyes. When he opened them, he said, very reasonably: "It's been a long winter. Things settle. Iron contracts in the cold. I'm sure it can be resolved quietly."

"*Resolved quietly*," said Quill, writing it down. He had a habit of repeating people's words back to them, exactly, as he wrote them down. They had trained him to at the weights school on the plateau, where a weigher who misquoted a witness could lose his license, and he had never found the place in himself where the habit could be switched off. "You said, *I'm sure it can be resolved quietly*."

"I did say that," said the chief, a little uneasily.

"What does *quietly* mean?"

The chief looked at the scale for a long time. "The pass opens in three days," he said. "The first caravan of the year comes up from the plateau and weighs in here, in public, in front of the whole town. It always has. And this year—" He stopped.

"This year?"

"This year the caravans are thin. You'll have heard. Attacks on the routes all last season. Houses losing loads. Half the plateau merchants are choosing other passes, or not coming up at all." He rubbed his face. "My fees are down by a third. The authority is reviewing my post. If the first caravan of the season arrives to find the town's scale has been cheating everyone all winter—"

"The scale isn't cheating anyone," said Quill. "It's reading heavy. That's not the same thing."

"It will sound the same," said the chief, "when it's told in the next town."

* * *

The Second Gate was the only inn in Icestep, and in winter it was the only warm place, and so everyone who was stranded or snowed in or simply had nowhere better to be ended up in its long low common room by evening. Quill ended up there too, because there was nowhere else to eat, and sat at the end of a bench with his book open and his back to the fire, and within a quarter of an hour Brisa Holloway had put a bowl in front of him.

"I didn't order this," said Quill.

"Nobody orders," said Brisa. "There's what there is. Eat it." She was a broad Northwind woman with flour on her forearms and a voice pitched for a room full of weather, and she had run the Second Gate for twenty years. She fed everyone who came through the door, the stranded travelers and the salt-fish trader who had been there since autumn and the customs clerks and the smith, whether they could pay or not. Quill had heard about her before he had heard about the scale. "You're the weigher."

"I'm the weigher."

"Found anything?"

Quill looked at the bowl. It was barley with salt fish and some kind of dried green herb that tasted of the sea. "I'm not able to say," he said.

"That means yes," said a woman down the bench.

She was sitting with her boots on the rail by the fire and a pipe in her hand, a lean weathered woman in a sealskin coat, and she was, Quill would learn, Tove Marrick, who had come up from the coast in late autumn with a cart of salt fish to sell on the plateau and had been caught by the first snows and had simply stayed, selling fish to the town all winter.

"I'm not able to say," Quill said again.

"It is said," said Tove, "that the scale's off." She tapped her pipe on the rail. "Don't look at me like that. I didn't see it. I don't know anyone who saw it. It is said. There's a difference."

Northwind speech made you say how you knew a thing, the same way plateau speech made you say whether you were sure of it. Quill had learned the forms at school and had never before heard anyone use them with such relish. Tove Marrick sorted everything she said into three piles: things she had seen herself; things she had been told by someone she would, as she put it, lend a boat to; and things that were merely said. She was very strict about the piles.

"When did it go off?" said Quill, before he could stop himself.

"It is said," said Tove, "after the big blow."

"The big blow?"

"The storm," said Brisa, putting bread on the table. "The worst of the winter. The night of the big blow. You'll hear about it. Everybody dates everything by it."

"When was it?"

"Midwinter," said Brisa.

"Before midwinter," said a clerk.

"A week after," said somebody else.

"The night my cart lost its wheel," said a stranded traveler, a quiet pilgrim with a Compass badge on his coat, which was no help to anyone.

"It was the night of the big blow," said Tove, with finality, "and I'll tell you when I saw it. I saw the snow come off the eaves in a sheet and fill the yard. I saw that. The rest, it is said."

Brisa refilled Quill's bowl, which he had not finished, and sat down across from him. "Did you hear," she said, "about the caravan?"

"The first caravan?"

"There's a rider came over the lower pass today, ahead of the snow on the plateau side." She leaned her elbows on the table. "He says Samir Tareh's riding with the first caravan this year."

The name went down the benches like a draft. Quill did not know it, and Brisa, seeing that, explained. Samir Tareh was a caravan negotiator from the plateau, and he was the man half the merchant houses on the routes listened to about which passes to use. After last season's attacks everybody wanted to know which roads were reliable, and Samir Tareh rode the passes every spring and wrote down which towns kept honest scales and honest tolls and safe yards, and where he said to go, the caravans went.

"He checks every scale," said Brisa. "Every single one. From here to the lowlands."

Quill looked down at his bowl, which was full again.

"Eat," said Brisa.

* * *

The customs house had one stove, one lamp worth reading by, and a window that looked straight across the yard at the Second Gate's patched roof. The winter ledger lay on the table under the lamp, and Quill sat with it until the lamp burned down.

It told him most of what he needed. Almost nothing had crossed the scale all winter. There had been no caravans, of course. The pass had been shut since the first heavy snow. But the town used the public scale for its own weighings, and every weighing was recorded: flour from the winter stores, measured out to households; salt fish, Tove's, weighed for the toll on each sale; firewood; a pig. The entries were steady through early winter. Quill compared them against the stores' own measures, and they matched, near enough.

And then, partway through the winter, they didn't. The flour weighed out to households started reading heavy against the stores' measures. It was a tenth heavy, every time, from one entry to the next, as if someone had pulled a lever.

The entry where it changed was dated, in the clerk's cramped hand, *after the blow*.

So were the six entries before it. So were the forty entries after it. The customs clerk, like everyone else in Icestep, had dated the whole second half of the winter from the storm, and had never written down when the storm was.

Quill sat back from the ledger and looked out of the window at nothing. Something had happened to the scale, once, on or near a night that nobody in the town could agree on. He needed to know when. Then he could know what had been overcharged, and to whom, and by how much, and then — he did not quite finish the thought. He wrote *after the blow = ?* in his book, underlined it twice, and went to bed.

* * *

The repair smith of Icestep was named Grell, and he maintained everything with moving parts in the town: the pumps, the gate hinges, the customs yard's great sliding door, the winch on the well, and the scale. He was the only man in Icestep who ever opened the scale's cradle. He had been rude to Quill on the afternoon of his arrival for no reason Quill could discover. And he had, according to the ledger, serviced the scale twice that winter, both times in the first weeks of the cold, and a month after the second time the readings had jumped.

Quill went to the repair shop on the second morning to ask him about it. He knew, even as he went, that he had already decided what he would find.

The shop was a low stone building at the bottom of the yard, cluttered and warm and hung with every tool the pass could ever need, and Grell was at the forge with his back to the door. He did not turn around.

"I know why you're here," he said.

"I'm here about the scale."

"I know you're here about the scale. You're the weigher." He hammered something. "You think I've been at it."

"I'd like to see your weights," said Quill.

Grell turned around then. He was a squat, grizzled man with forearms like cable, and he looked at Quill for a long, contemptuous moment. Then he took a box down from a shelf, opened it on the bench, and stepped back.

It was a service set: a full range of brass weights, the kind a scale's keeper used to check the beam after cleaning. Quill checked them against his own reference set with his hand balance, one after another, all the way down. Every one was true. They were better kept, in fact, than his own.

"Well?" said Grell.

"They're true," said Quill.

"They're true," said Grell.

"When you serviced the scale," said Quill, and made the mistake of opening his book, "the second time, you told the chief, and I'm quoting the clerk's note, *the old girl's in fine fettle, true to a hair*." He looked up. "Then why is she reading heavy?"

It was, Quill would think later, a reasonable question, asked at the worst possible moment in the worst possible way. Grell looked at him and at the open book, where his own words were written down.

"Out," he said.

"I only—"

"*Out*," said Grell, and Quill went out, and stood in the cold yard for some time with his book in his hand, wondering why he had been so sure.

* * *

The road shrine of the Infinite Compass stood at the top of the town, where the pass road left Icestep and began to climb toward the gate. It was one room of piled stone, with a painted compass rose on the floor, pegs for travelers' coats and a great book on a lectern that had been kept for as long as the shrine had stood. Every pilgrim and traveler who passed through signed it: their name, where they were going and where they had come from. And the shrine's keeper wrote in it every evening what the day's weather had been on the pass, because the Compass held that a traveler setting out deserved to know what the road had done to the travelers before.

The keeper was an old woman named Maudie Vance, who had kept the shrine for thirty years. She was very pleased to be asked.

"The blow," she said, and turned the heavy pages back. "The night of the big blow. Oh, everybody wants to know that. Let me see." She ran her finger down a column. "Here."

It was a plain entry in a small neat hand, dated precisely: day and month and year. *Great wind from the northwest from dusk; worst of the winter; the Second Gate's roof covering loose at midnight; snow off every roof by dawn; pass impassable.* Underneath, in a different hand, a pilgrim had added: *Stayed up all night. The Compass keep the innkeeper and her roof.*

Quill copied the date into his book and underlined it. Then he took out the ledger notes he had copied the night before and counted forward from the date to the entry where the flour began weighing heavy. It was the first weighing after the storm. It was the very next morning.

"Is that useful?" said Maudie Vance.

"Very," said Quill.

Tove Marrick came in while he was still writing. She had come to leave a coin for the shrine, as she did every week, she said, because the Compass looked after people on roads and she had been stuck on this one since autumn. She saw the open book and the date and came to look over his shoulder.

"There," she said. "The night I saw the Gate's roof go."

"You said you saw the snow come off the eaves," said Quill, reading from his book. "At the Second Gate, the first night. You said, *I saw the snow come off the eaves in a sheet and fill the yard. I saw that. The rest, it is said.*"

Tove stopped. She looked at him with something like respect.

"That's right," she said. "That's exactly what I said. I saw the snow. The roof—" she frowned. "No. I was told about the roof. I was asleep for the roof. Brisa's stable boy told me in the morning there'd been somebody up on the Gate's roof half the night, holding it down in the wind. Somebody I'd lend a boat to told me." She said the form carefully. "I didn't see it."

Quill wrote it down. *Told, not saw. Someone on the Second Gate roof, the night of the storm.* He looked at the words for some time. He did not yet know why they mattered. He only knew he was glad, suddenly and very much, that Tove Marrick sorted her knowledge into piles.

* * *

The news came at dusk on the second day: the plateau side of the pass was clear, the first caravan had started early, and the pass would open a day ahead of schedule. Samir Tareh would be at the scale tomorrow at noon.

Quill spent the night re-weighing his own reference set against itself.

He had been trained to do it if he ever doubted his instruments, and that night, for the first time, he doubted them. Maybe the scale was true and his set was wrong. Maybe his ten-stone had been knocked or chipped or corroded on the long journey up from the plateau, and he had spent two days insulting a town's honor because his own weights were light. He sat at the end of the Second Gate's common room with his hand balance and his box and weighed each piece against the others: ten against five and five, five against two and two and one, one against the halves, around and around, until the brass was warm from his hands.

They were true. They had always been true. He did it all again anyway.

Brisa Holloway came and sat across from him sometime after midnight, when the common room had emptied and the fire had burned low. She did not bring him a bowl. She brought two cups of something hot and put one beside his hand balance, and watched him weigh the five-stone against the two-and-two-and-one for what he suspected was the eleventh time.

"Is it you, then?" she said. "Or the scale?"

"It's the scale," said Quill. "It's always been the scale." He put the weights down. "I just don't know why."

"And when you know why?"

"Then I'll know who was overcharged, and by how much, and I'll have to say so. At Pass Opening. In front of Samir Tareh." He looked at his hands. "The chief wants it resolved quietly."

"And you don't?"

"The flour in the ledger was weighed out to families. Every sack after the storm was weighed heavy. They paid for a tenth more than they got, all winter, and they don't know." He hesitated. "It isn't much, a tenth of a sack. But they paid for it."

Brisa looked at him for a long time across the table.

"You're not what I thought," she said. "When you came in the first night, with your book and your little weights. I thought you'd be one of those who's right about everything and kind about nothing."

"I usually am," said Quill.

"Precise," said Brisa, "isn't the same as unkind. Not if you're being precise *for* somebody." She pushed the cup a little closer to him. "Drink that. It's a long way to noon."

He drank it. When she had gone, he found he had written down what she said, exactly, without meaning to, and that he did not mind.

* * *

He woke before dawn to a sound he did not recognize and lay listening to it for a long moment before he understood that it was water. It was the thaw. The wind had gone round to the south in the night, warm off the plateau, and every roof in Icestep was dripping, and in the yard the drifts were going gray and soft and sinking.

He went out with his coat over his nightshirt, because he could not have said why, and stood in the customs yard in the thin blue light and looked at the Second Gate's roof.

The drift under the eaves on the shady side had dropped by half in the night. Something dark stood out of it near the wall, at the bottom, where the snow had slid off the roof in the storm and buried whatever had fallen with it.

Quill waded in to his knees and dug it out with his hands.

It was a counterweight. It was a block of dressed stone the size of a loaf, far heavier than it looked, with an iron ring set in the top and the Icestep mark cut into its face, and under the mark, in the plateau figures every weigher learned before he learned to read: *1 stone*. It was the town's stone, the one that had ridden in the scale's cradle for a hundred years. He knew it the moment he saw it, as he would have known the scale itself.

He left it standing in the slush, went to the scale, unhooked the cradle, and lifted the lid.

The stone inside was a Northwind net-stone. He had seen them on the coast in his school year, hung by the dozen along the foot-ropes of drift nets to sink them. It was a round, smooth sea-stone with a hole bored through it for the rope, and cut neatly into its face, in coastal letters, was the Northwind mark for *one stone*.

Northwind and the plateau both called their unit of weight a stone. It was the same word in both trade tongues. It had never been the same weight. Quill had learned that too at school, on the day the teacher warned them about false friends between measures, and had forgotten it the same afternoon, because who would ever mix up a sea-stone with a scale-stone?

The net-stone was lighter. The difference was about a tenth. Every sack of flour weighed against it since the night of the storm had needed the stone hung a tenth further out along the beam to balance it, and so had read a tenth heavy.

Nobody had lied. Somebody had simply put one stone where another had been, and both of them had said *one stone*, and both had been telling the truth.

He stood in the cold yard between the two stones for a long time. Then he looked up at the patched roof of the Second Gate, at the tarred sailcloth weighted along its edges with stones, and thought of a woman up on that roof in the worst wind of the winter, trying to hold a covering down. She would have been looking for anything heavy enough to tie a corner down, and small enough to carry up a ladder. There was nothing in the yard that fitted better than the scale's counterweight.

*The Compass keep the innkeeper and her roof.*

* * *

The first caravan of the year came over the pass at noon, and the whole of Icestep was in the customs yard to see it weigh in.

It always had been. Pass Opening meant the first bells up the road, the first beasts through the gate, and the first loads weighed on the public scale while the town watched to see that it was done right, and then the ice breaking in the tarn below the town, and supper, and the season beginning. Northwind folk and plateau folk alike had stood in this yard every spring for a hundred years to see it happen.

Samir Tareh rode in at the head of the caravan. He was a tall, quiet man in a traveling coat, with a route book in a satchel over his shoulder, and he looked, Quill thought, more tired than a man should look at noon on the first day of a season. He dismounted, greeted the chief, and walked straight to the scale.

The chief had spent the morning trying to persuade Quill to weigh the caravan's goods against his reference set, discreetly, in the customs house, and correct the figures afterward on paper. Quill had listened to all of it and written it all down.

"Master Tareh," Quill said, "before we weigh anything, the pass authority's weigher has to report a fault on this scale."

The yard went silent. The chief made a small sound.

Quill reported it. He did it plainly and exactly, the way he had been trained, and he did not hurry. The scale had been reading heavy by one part in ten since the morning after the storm. He named the date from the shrine log, and heard, around the yard, a murmur of people who had been arguing about the date all winter and had just lost. He explained the cause: the town's stone had been lost off the Second Gate's roof in the storm and replaced before morning with a Northwind net-stone that also said *one stone* and was not. He held up both stones, one in each hand, so the whole yard could see them.

He did not say who. He did not have to. Brisa Holloway was standing near the front of the crowd with flour on her forearms, and he watched her face as she understood, and it was one of the worst things he had ever seen.

"I didn't know," she said. It came out very small in the big voice. "I needed something heavy. The roof was going. I took the stone, and then the wind took the stone, and I thought — I thought one stone was one stone. I put ours in. I didn't know."

"You said," said Quill, and he opened his book, and the whole yard watched him do it, and he saw Brisa flinch. He read it exactly, as he had written it down, the words and the speaker and the place. "*Precise isn't the same as unkind. Not if you're being precise for somebody.* Brisa Holloway. The Second Gate. Last night."

He closed the book.

"The ledger's worked back to the storm night," he said to the yard. "Every weighing since then is recorded. I can tell you what every household was overcharged for its flour, to the half-measure. I can tell you what Tove Marrick was overcharged in tolls on her salt fish. It's written down. It can all be paid back."

There was a pause, and then the whole town began shouting at once.

It went on for some time. Quill had been warned that Icestep settled things by shouting, and it was true. Somebody said the town should pay. Somebody said the pass authority should pay. The chief said, loudly, that the authority would certainly not pay and also that it was nobody's fault. Somebody said it was the storm's fault and the storm should pay. Brisa said she would pay it all herself, and at least forty people shouted her down. Then somebody said that a tenth of a sack of flour came to less than a bowl of supper per household, near enough. Somebody else said then give them a supper, then. The Second Gate always fed everybody at Pass Opening anyway, and this year the town could pay for it, and every household that had been overcharged could eat what it was owed.

Quill did the arithmetic in his book, standing there in the shouting. It came out close enough that he said so, and the yard cheered as though he had done something clever.

Tove Marrick walked up to him through the crowd.

"My tolls," she said. "What does it come to?"

He told her. It was not a small sum; salt fish was heavy, and she had sold a great deal of it.

"Coin," said Tove. "I want it in coin. I saw what I was charged. I want to see what I'm paid."

The chief, with visible pain, counted it out of the customs chest into her hand. Tove weighed the coins in her palm for a moment, looking at them. Then she turned and walked across the yard to Brisa Holloway and put every one of them into Brisa's floury hands.

"For the supper," she said. "Buy something that isn't salt fish."

Samir Tareh had watched all of it from beside the scale without saying a word. When the yard had quieted, and Grell the smith had been sent for, and the town's own stone had been washed and hung back in its cradle and the reference weights had read true on the beam, Samir weighed in his caravan's first bale himself. He watched the beam settle, nodded once to Quill, and took out his route book.

* * *

The supper at the Second Gate ran late. It was always going to.

Brisa cooked as if she were trying to outdo the storm: brined fish crusted in plateau spices, which the coast folk said was too hot and the plateau folk said was not nearly hot enough; fish cakes fried in stacks; caravan flatbread by the basket. Everyone who had been overcharged came and ate what they were owed, and then went on eating what they weren't, and nobody counted.

Quill wrote his report at the end of a bench, with a bowl at his elbow that Brisa kept refilling. It was short. It said the scale had been tested, found to read heavy, and corrected. It named the date and the amount. Under *Cause*, after some thought, he wrote: *Weather.*

He took his bowl over to where Grell was sitting alone near the door, and sat down across from him.

"I was wrong," he said. "About you. I'd decided before I came to your shop. I'm sorry."

Grell looked at him over his fish.

"You were," he said. "You did." He went back to his fish. After a while, without looking up, he said, "Your ten-stone's got a scratch on the base. Bring it round in the morning and I'll dress it."

The chief stood up in the middle of the room, called for quiet, and gave a short speech about the pass authority's commitment to honest weights and the town's pride in its public scale, and how proud he was that Icestep had chosen to correct its error openly, in front of the whole season's trade. Everyone let him. Quill, who had it all written down, let him too.

Late in the evening Samir Tareh found a place on the bench beside Quill. He had his route book open. He turned it so Quill could see the page.

The entries were short, in a neat plateau hand. There were passes, towns, water, yards and scales, a line or two for each. The line above the last said: *Seven Wells — water and guides, one roof.* Below it, freshly written: *Icestep — scale honest, corrected in public.*

"Most years," Samir said, "I find a wrong scale somewhere and the town swears it's right. This year every report I get is worse than the truth, or better than it. Every pass says the other passes are unsafe. Every yard says its own is the only one." He closed the book. "An honest scale is useful. A town that tells me its scale was wrong, in front of everyone, before I can find it for myself, is rarer than that. I can send caravans to a town like that."

"And the others?"

Samir was quiet for a moment. "There's a pass town further east," he said, "whose scale has been reading wrong all winter, always in the town's favor. They showed me the stamp on it and swore to it. It isn't true." He said it without any pleasure at all. "It won't see my caravans this year. That will cost them a great deal. I'm sorry for it. But I can't send people where the weights lie."

At the other end of the table, the caravan's cook had been given a bowl of Brisa's fish and was eating it with the expression of a man making professional notes. He was a Deepwood man called Ilo Sedgewater, who had cooked for the caravans since the blight took his village's mushroom harvest the summer before. He had been complaining since the moment he came through the gate.

"Mushrooms," he said to nobody in particular. "Have you seen what they want for mushrooms on the plateau? Forest mushrooms? More than meat. More than *meat*. I've cooked forty years and I never thought I'd see a mushroom cost more than a goat." He took another mouthful of fish and chewed it critically. "This is good," he conceded. "Too much pepper."

"It's the plateau spice," said Brisa, going past with bread.

"It's too much of it," said Ilo Sedgewater, and held out his bowl for more.

Near the fire, Tove Marrick had her boots up on the rail again and her pipe going, and a ring of stranded travelers and caravan hands and customs clerks around her. She was telling the story of the scale. She told all of it, from the storm to the stone in the drift, and she tagged every part of it properly.

"And then the weigher," she said, "stood in the yard with a stone in each hand, in front of the whole town and Samir Tareh, and told everyone exactly what they were owed."

She drew on her pipe. She looked across the room at Quill, and at Brisa beside him with the breadbasket, and at the chief finishing his speech for the second time.

"I saw it," she said.
