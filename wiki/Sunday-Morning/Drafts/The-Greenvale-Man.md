# The Greenvale Man — draft

## Status

**L3 draft. Provisional, non-canon.** Prose draft of [The Greenvale Man](../Stories/The-Greenvale-Man.md). Its pass history is in the story page's Stage 3 — DO section. Not yet reviewed at L4. Kettle Cove, Narrow Sound, the regatta and every character are provisional; see the [promotion rule](../Notes/Rules.md#promotion-rule). Northwind's two "we"s and its evidential forms are rendered in English, following [Language and Thought](../../Culture/Language-and-Thought.md#northwind-languages); no conlang words are invented.

**Fourth pass (uniqueness):** the story opens on the haul-out, not on a line of dialogue, since five of the seven drafts opened that way.

**Third pass:** prose only. It uses medium sentences by default, fewer punchline scene endings, and humor that differs by character. Hild collects rumors the way other people collect shells. Aldo is polite to a fault. Rask uses grammar as a weapon. Brenna is blunt. No beats were added; the story's weight is already in the "we" and in Narrow Sound's lost boat.

Story 2 of 7 in [Collection](../Notes/Collection.md). Year 1, late autumn.

## The Greenvale Man

They hauled the *Kittiwake* out three weeks before Last Sail, and half of Kettle Cove came down to the slipway to watch, because that was how the cove did things: nobody could fix anything until everybody had seen it. The crack was on the landward side, along the fourth plank below the gunwale, as fine as a hair and as long as a man's arm.

"That's sprung," said somebody.

"That's rot," said somebody else.

"That's neither." Brenna Scarth was on her knees in the shingle with her face an inch from the hull. "Something hit it. Ice, probably, in the spring. It's opened along the grain."

Aldo Fenwright stood at the back of the crowd. He had lived in the cove for twenty years and had learned in the first of them that the back of a Kettle Cove crowd was where a Greenvale man stood.

Brenna sat back on her heels. "It'll hold on a calm day. It won't hold for a race."

The whole slipway breathed out at once.

Last Sail meant the regatta against Narrow Sound, across the headland. Narrow Sound had beaten the *Kittiwake* three years running, and the regatta bell hung in their tower now, and everybody in Kettle Cove knew to the day how long it had been there.

"We'll write to your father," said Old Rask.

There were two ways to say *we* in Northwind. One had room in it for whoever you were talking to, and one didn't. Most people used the narrow one for family and the wide one for the cove. Rask used the narrow one for the cove, and he never once used the wide one when Aldo could hear.

"We've already had a letter from him," Brenna said, and took it out of her coat. "It came yesterday."

She read it aloud, because that was also how the cove did things. Old Scarth wrote the way he talked, in short sentences dropped like planks. The country was too flat. You could see a storm coming for a day and a half and there was nothing to do but watch it. His knees were no better and his son was well. The town he lived in now, Harveston Vale, where even the square had two names, had just held two festivals at the same time in the same square and eaten everything, and he had never seen anything like it. He hoped the *Kittiwake* was sound for Last Sail.

"He'd want to know," somebody said.

"We could borrow," somebody else said, and the slipway went quiet in a different way.

There was only one place near enough to borrow a racing boat from, and it was Narrow Sound. To race Narrow Sound in Narrow Sound's own spare boat would be talked about, Hild said later, until the sea dried up, and then they'd talk about that.

"We don't borrow from them," said Rask. It was the narrow *we*. He was looking at the crack, and Aldo was standing right behind him.

---

"No," Aldo said.

"I didn't ask," said Brenna.

"You're going to. I make barrels."

The cooperage stood at the landward end of the drying racks, out of the wind. Everyone who came in said first that it was warm and then that it was quiet. Along one wall stood the steam box Aldo had built his first winter in the cove, back when he still believed good work was enough to win a town over. It had won him Hild, at least, which he privately considered the better half of the bargain.

Brenna held her hands over the steam box. "You steam oak till it bends and you fit it so tight it holds water without pitch. I've watched you do it."

"A barrel isn't a boat."

"A plank's a long stave. My father used to say so."

"Your father was being kind to me." Aldo picked up his drawknife. "And I get seasick. On the ferry. Once on the slipway, when it was windy."

At the back of the room, where she was mending a net across two trestles, Hild laughed. She had been laughing since she heard about the crack.

"He did," she said. "On the slipway. I was there."

"Thank you, Hild."

"Any time."

One of the young rowers had come to the door behind Brenna to see what she wanted, and he grinned. "Soft as Greenvale butter."

He meant it as a joke, mostly. It was the kind of thing the cove said. Brenna turned and looked at him until he went away.

Aldo never answered when somebody said that. He went back to the stave on his bench, and for the rest of the afternoon he thought about the fourth plank, and Hild, mending, watched him think about it and let him.

---

He went to the boat shed the next morning to look. Only to look.

Up close, a racing hull was a set of thin planks curved two ways at once and fastened edge to edge, holding water by being tight. He could see where the cracked plank would come out. He could see how the new piece would have to be steamed, bent over a form and left to set, then fitted and taken out and fitted again until the joins disappeared. He could see all of it the way he could see a barrel in a pile of oak, and he stood there longer than he had meant to.

"Well?" Brenna said behind him.

"You'd have to tell me how it's fastened, and where it has to flex. I don't know boats."

"I know boats. I can't build them."

"Then between us," Aldo said, "we know one boat." He heard himself say it and was sorry at once.

Hild had followed them down with a basket of bread. "He's saying yes," she told Brenna. "That's what yes sounds like in Greenvale. It sounds like complaining."

---

The clan hall met in the first week, and Rask stood up to object. He did it properly, on his feet, with his hands on the back of the bench in front of him.

"The *Kittiwake* is the cove's boat. She was built by a cove man and sailed by cove crews for twenty years. And we do not hand our boat to—" He paused, and everyone heard the pause. "—to a barrel-maker. However good his barrels."

He didn't look at Aldo. Aldo was at the back of the hall, where he always sat.

"It's the Greenvale man or Narrow Sound's spare," said Brenna.

"Or we don't race."

That got a murmur, some of it for and some against. Then the planking came up, and there wasn't any. Good seasoned oak was gone everywhere this late in the year; the yards up the coast had sold what they had to the convoys, and what was left was green and would split in the steam box. Aldo said so. Rask said so too, and seemed pleased about it.

Then the door opened and a messenger came in off the headland road with salt on his coat and a letter under his arm, and the hall forgot about the boat.

The letter was from Maris Bleakshore. Everyone knew the name. She had been up and down the coast all summer about the raids, which had been worse this year than anyone could remember: boats taken, drying racks burned, a whole catch lost off the northern skerries. Now she was asking every cove to name the coves it would sail with in convoy next season. Coves it could trust, the letter said. Coves whose waters were clean.

The messenger stayed for a cup of something hot. He'd been through three coves in three days, he said, and in every one of them people were asking about Narrow Sound.

"Asking what?"

He shrugged. "It is said they've given water to raiders. That their headland has a cove nobody watches." He drank. "It is said."

He was careful about it, and used the rumor form every time.

The hall heard it anyway. Aldo watched it land. He watched Rask's face not change, and the young rower lean over and say something low to the man beside him, and for a moment the argument about a boat turned into an argument about who could be trusted. Then Brenna asked about the planking again and the moment passed.

"We'll talk about Narrow Sound," Rask said, "before Last Sail."

---

Gossip in Kettle Cove moved at the speed of fish-turning. The drying racks ran the whole length of the harbor wall, and all autumn the cove turned the split fish by hand twice a day, down one row and up the next. It was cold, slow work that nobody could do without talking, and whatever was said at the top of the racks in the morning had reached the bottom by the afternoon turn.

By the second week there were two rumors on the racks.

The first was about Aldo, and Hild brought it home every evening the way other people brought home shells from the beach, turning each one over to show him.

"It is said," she reported, "that you've pulled the whole side off the *Kittiwake* and put in staves."

"I haven't."

"It is said you're hooping her."

"I'm not."

"It is said she'll roll."

"Hild."

"I'm going to tell them you've found a bung-hole."

"Please don't."

The second rumor was about Narrow Sound, and nobody brought that one home for fun.

Narrow Sound's children spied on the boat shed that week. Everybody's children spied on everybody's sheds before Last Sail; it was practically part of the festival. Two of them lay on the shingle bank above the shed door every afternoon in plain view and carried everything they saw back across the headland. Aldo waved at them. After a few days they waved back.

One of them was the niece of Narrow Sound's champion rower, Sigra Ulfsen, who was fast and tall and everybody's cousin, Brenna's included. Her uncle had lost his boat to raiders that summer. Kettle Cove had sent food.

"And now it is said they shelter them," Hild said, turning fish beside him on the one afternoon he came to help. It wasn't her shell voice.

Aldo turned a fish and didn't answer. He was thinking about staves and hoops, and how nobody in the cove had actually been inside the shed except Brenna and Hild and, once, from the doorway, Rask.

---

The storms came in the second week and kept coming.

There was no launching in weather like that. The *Kittiwake* sat in the shed with a gap in her side where the old plank had been, and the new plank hung in its form beside the steam box, setting. Nobody had seen it on the boat, because it wasn't on the boat yet, and nobody had seen the boat float, because there was no weather to float it in. So by every rule of Northwind speech, the *Kittiwake*'s seaworthiness was a rumor. *It is said she'll float.* Aldo found this funnier than anyone else did.

He did not find the plank funny. It was green oak, too green. He had got one piece out of it by steaming it slower than he liked and bending it in stages, and it had held the form, but he didn't trust it, and neither did Brenna.

On the fourth night of the storms somebody knocked on the cooperage door, and it was Rask, with snow on his shoulders and a long bundle wrapped in sailcloth under one arm. He unwrapped it on the bench without a word. It was oak — pale, close-grained and old, seasoned for years in somebody's rafters.

"My father's," Rask said. "He put it by for a boat he never built. It's been in my roof since before you came." He looked at the oak and not at Aldo. "It's for the cove's boat. We look after our own."

It was the narrow *we*. But he had carried it through a snowstorm to the Greenvale man's door, and when the door had shut behind him Hild had stopped mending and was looking at it with an expression Aldo had not seen on her face before.

"That," she said, "is the most anyone in his family has ever given anyone."

---

The storms broke four days before Last Sail. The new plank went in the next morning, and it was Rask's father's oak, steamed slow over two storm nights; the green plank had gone on the cooperage fire. It was fitted, taken out, fitted again and fastened, and by the afternoon the joins had disappeared.

A boat with a new plank always weeps at first. The wood drinks and swells and closes the last gaps, and until it does somebody has to be aboard to bail, and to watch the seam, and to say whether the weeping is the ordinary kind or the kind that means the plank comes out again. Brenna would sail her, and Brenna said, loud enough for the whole slipway, that she wanted the man who'd fitted the plank aboard to watch it.

Aldo went.

He was sick before they cleared the harbor mouth, and three more times on the way out to the point. In between he bailed with a cooper's wooden scoop, steadily, not fast, watching the seam along the fourth plank where the water beaded through and ran, and then beaded less, and ran less, and stopped. When it stopped he put his hand flat on the plank from inside and held it there, and felt the boat go stiff and sure under him the way a barrel did when the staves took up. Then he was sick again.

Old Rask watched from the end of the slipway the whole time. He didn't go back for his coat when the wind got up. He stood with his hands in his sleeves and watched the *Kittiwake* go out to the point and come back, with the Greenvale man gray-faced in the bottom of her and one hand on the new plank.

When they brought her in, Rask walked down the slipway into the shallows in his boots and looked at the seam. It was dry. He didn't say anything. He walked back up the slipway and went home.

---

That night the clan hall met to vote on Last Sail.

It was a full hall. The convoy letter lay on the table, and the messenger had come back through that afternoon with more from up the coast: *it is said* that Narrow Sound's headland had hidden raiders' boats in the summer, *it is said* that a Narrow Sound family had taken payment. Half the hall wanted the regatta called off. You didn't race people you couldn't trust, they said, and you didn't sail with them either, and the sooner the cove told Maris Bleakshore so, the better.

Rask stood up with his hands on the bench.

"Who saw it?" he said.

Nobody answered.

"Narrow Sound sheltering raiders. Who in this hall saw it?"

Nobody.

"Who heard it from someone they'd trust with their boat?"

Somebody coughed.

"I have seen the Greenvale man bail a boat he mended." He used the sight form, and said it slowly so the whole hall could hear which form it was. "I saw it today, from the slipway. I have not seen Narrow Sound shelter anyone. It is said. That's all it is." He sat down. "We race."

Brenna heard the next morning from a cousin that three coves up the coast had already sent Maris Bleakshore their convoy lists without Narrow Sound on them. Kettle Cove put Narrow Sound on its list that week.

---

Last Sail came in cold and clear. Half of Narrow Sound walked round the headland to watch from Kettle Cove's shingle, because the course ran past it, as it always had.

Before the start, Sigra Ulfsen came down Kettle Cove's slipway in her racing gear, found Rask, and said, "Thank you." She didn't say what for. Rask nodded once.

The race was close. Frosted mead cakes went round the shingle in baskets and people ate them with cold fingers and shouted. The *Kittiwake* was ahead at the point, behind at the turn and level on the long run home. Aldo watched from the roof of the cooperage, which Hild said was the only place in the cove high enough to see the race and low enough that he wouldn't be sick.

Narrow Sound won by half a length, and the bell stayed across the headland.

The cove walked home slowly along the harbor wall in the long cold light, and Rask told the story of the test launch to anyone who would listen. He told all of it in the sight form. I saw him go out. I saw him sick at the harbor mouth. I saw the seam dry.

At the end of the wall, where the path split up toward the clan hall and down toward the cooperage, Rask stopped and looked at the water for a while, and then he looked at Aldo.

"We'll take them next year," he said.

It was the wide *we*, and Aldo was the one listening. Hild took his arm, and they went down the path.

---

A letter came from Greenvale the week after Last Sail. Old Scarth hoped they had raced. The country was still flat. He wanted to know only one thing, which was whether the plank had held.

Brenna wrote back that it had. She told him who had fitted it, and that the Greenvale man had been sick all the way out and had bailed anyway, and she thought her father would probably laugh at that.

The Long Dark Feast was a month away. The first winter grain had come up the coast on the last barges before the ice, and Hild brought home a sack of flour for the feast bread and dropped it on the cooperage bench and went to fetch the bowl.

Aldo looked at the sack. It carried a Sunplains house's purchase stamp, cut slightly crooked: COMMONS / PRESS YARD on one line, and PRESS YARD / COMMONS on the next. He laughed out loud.

Hild came back with the bowl and looked at him.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's a Greenvale thing."

"Everything's a Greenvale thing with you," she said, and started on the bread.

That afternoon there was a knock on the cooperage door. It was the young rower, the one who'd said the thing about butter, holding his cap in both hands. His family needed a new water barrel before the ice, he said, if the Greenvale man had time.

Aldo said he had time.
