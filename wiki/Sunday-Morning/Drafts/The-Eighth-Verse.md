## The Eighth Verse

The second cheese from the end on the top shelf was sweating, and it had no business to be.

Ulla Quarrie couldn't have told you how she knew, from the bottom of the steps, that one of them was wrong. The cellar under the meeting house had a smell, cold stone and sacking and a hundred barrels of apples keeping their breath in, and over all of it the co-op's cheeses in their cloths, a dozen kinds of sour. Somewhere in that there was a note that hadn't been there yesterday. Wetter. She went along the shelf with her lamp, sniffing like a dog, and found it, and turned back the cloth, and there was the rind gone slick and shining.

She moved it down to the cold end, by the wall, and turned it over. Caught today, it would keep. Left till the end of the week, it'd be for the pigs.

"Ulla."

It wasn't so much a voice as a draft with words in it. Vessa Ruel was standing at the top of the cellar steps with a shawl round her head and another round her throat, holding the rail.

"You shouldn't be out," Ulla said.

Vessa came down anyway, one step at a time. She'd had the cough since the first frost, the deep barking kind that goes round a village in winter and stays longest with the oldest, and somewhere in the last week it had taken her voice and not given it back. What was left was a whisper that carried further than most people's shouting, with an edge on it you could split kindling with.

"Tonight," Vessa said.

"I know it's tonight."

"I can't sing it."

Ulla had been expecting this for about four days, ever since the cough settled in, and she'd spent the four days hoping that if she didn't think about it, it wouldn't happen. She went on along the shelf.

"Somebody else'll lead," she said. "Fallon's girl. She's got a voice."

"Fallon's girl doesn't know the order."

"So tell her the order."

"She doesn't know the *verses*." Vessa got to the bottom step and sat on it. "Seven verses, seven doors. Every year since the Blight. Who else knows all seven?"

Ulla didn't answer. She knew all seven. Everybody in the Bend knew the first verse and most knew the second, and after that people came in on the last line of each, loudly, the way you do when you're sure of the end and not the middle. But Ulla had walked the round behind Vessa every Root Cellar Night since she was small enough to carry the lantern, and she'd heard every verse at every door, and they were in her whether she liked it or not.

"I can't sing," she said.

"I know."

"No, I mean I *can't*. You've heard me."

"Everyone's heard you." Vessa coughed, a dry one, and pressed her shawl to her mouth. "Doesn't matter. Nobody's listening to the tune. They're listening to see if you know the words."

"They'll be listening to the tune."

"Then let them." Vessa looked round the cellar, at the barrels and the sacks and the long shelves. "You know every cellar in this village better than the people who own them. You can do one night of standing in front of them."

That was the other thing. Ulla was the Bend's cellar warden, and had been since old Pettigrew's knees gave out. It meant she went round. Every household's stores, once a fortnight in winter: apples, roots, grain, cheese, cider. She sniffed, she poked, she turned things over, she told people what was going and what would keep, and then she went on to the next. She'd never in her life sat down in somebody's cellar and had the cup. A warden checks and goes. That was how she'd been taught, and it suited her.

"I'll think about it," she said.

"You've got till dusk."

Vessa got herself back up the steps, one at a time, and Ulla stood in the cellar with the lamp and tried to remember the tune of the first verse, and couldn't, and tried again, and got something that might have been the tune of a different song.

---

The young man from the reform circle came in out of the cold just as the lamps were being lit in the meeting house, and for a while nobody could get any sense out of him, because his teeth were going.

He'd walked from the market town downriver, he said, when he could say anything. Since before light. The road was ice the whole way. His name was Yorick Fell. He had a satchel slung across him with a stack of papers in it, and he kept one hand on it the whole time somebody was rubbing his feet, as though the Bend might take it off him.

He was all right once he'd had soup. He was more than all right. He stood up by the fire with his paper and his cheeks pink and read it out to the room, and you could tell he'd read it out a dozen times already in a dozen meeting houses, and that he believed every word of it more each time.

"*To the cooperative villages of the river,*" he read, "*from the reform circles, in common cause.*" He looked up. "That's all of us. Seven circles. Eleven villages have signed so far."

It was a notice. It asked two things. The first was that every village of the river, on Root Cellar Night, add to its cellar song an eighth verse, *for the year the cellar rats came*: the year the buyers came up the river after the harvest failed to sell, and bought up people's debts, and then their fields. The second was that each village put its mark to the circles' joint statement. The buying-up of farms all along the river, the statement said, wasn't bad luck at all. It was coordinated theft.

There was a murmur when he got to *theft*. Some of it was the sort of murmur that agrees with you.

"They've been here," somebody said from the back. "The buyers' men."

"They've been everywhere," Yorick said. "That's what it is. That's the point. It isn't one village having a bad year. It's the same men, the same offers, all up the river. Somebody planned this." He held up the paper. "We're not asking anyone to do anything. Just to sing it. So it's sung. So nobody forgets it was done to us."

Ulla was standing at the back by the kitchen door, and she watched the room do what a room does when nobody wants to be the one who says no to a cold young man who's walked all day. It went quiet, and the quiet sounded a lot like yes.

Vessa was sitting by the fire with her shawls. She beckoned Ulla over and whispered in her ear, and Ulla straightened up and said it out loud for her, because that was apparently her job now too.

"Vessa says the order's the same as always."

People nodded. Everybody knew the order. The co-op cellar first, under their feet. Then the Wicks, then the Fallons, then old Pettigrew's, then the Yeos, the Elwins, and last of all the Kembles, out at the end of the lane where the bank went down to the reeds. It had always been the Kembles last. Nobody had to say why. It was just where the round ended.

Then somebody did a sum in their head, and you could see it go round the room.

The Kembles had sold. Not the house, but the fields, in the autumn, to one of the buyers, and they'd stayed on as tenants on what used to be theirs. Jessop Kemble still worked it. He just didn't own it anymore. Not a word had been said to them about it. People had been kind, in the Bend way, which meant keeping quiet and leaving a basket of eggs on the step.

The eighth verse would come after the seventh. And the seventh was sung at the Kemble door.

Yorick hadn't done the sum. He didn't know the order, or the Kembles, or anything about the Bend except that it was on his list. He was handing out copies of the verse to anyone who'd take one.

---

Ulla's copy said *cellar cats*.

She read it twice to be sure. *The eighth year came the cellar cats, and bought the fields from under.* She turned the paper over. It was in a careful hand that had got less careful toward the bottom, the way your writing does when you've copied a thing twenty times by lamplight and you're cold and your hand hurts.

"It says cats," she said.

Yorick took it from her and looked at it, and went red right up to the hair. "That's the last lot. I did those at the inn. It's *rats*. It should say *rats*."

But Fallon's youngest had seen it over Ulla's elbow, and by the time Yorick had found his pen and corrected Ulla's copy, the word had gone out of the kitchen and into the meeting house, and the children were meowing.

It didn't stop. Ulla practiced in the meeting house kitchen with Vessa whispering the tune at her, and every time she got to the end of a verse there was a small meow from under the table. She had the first verse more or less, if more or less was a tune you could find in the dark. The second was worse. By the fourth, Vessa had stopped whispering the tune and was just whispering "No," gently, every few notes.

"It's flat," Ulla said.

"It's flat," Vessa agreed.

"I told you."

"You did." Vessa sipped her honey water. "Sing it anyway."

"Why?"

Vessa just looked at her, and Ulla started the fifth verse.

Somewhere around the sixth she realized that out in the meeting house people had started trying the eighth. Not all of them. A few voices, in a corner, working out how the words fitted the tune. *The eighth year came the cellar*—and then a pause, and then about half of them said *rats* and the other half said *cats*, and everyone laughed, and they did it again. It was getting popular. It was getting funny. Ulla stood in the kitchen with the song in her mouth and didn't like that one bit, and didn't know why yet.

---

They started at the co-op cellar at full dark, because you always did.

Thirty or so of them came, with lanterns, wrapped up to the eyes. The Kembles weren't among them. They'd walked every round anyone could remember, Jessop at the back with the big lantern. Nobody mentioned it, and everybody counted. Ulla took Vessa's place at the head of the cellar steps under the meeting house, and looked at them looking at her, and opened her mouth.

It was every bit as bad as she'd said it would be. Worse, because outside in the cold her voice went thin and wandered off the note and didn't come back. But she knew the words, every one, and when she got to the last line the whole round came in on it like a door slamming, *and we kept, and we kept, and we kept*, and that drowned her out, which was a mercy.

Then everybody trooped down into the cellar for the cup, and Ulla stayed where she was, at the head of the steps.

"Your pippins are fine," she called down. "The russets want using. Not tonight. This week."

"Yes, Ulla," said about six people.

The Wicks were next. Their door was half under a hedge, and the cellar behind it smelled of onions and wet sacking and, very faintly, of something going in the far corner. Ulla sang the second verse, *The second year we ate the seed / and the spring came up in weeds*, and hit about one note in three, and the round came in on the end like they'd been waiting all their lives for it.

"Your onions are going at the back," she told Wick, from the doorway, while the cups went round inside.

"They're not."

"They are. Left-hand corner. Under the sacks."

Wick went and looked, and came back up with a face, and gave her a cup of cider to take out with her, which she held and didn't drink.

At the Fallons' she sang the third verse, and at old Pettigrew's the fourth. Pettigrew couldn't do the steps anymore, so his grandson held the lamp and Pettigrew sat in his chair in the doorway with a blanket round his knees and sang along with every word, very loudly and even flatter than Ulla, and that cheered her up more than anything had all day.

"Your carrots," she told him.

"I know about the carrots."

"I'm only saying."

"You're always only saying." He patted her hand. "You sing like a gate, Ulla Quarrie."

"I know."

"It's good. Keep doing it."

When the round moved on, he made two of his grandsons carry him along behind it in his chair, blanket and all.

Somewhere between Pettigrew's and the Yeos', somebody at the back started the eighth verse early. Just the first line, *the eighth year came the*—and a couple of others picked it up, and then somebody hushed them, not because it was wrong, but because it wasn't time. You sang the verses in order. Everybody knew that.

Yorick was walking near the front with his satchel, looking pleased. Every so often one of the children would meow at him, and he'd smile a stiff sort of smile and go red again.

---

The Yeos kept their cellar out behind the house, dug into the side of a little rise, with a stone step in front of it worn into a dip by about two hundred years of people standing on it. Ulla sang the fifth verse there, *The fifth year we boiled the harness / and called it soup, and laughed*, and the round came in on the end and went down for the cup. Ulla sat down on the step.

After a while Vessa came and sat down next to her.

It was a clear night, and very cold. The stars were out over the reeds, more of them than you'd think there was room for, and the frost had got onto everything, the grass and the hedges and the edge of the step, so that the lanterns down the lane all had little haloes round them. From inside the cellar came the sound of thirty people talking at once with cider in them: the Yeos' cheese being argued about, somebody's bad knee, somebody's daughter. The cellar breathed out at their backs, warm and sour.

They sat a good while without talking. Ulla's feet went numb. She didn't mind. Her throat hurt from singing. She didn't mind that either.

"You know what the seventh's about," Vessa said at last.

"The seventh year."

"The seventh year." Vessa pulled her shawl tighter. "Everything was gone by then. Every cellar on the river was empty, and the ones that weren't, people sat in them with the door barred. And a Kemble opened theirs."

"Which Kemble?"

"Nobody knows. Some Kemble. Jessop's great-great-somebody." Vessa waved a hand at the dark, at the whole long run of years. "There've been Kembles in that bank since before there was a Bend."

Ulla knew the words. *The seventh year we had one cellar / and the whole Bend fit inside.* She'd sung them, or heard them sung, every year of her life. She'd never once thought about whose cellar it was.

"The whole Bend," Vessa said. "All winter. Out of that one door. They'd been putting by against a bad year for as long as anyone could remember, and everyone laughed at them for it, and then the bad year came." She coughed into the shawl. "That's why it's last. You end the round where you were fed."

Down in the cellar somebody started singing something else, not the Cellar Song, a drinking song, and somebody else told them to stop.

"Does Yorick know?" Ulla said.

"How would he?"

"Somebody ought to tell him."

Vessa looked at her sideways in the lantern light. "Somebody ought to," she agreed, and didn't, and nor did Ulla. They sat there on the cold step until the round came up out of the cellar and it was time to go on.

---

At the Elwins' Ulla sang the sixth verse, and while the round was down in the cellar a boy came running back up the lane from the far end, where he'd gone to see.

"The Kemble door's shut," he said. "And their lamp's not lit."

Everyone heard. You could feel it go through them.

On Root Cellar Night every house on the round lit a lamp over its cellar door, so the round could find it. Every house, every year. A door with no lamp meant nobody there was expecting anyone.

"Then it'll mean something," Yorick said.

Ulla had been watching him all evening, and there was nothing cruel in him. He'd been warmed up and fed and he'd been walking at the front all night with the whole round behind him, and he'd forgotten for a while how cold he'd been. His eyes were bright.

"My uncle sold," he said to her, low. "Two harvests back, downriver. Nobody asked him twice. They don't have to ask twice. That's how they do it." He took a breath. "So yes. Let it mean something."

Ulla let that be. There wasn't anything she could have told him that he'd have heard tonight. The cider she hadn't drunk was cold in her hand, and she poured it out on the hedge.

---

The Kemble cellar was the oldest in the Bend. It went straight into the bank at the end of the lane, where the ground dropped down toward the frozen reeds, and its door was stone, a thick slab of it on iron pins, with a little shelf above it for the lamp. The shelf was empty. The house behind it was dark except for one window at the back.

The round came down the lane slowly, and bunched up in front of the door, and stopped.

For a while the only sound was the lanterns creaking on their handles and somebody's boots on the frost.

At the back, quite quietly, somebody started the eighth verse. *The eighth year came the cellar*— and stopped, as if they'd surprised themselves, and a couple of others started it again from the beginning, a little louder, and Yorick was smiling, and the children were getting ready to shout *cats*.

Ulla opened her mouth and sang the seventh.

*The seventh year we had one cellar—*

It was dreadful. It was the worst she'd sung all night. Her voice cracked on *cellar* and went off somewhere on its own, and the voices at the back that had been doing the eighth stumbled and stopped, because you couldn't sing over somebody, not in the round, not when they were doing the right verse in the right place. Ulla sang the first line by herself in the dark, flat as a board, and then went to sing the second line, and it wasn't there.

She'd known it all her life. It was gone. There was the cold, and the dark door, and thirty people looking at her, and nothing in her head at all.

"*And the whole Bend*," Vessa whispered at her elbow.

"*And the whole Bend*—" Ulla sang.

And the whole Bend did.

They came in on it all at once, the way they came in on the last lines, except it wasn't the last line, it was the middle, and they'd never sung the middle before. Old Pettigrew, and the Wicks, and the Fallons, and the Yeos and the Elwins, and the children, every one of them, *the whole Bend fit inside*, and then on into the rest of it, the bit about the door and the bit about the bread. Half of them didn't know the words. They sang them anyway, a word behind, or made up something that sounded about right. Ulla was singing too, somewhere in the middle of it, and she couldn't hear herself at all, which was the best thing that had happened to her voice in her whole life.

Then it was over, and it was very quiet.

Nobody sang the eighth. Ulla never found out if anybody had been going to. Yorick was up near the front with the paper still in his hand, not singing anything, looking at the door.

The lamp came on at the back window. Then there was a scrape, and a long iron groan, and the stone door swung out on its pins, and Jessop Kemble was standing in it with a lamp in one hand. Oda was behind him in the cellar with her sleeves rolled up.

The smell came out past them. Ulla knew it before she knew anything else. Fresh cheese. Herbed farm cheese, newly pressed, the kind you make a week before you need it and turn every day.

Jessop looked at the round, and the lanterns, and at Ulla, and he didn't seem to know what to do with his face.

"We sold the fields," he said. "We didn't know if we'd sold this too."

Nobody answered him. Ulla could feel everyone behind her wanting to and not knowing how.

"That cheese is ready," she said. "Are you going to make us stand out here?"

---

They had supper in the Kemble cellar, because there was always supper at the last door, and because once Oda had started bringing things out there was no stopping her. There was the herbed cheese, and grain baked in honey and dried in a cooling oven, and a crock of pickled beans, and bread, and the Kembles' cider, which was famous, and which everybody had been privately worried they wouldn't get to drink this year.

The cellar was too small for thirty people, and thirty people got into it anyway, the way they always did. Somebody sat on the potato bin. The children sat on the steps. Old Pettigrew's grandsons had got him down the steps in his chair somehow, and he was holding court by the cider barrel.

Ulla stood in the doorway.

After a while Yorick came out and stood next to her. He had a cup in one hand and a piece of bread with cheese on it in the other, and he looked like he'd been crying a bit, or nearly, and was pretending he'd just got cold again.

"You're not going to sign," he said.

"No."

"Can I ask why?"

She thought about it. She thought about telling him about the Kemble nobody could name, and the seventh year, and the whole Bend fitting inside, and she knew she'd get it wrong if she tried, because it wasn't the kind of thing you could tell somebody. You had to sit on a cold step and have it told to you.

"We'd have had to sing it here," she said.

Yorick looked into the cellar for a long time.

"It's still theft," he said eventually. "What they did. Up and down the river. I believe that."

"I know you do."

"I'm going to take it on. To the next village. Tomorrow."

"I know."

He nodded, and that seemed to be the end of what either of them had. Then one of the Fallon children came up the cellar steps behind them with a mouthful of bread and, seeing Yorick, said "Meow" very solemnly, and went back down.

Yorick laughed. It surprised him. He laughed till he had to put his cup down on the step, and wipe his eyes, and say "*Cats*," and laugh again.

In the morning somebody walked him to the end of the river road with a bag of honeyed grain and a heel of cheese, and he went on to the next village with his satchel and his papers, and the Bend's mark wasn't on any of them.

---

Five days later Ulla did the Kemble cellar on her warden's round, the same as every fortnight in winter.

She went down the steps with her lamp and did what she always did. She sniffed. She poked. She turned the cheeses on their shelf and pressed a thumb into each one. She went down the line of apple barrels with her nose, and the Kembles' apples were fine, every one, the way they always were. The roots were fine. The cider was better than fine.

"Your beans want eating," she said. "The second crock. This week."

"Yes, Ulla," Oda said.

Oda was sitting on an upturned barrel by the cider, with two cups. She'd filled them both. She didn't hold one out or say anything about it. She just sat there with it on the barrelhead next to her, as if it happened to be there.

Ulla looked at it. She looked at the steps, and the door at the top of them standing open on the cold white morning, and the next cellar on her round, which was the Wicks', and the onions she knew perfectly well they hadn't sorted.

She set her lamp on the shelf, sat down on the potato bin and picked up the cup.
