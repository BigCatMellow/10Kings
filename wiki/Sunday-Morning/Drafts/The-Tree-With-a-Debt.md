# The Tree With a Debt — draft

## Status

**L3 draft, first pass — provisional, non-canon.** Prose draft of [The Tree With a Debt](../Stories/The-Tree-With-a-Debt.md), written 2026-09-27 from that page's scene plan and promise ledger. Not yet independently reviewed (L4). Every character except Naruin Mossglade is provisional, as are the tree, the pledge and its wording, and the lien practice; see the [promotion rule](../Applying-to-Two-Sons.md#promotion-rule). Naruin stays offstage. Deepwood's animacy classes are rendered in English, following [Language and Thought](../../Culture/Language-and-Thought.md#deepwood-languages); no conlang words are invented.

The buyer's letter carries the connected-offer markers, matching the authoritative end in [Inspected, Not Guaranteed](Inspected-Not-Guaranteed.md): unsigned, heavy cream paper with a ship's-lantern watermark, and "should the market fail to recognize its worth" ([Anthology C6](../Anthology.md#cross-story-promise-ledger)).

**Drafting note, routed to the story page:** the clause mechanism is specified here as the story page implies but does not spell out. The pledge's text says only "for as long as it stands"; the old Highridge convention for such a standing pledge is that the debt, with its increase, is carried and cannot be called until the condition ends, which is when the tree falls. The pledge also carries the Highridge consent right: no works on pledged collateral without the holder's consent. So for sixty years the holder has had a reason to want the tree down and the only legal power to stop anyone felling it. Renewal as a guardianship keeps the power and waives the payout.

Story 6 of 7 in [The Anthology](../Anthology.md). Year 2, midsummer.

## The Tree With a Debt

The market master of Twilighthollow introduced them under the tree, nine days before the herb fair, and they disagreed before he had finished saying their names.

"Warden Sessa Alderwater," said the market master, "Surveyor Ismet Carrow. The mule path from the upper market to the herb yards. It goes around the tree. You'll do it together. The fair opens in nine days, and the herb carts have to come down something that isn't a goat track."

"Around it how far?" said Sessa.

"Around Lot Fourteen," said Ismet, at the same time, and consulted a folded plan. "Which is, I should say as a footnote, the plot designation in the district survey. The plan shows the path passing at a distance of twelve paces from the trunk, which is the standard allowance for—"

"It isn't a lot," said Sessa.

Ismet looked up from the plan. "I'm sorry?"

"It isn't a lot. It's—" She said a word in her own speech, and then, seeing his face, put it into the trade tongue as best she could. "It's her. It's someone. It isn't a piece of ground."

The tree stood at the head of the square between the Deepwood half of Twilighthollow and the Highridge half, where the forest came down off the uplands and met the first bare shoulder of the plateau. It was older than the town. It was older than the town's oldest argument. Its trunk was wider than a cart was long, and its bark was carved in places with marks from several centuries: tally marks, a lovers' knot, a boundary sign in a script nobody read anymore, and near the base, very faint, the Highridge figure *14*.

In Sessa's language, rivers and old trees and some paths belonged to the same grammatical class as people and animals. They were things that acted, participants in whatever was happening, not the ground it happened on. It did not make Deepwood people mystics. Sessa had grown up in the Highridge half of town and gone to the Deepwood school, and had never once in her life felt mystical about anything. It only meant that when she talked about the tree, the grammar made her say what the tree was doing.

In Ismet's records, the tree was not doing anything. It was Lot Fourteen.

"Then we'll need to settle," said Ismet carefully, "what exactly is being gone around."

"And I'll tell you now," said Sessa, "I'm not supposed to be building paths at all this summer." She crossed her arms. "My senior warden's orders. Naruin Mossglade. No new paths. Every warden in the district is to stand in front of any new path that's staked and not move."

The market master made a pained noise.

"Then why," said Ismet, "are you here?"

"Because the herb carts have to come down something," said Sessa, "and I'd rather it was a path I'd walked than one you'd drawn."

Ismet looked at her for a long moment, and then down at his plan, and then made a note in the margin.

"What did you write?" said Sessa.

"A footnote," said Ismet.

* * *

They staked the line the next day, and the mule refused it.

His name was Contract. He belonged to the mule yard at the bottom of the Highridge half, where he had carried herb panniers between the market and the drying sheds for eleven years, and he had been named, the yard master explained with some pride, because he never did anything he hadn't agreed to. Ismet walked him along the staked line with a loaded pannier to test the grade. Contract went three paces, stopped, looked at the ground, and walked calmly off the line to the left.

Ismet led him back. Contract went three paces, stopped, and walked calmly off the line to the left.

"He does that," said the yard master.

"Does what?"

"Doesn't agree."

They tried from the other end. They tried with a lighter pannier. They tried with an apple. Contract ate the apple and walked off the line to the left.

Sessa sat on a root and watched all of this with an expression Ismet was beginning to recognize, which was the expression of a person keeping several opinions to herself at once.

"It's a very old tree," Ismet said at last, sitting down beside her, because he could think of nothing else to say to a warden while a mule ignored him. "I'd put it at four hundred years. Perhaps five."

"It's old," Sessa agreed. And then, because he had said *old* the way plateau people said it — as one flat word for everything with too many years in it — she told him that she had four.

There was old like a stone, which had never been young and never would be anything else. There was old like a grandmother, which was old and tired and was owed a seat by the fire. There was old like a stump that was still sending up shoots, old and not finished yet. And there was a fourth kind, which she did not translate. She only said it, and put her hand flat on the root she was sitting on, and Ismet understood that it was the word for this.

"What does it mean?" he said.

"It means old," said Sessa, "and still deciding things."

Ismet wrote it down. He did not say it was a footnote this time.

* * *

The Twilighthollow records office occupied two rooms above the herb weighhouse, and it held the Highridge half's deeds and contracts and registers in the precise, cross-referenced order that Highridge clerks had kept for longer than there had been a town. Ismet went there on the third day to check the district survey against the town plan, and to find out, for his own satisfaction, why the tree was a lot.

He found the lien register on the bottom shelf of the back room, under a quarter-inch of dust.

It was a Highridge credit register of the old form, in which a lender recorded what he had lent, to whom, and what had been pledged against it. The entry was sixty years old. The town of Twilighthollow, it said, had borrowed a sum from the house of Varne, a plateau lending family, to rebuild the herb weighhouse after a fire. As security it had pledged *the great tree at the head of the square, recorded in the district survey as Lot 14.*

Ismet read it twice. Then he read the section of Highridge practice that the entry cited in its margin, which he knew by heart and read anyway. No works might be undertaken on pledged collateral without the consent of the holder of the pledge.

The tree was pledged. The path would go within twelve paces of its roots. It was works.

"There was a loan," he told Sessa and the market master that afternoon, in the square, under the tree. "Sixty years ago. The town pledged her." He caught himself. "It. Lot Fourteen. As security. And it was never repaid."

"Never?" said the market master.

"There's no discharge in the register. None." Ismet folded his notes. "Under Highridge practice, which governs the pledge, I can't sign off on a path this close to pledged collateral without the holder's consent. The holder is the house of Varne. I'll have to write to them."

"The fair's in seven days," said the market master.

"I'm aware of that," said Ismet. "I'll write today."

Sessa said nothing at all. She was looking at the tree. After a while she put her hand on its bark, near the faint carved *14*, the way you might put a hand on the shoulder of a friend who had just been told something unwelcome about herself.

* * *

Canopy Vigil fell on the sixth day before the fair, and on Canopy Vigil, in the Deepwood half of Twilighthollow, nobody worked.

It was one of the oldest customs in the district. For one day at midsummer, under the fullest canopy of the year, the Deepwood households put down their tools and went out to sit quietly in the forest, or beside a stream, or under a particular tree, and watched. They did not pray, exactly, or not all of them. They watched what was there and what it was doing, and then in the evening they compared notes. Some families treated it as holy and some as habit, and some simply liked having a day off.

It fell on exactly the same day as the opening of the Midsummer Debates in the Highridge half, which were the opposite in every respect.

Sessa and Ismet were obliged to spend the vigil together, under the tree. That was the market master's doing. The path could not be surveyed during the vigil, but the work was not allowed to lose a day, so he ruled, in a burst of administrative creativity nobody ever forgave him for, that the surveyor and the warden would *observe the site jointly* while the vigil lasted. That meant sitting side by side on the roots from dawn to dusk while the whole Deepwood half of town sat in silence around them, and the whole Highridge half of town argued at full volume across the square.

The debate hall's doors stood open for the heat. Every word came out of them.

"—and I put it to the honored speaker that the conditional in her third proposition *cannot bear the weight* she has placed on it—"

Ismet winced. Sessa did not move. She sat on the root with her hands on her knees and watched the tree.

After a while, because there was nothing else to do and because it would have been rude not to, he watched it too.

It was a long day. The light moved. Birds came and went in the canopy. Beetles worked along a seam in the bark. At midmorning a child's bare foot appeared on a branch above them, followed by the rest of a child, a small brown girl of about eight who climbed down through the tree as if it were a staircase in her own house, nodded to Sessa, ignored Ismet, and settled on a low limb with her chin on her knees.

"That's Pip," said Sessa, very quietly, because it was the vigil. "She's up here every day."

Pip pointed down at the ground. She didn't speak either; she was a Deepwood child and knew the rules. She pointed at a place a few paces to the left of Ismet's staked line where the earth humped up in a long low ridge under the grass. Then another. Then another, further out. Then she drew a line in the air with her finger, following the ridges round.

Ismet looked. He had walked that ground three times with a chain and a staff and had never once seen it. The roots came up there. They ran out under the grass far beyond the canopy, well past his twelve paces, and they were thick enough and shallow enough that a loaded mule would feel them through its hooves at every step.

He looked at Pip's finger, and at the humped ground, and at the mule yard at the bottom of the square, and thought of Contract walking calmly off the line to the left, every time, onto the smooth ground just beyond the root edge.

"—I will not be *lectured on obligation* by a man who cannot *distinguish a promise from a hope*—" said the debate hall.

In the afternoon, without looking at him, Sessa began to talk. She talked very quietly, because of the vigil, and because it was easier to say things to a tree than to a surveyor.

She told him about Naruin Mossglade. He was not a fool, and he was not paranoid. Plans had come into the district that spring, passed hand to hand among the wardens. They were real plans for a real road, wide enough for timber wagons, surveyed by people nobody knew, and they ran straight through Twilighthollow and up into the forest. Nobody had been told why, or by whom. Naruin had grown up on his grandmother's stories of the old timber tithes, when outsiders had taken the forest by the wagonload under agreements the Deepwood villages had been in no position to refuse. When the plans came, he had read them the way his grandmother would have read them. So every path was the first stake of the road, and every warden was to stand in front of every stake.

"He isn't wrong," said Sessa. "The road's real. I've seen the plans. Somebody wants it."

"And this path?"

"This path is a mule path to the herb yard." She looked at Pip on her branch. "I think. I thought. And then you told me she's pledged to a plateau bank." She was silent for a while. "I don't know what anyone wants anymore."

Ismet did not answer. It was the vigil. He only sat beside her, watching the tree and the debate hall's open doors, and for the first time since he had come to Twilighthollow he did not feel the need to add anything.

* * *

Hollis Varne arrived on the day of the Midsummer Debates' closing session with a satchel of papers and a very good coat that had seen better years.

He came up from the plateau on the mail cart. The surveyor's letter had reached his family the same week as another letter, he said, and he had come, he told the market master within the first ten minutes, to find out what his family's pledge on Twilighthollow was worth. It was sixty years old, he understood, and it had never been repaid. He had done the arithmetic on the cart. Sixty years of interest on the original sum, compounded at the plateau rate, came to a figure he said aloud in the market square. The market master sat down on the edge of the fountain, and the herb sellers stopped selling.

The Weaver-path elder, who had been sitting under the market arcade documenting the Debates for the community record, as she documented everything, turned to a fresh page and began to write very fast.

It was not, Ismet thought afterward, that Hollis Varne was a hard man. He had helped the mail carter unload the cart before he said a word to anyone, and he had given Pip, who had come down from her tree to stare at him, a honey stick from his own pocket. He was a young man in an old coat, with a tired face, and when he said the figure he looked as though he were apologizing for it.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I know how it sounds. But my family lends to caravans. We've lent to caravans for a hundred years. And this last season—" he spread his hands. "The attacks. Half our loans went down with the loads. We're not ruined. But we're not far off it. And then this came."

He took a letter out of his satchel and held it up.

"Somebody wants to buy it," he said. "The pledge. Our old pledge on your tree. They've offered us a good price for it, better than good. More than I thought it could possibly be worth. And every other family on the plateau with an old pledge along the road line has already had the same offer, and taken it. All my neighbors. I'm the last." He looked at the tree. "So I came to see what it was worth. Before I sold it."

Ismet held out his hand for the letter. Hollis gave it to him.

It was heavy paper, cream-colored and very fine, far finer than anything a plateau lending house would use. It was unsigned. It said that the undersigned would purchase the Varne family's pledge on Twilighthollow for a sum that made Ismet blink, *should the market fail to recognize its worth*, and that a reply left with the mail carrier would find its way. When Ismet held it up to the light he could see a watermark in the grain: a small ship's lantern, the kind that hung from a stern rail.

"Port paper," he said, to no one in particular. "One of the harbor stationers uses that mark. Half the counting houses in Port write on it." It was the kind of thing he knew. He gave the letter back and thought nothing more of it.

The town, meanwhile, was panicking. The Weaver-path elder had filled two pages. The market master had put his head in his hands. Somebody in the Highridge half had already proposed a debate on the question, and somebody in the Deepwood half had said that if the plateau wanted the tree they could come and carry it off themselves.

Sessa stood in the middle of it, very still, looking at Hollis Varne. He was looking at the tree.

* * *

That night, when the Debates had ended and the town had gone to bed arguing, Ismet went back to the records office with a lamp and read the pledge again. He read it word by word, in the old Highridge trade register it had been written in, the one his grandfather had used and nobody in his own guild used anymore. He read every conditional and every obligation marker, as he had been trained to do and had not done the first time, because the first time he had been reading for the rule and not the words.

At the same hour, Sessa went back to the tree. She sat on the root where she had sat all through the vigil, with her hand on the bark, and thought about what a tree that was pledged would have to be doing, grammatically, in her language, for the pledge to be true.

They met at the tree a little after midnight. Neither was surprised to see the other.

"Read it to me," said Sessa. "The pledge. The words. Not what it means. What it says."

Ismet held the lamp up to his notes and read it to her. He read it in the old trade register, and then, slowly, in the ordinary trade tongue.

*The town of Twilighthollow pledges to the house of Varne, against the sum lent, the great tree at the head of the square, for as long as it stands.*

Sessa said, "Again. The end."

"*For as long as it stands.*"

"That's what I thought you said." She took her hand off the bark. "Ismet. In my language that's not a length of time. It's something she does. *Standing.* That's her verb. The pledge is only true while she's doing it." She turned to him. "So what happens to the debt while she's doing it?"

Ismet looked at the notes in his hand. He had read *for as long as it stands* three times in the records office and heard a term clause, a duration: how long the pledge was good for. In the old trade register it was that. It was also, he now saw, a condition — and all conditions had a subject. And there was an old plateau convention for a pledge that stood on a condition, one his grandfather's generation had used and his own had forgotten. The debt was carried, not called. It could not be demanded while the condition held. It fell due when the condition ended.

"Nothing," he said slowly. "Nothing happens to it. It can't be called while she stands. Not the interest. Not the principal. Nothing, for sixty years. It comes due the day she falls. It's all waiting on the tree."

"And that's what they're buying," said Sessa.

He did not understand at first. Then he did.

Whoever held the pledge was owed nothing while the tree stood, and sixty years of compounded interest the day it fell. The road would fell it. A road for timber wagons, straight through the square, would take the tree at the head of it on the first morning. And whoever held the pledge had the consent right. They could agree to the works that took it down, and be paid for agreeing.

"The buyer," said Ismet, "isn't buying a debt. He's buying the day she falls."

"And the Varnes," said Sessa. "For sixty years—"

"For sixty years the Varnes have held the only legal power there is to stop anyone felling her." Ismet laughed, once, very quietly, in the dark. "Nobody could touch this tree without their consent. Not the town, not the district, not a road. A plateau lending house has held the only power to stop anyone felling a Deepwood tree for sixty years, with every reason to let it fall, and nobody on either side knew."

Sessa looked up into the canopy, black against the stars.

"The man who lent the money thought she'd fall first," she said. "Didn't he? He looked at a tree that old and thought, she'll come down in a storm in a year or two, and I'll have my money with interest."

"I expect so."

"He was wrong." She said the fourth word for old, the one she hadn't translated, and this time Ismet did not need her to. Old and still deciding things.

* * *

They told Hollis Varne in the records office in the morning, two days before the fair. Ismet read the pledge aloud in the old register and then in the new. Sessa said what it meant in her language, and the Weaver-path elder wrote everything down.

Hollis listened to all of it without speaking. When they had finished, he took the buyer's letter out of his satchel and looked at it for a long time.

"If I sell this," he said, "whoever buys it wants the road."

"Whoever buys it," said Ismet, "profits when the tree comes down. And has the right to say yes to whatever brings it down."

"My neighbors sold theirs." Hollis turned the letter over. "All along the road line. Every old plateau pledge on every stand of forest from here to the uplands. I thought they were lucky." He was quiet. "I suppose the road's coming anyway."

"Perhaps," said Sessa. "But not through her."

Hollis Varne tore the letter in half, and then in half again, and put the pieces in the records office stove. The Weaver-path elder wrote that down too.

The town settled the rest by lunchtime. The Highridge half proposed, in the manner of the Midsummer Debates, that the original sum be repaid, the small principal only, sixty years late, with no increase. The Deepwood half proposed that it be repaid as a gift, not a debt, because nothing had ever been owed. Everyone agreed on the amount, which was small, and argued about the word, which was large, until the market master made them write both words in the register side by side. Hollis accepted the money. He looked, when he took it, like a man who had expected to be rich and was surprised to find himself relieved instead.

Then he did the thing nobody had asked him to do. He renewed the pledge. It was rewritten in both registers, the old trade tongue and Sessa's, as a guardianship. The house of Varne would hold the tree for as long as it stood. The increase was waived; the debt was discharged. The consent right was kept. No works could be done upon the tree, or within its roots, without the agreement of the house of Varne and the wardens of Twilighthollow together.

Sessa read it through twice, in her own language. The tree was the subject of every sentence.

The path was staked that afternoon. It did not run twelve paces from the trunk. It ran along the edge of the root zone that Pip had been climbing over for years, where the humped ridges under the grass gave out onto smooth ground. When Ismet led Contract along it with a loaded pannier, the mule walked the whole length from the upper market to the herb yards without stopping once.

"He agrees," said the yard master, deeply satisfied.

* * *

The herb fair opened on time.

The carts came down the new path at dawn, drawn by mules that did not stop or balk, loaded with dried leaf and root and bark and resin and flower from both halves of the district and beyond. The herb yards filled. The sellers argued with the buyers about quality and the buyers argued with each other about price, and the whole town smelled of mint and smoke and something bitter from the uplands that the Deepwood sellers claimed cured fevers and the Highridge buyers claimed cured nothing but money.

The tree stood at the head of the square with a new board nailed at its foot. The board gave its name twice: once in Highridge figures, *Lot 14, held in guardianship*, and once in Sessa's language, in the class of participants, with the verb for standing.

The survey was filed that morning at the records office, co-signed by the warden and the surveyor, and written in a register that was not quite either of theirs. The Deepwood words for ground and root and water sat inside the Highridge form for obligations and conditions. Neither of their guilds would have accepted it on its own. Together, the clerk said, there was nothing in it he could object to.

It had one footnote. It was at the bottom of the second page, under the route description, in Ismet's small precise hand:

*¹ The route follows the root edge as identified by Warden S. Alderwater from ground evidence, with assistance from a resident (Pip) and a mule (Contract). The surveyor concurs in every particular.*

Sessa read it at the records office counter. She read it twice. Then she looked at Ismet, who was pretending to check a column of figures.

"*Concurs in every particular*," she said.

"It's a standard form," said Ismet.

"It is not."

"No," Ismet agreed, and went on checking figures, and after a moment she took the pen and initialed the footnote beside his, which was not a standard form either.

She sent a fair copy of the survey to Naruin Mossglade that afternoon by the warden's post, up into the forest. She did not write a covering note. She thought it said everything she could have said, and in both languages, which was more than she could manage.

By the end of the first day of the fair, Hollis Varne had made up his mind not to go back to the plateau. He had the principal, and nobody to lend it to, and a family that could manage without him for a season. And there was a very good spot, he pointed out, at the bend of the new path, just past the root edge. Every herb seller and buyer in the district would walk past it twice a day, hot and thirsty and arguing.

"A tea stall," he said to the market master.

The market master looked at the spot, which was on the Deepwood side of the square, within sight of the Highridge half, on a path that had taken two guilds, a mule, a child and a sixty-year-old debt to agree on.

"You'll need a permit," he said, wearily. "From both halves."

"Of course," said Hollis.

"They'll debate it."

"I'd expect nothing less," said Hollis, and went off to find the Weaver-path elder, who would want to write it down.
