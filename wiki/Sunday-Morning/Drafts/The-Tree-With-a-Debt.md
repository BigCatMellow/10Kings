## The Tree With a Debt

"Around Lot Fourteen," said the surveyor.

"It isn't a lot," said the warden.

The market master hadn't finished introducing them yet.

"Warden Sessa Alderwater," he went on, a little louder. "Surveyor Ismet Carrow. The mule path from the upper market to the herb yards. It goes around the tree. You'll do it together. The herb fair opens in nine days, and the carts need to come down something that isn't a goat track."

Ismet unfolded a plan. "Which is, I should say as a footnote, the plot designation in the district survey. The plan puts the path twelve paces from the trunk, which is the standard allowance for—"

"It isn't a lot," Sessa said again.

"I'm sorry?"

She said a word in her own speech. Saw his face. Tried again in trade tongue.

"It's *her*. It's someone. It's not a piece of ground."

The tree stood at the head of the square, right between Twilighthollow's two halves: Deepwood on one side, Highridge on the other. Forest coming down off the uplands, meeting the first bare shoulder of the plateau. The tree was older than the town. Older than the town's oldest argument. Its trunk was wider than a cart was long. The bark was carved with centuries of marks. Tallies. A lovers' knot. A boundary sign in a script nobody could read anymore.

And near the base, very faint, the Highridge figure *14*.

In Sessa's grammar, old trees went in the same class as people and animals and some rivers. Things that *did* things. Not the ground that things happened on.

It didn't make her mystical. She'd grown up on the Highridge side of town and gone to the Deepwood school, and she had never once in her life felt mystical about anything.

It just meant that when she talked about the tree, the grammar made her say what the tree was doing.

In Ismet's paperwork, the tree wasn't doing anything.

It was Lot Fourteen.

"Then we'll need to settle," Ismet said carefully, "exactly what's being gone around."

"And I'll tell you now." Sessa crossed her arms. "I'm not supposed to be building paths at all this summer. My senior warden's orders. Naruin Mossglade. No new paths. Every warden in the district stands in front of any new stake and doesn't move."

The market master made a pained noise.

"Then why," said Ismet, "are you here?"

"Because the carts have to come down something." She looked at the tree. "And I'd rather it was a path I walked than one you drew."

Ismet looked at her. Then at his plan.

He made a note in the margin.

"What did you write?"

"A footnote."

---

The mule's name was Contract.

He'd carried herb panniers between the market and the drying sheds for eleven years. He'd been named, the yard master said proudly, because he never did anything he hadn't agreed to.

Ismet walked him along the staked line with a loaded pannier.

Contract went three paces. Stopped. Looked at the ground.

Walked calmly off the line. To the left.

Ismet led him back.

Three paces. Stop. Look.

Left.

"He does that," said the yard master.

"Does what?"

"Doesn't agree."

They tried from the other end. A lighter pannier. An apple.

Contract ate the apple.

And walked off to the left.

Sessa sat on a root through all of it with an expression Ismet was starting to recognize. The face of someone keeping several opinions to herself at once.

He sat down beside her, because there was nothing else to say to a warden while a mule ignored you.

"Very old tree," he said. "Four hundred years? Five?"

"It's old."

He'd said *old* the way plateau people did. One flat word for anything with too many years in it.

So she told him she had four.

Old like a stone, which had never been young and never would be anything else.

Old like a grandmother. Tired. Owed a seat by the fire.

Old like a stump still sending up shoots. Not finished yet.

And a fourth one. She didn't translate that one. She just said it, and put her hand flat on the root she was sitting on.

He understood it was the word for this.

"What does it mean?"

"Old," said Sessa. "And still deciding things."

Ismet wrote it down.

He didn't call it a footnote.

---

The lien register was on the bottom shelf of the records office's back room, under a quarter-inch of dust.

Sixty years old. A Highridge credit entry in the old form. The town of Twilighthollow had borrowed from the house of Varne, a plateau lending family, to rebuild the herb weighhouse after a fire.

And as security, it had pledged:

*The great tree at the head of the square, recorded in the district survey as Lot 14.*

Ismet read it twice.

Then the practice cited in its margin. He knew it by heart. He read it anyway.

*No works may be undertaken on pledged collateral without the consent of the holder of the pledge.*

---

"There was a loan," he told them that afternoon. Under the tree. "Sixty years ago. The town pledged her." He caught himself. "*It.* Lot Fourteen. As security." He folded his notes. "And there's no discharge in the register. It was never repaid."

"Never?" said the market master.

"Under Highridge practice, which governs the pledge, I can't sign off on a path this close to pledged collateral without the holder's consent. The holder is the house of Varne. I'll have to write to them."

"The fair's in seven days."

"I'm aware. I'll write today."

Sessa didn't say anything.

She put her hand on the bark, near the faint carved *14*. The way you'd put a hand on a friend's shoulder after somebody had just told her something unwelcome about herself.

---

On Canopy Vigil, the Deepwood half of Twilighthollow didn't work.

One day at midsummer, under the fullest canopy of the year. Tools down. Out to sit quietly in the forest, or by a stream, or under one particular tree. And watch. What was there. What it was doing. Compare notes in the evening.

Some families called it holy. Some called it habit. Some just liked a day off.

It fell on exactly the same day the Midsummer Debates opened in the Highridge half.

Which were the opposite in every way.

Sessa and Ismet spent the vigil together, under the tree. The market master's idea. No surveying during the vigil, but the job couldn't lose a day. So, in a burst of administrative creativity nobody ever forgave him for, he ruled that the surveyor and the warden would *jointly observe the site*.

Side by side on the roots. Dawn to dusk. The whole Deepwood half of town silent around them.

And the whole Highridge half, across the square, arguing at full volume.

The debate hall had its doors open for the heat.

Every word came out.

"—and I put it to the honored speaker that the conditional in her third proposition *cannot bear the weight* she has placed upon it—"

Ismet winced.

Sessa didn't move. Hands on her knees. Watching the tree.

After a while, because there was nothing else to do, he watched it too.

The light moved. Birds came and went. Beetles worked a seam in the bark. The air smelled of hot leaves and old wood and, very faintly, the herb yards downhill.

Mid-morning, a bare foot appeared on a branch above them.

Then the rest of a child.

Small, brown, about eight. She came down through the tree like it was the staircase in her own house, nodded to Sessa, ignored Ismet completely, and settled on a low limb with her chin on her knees.

"Pip," Sessa murmured. "She's up here every day."

Pip didn't speak either. Deepwood kid. She knew the rules.

She pointed.

A spot a few paces left of Ismet's staked line, where the earth humped up in a long low ridge under the grass.

Another ridge.

Another, further out.

She drew a line in the air with one finger, following them round.

Ismet looked.

He'd walked that ground three times with a chain and a staff. He'd never once seen it. The roots came up there. They ran out under the grass way past the canopy, way past his twelve paces, thick enough and shallow enough that a loaded mule would feel them through its hooves at every step.

He looked at the ridges. At Pip's finger.

At the mule yard at the bottom of the square.

Contract, walking calmly off the line. To the left. Every time. Onto the smooth ground just past the edge of the roots.

"—I will not be *lectured on obligation* by a man who cannot *tell a promise from a hope*—" said the debate hall.

---

In the afternoon, Sessa started talking.

Quietly, because of the vigil. And because it was easier to say things to a tree than to a surveyor.

She told him about Naruin Mossglade.

"He's not a fool," she said. "And he's not paranoid. Plans came through the district this spring. Wardens passing them hand to hand. A real road, wide enough for timber wagons, straight through here and up into the forest. Surveyed by people nobody knows. Nobody's said why."

A beetle crossed her boot. She let it.

"His grandmother used to tell him about the timber tithes. Outsiders taking the forest by the wagonload, under agreements the villages couldn't refuse. So when the plans came, he read them the way she would have. Every path is the first stake of the road." She shrugged. "So every warden stands in front of every stake."

She looked at the tree.

"And he isn't wrong. I've seen those plans. Somebody wants that road."

"And this path?"

"This path is a mule path to the herb yards." She looked up at Pip. "I think. I thought." A long pause. "And then you tell me she's pledged to a plateau bank."

Another pause.

"I don't know what anybody wants anymore."

Ismet didn't answer. It was the vigil.

He just sat next to her. Watching the tree. Listening to the debate hall.

For the first time since he came to Twilighthollow, he didn't feel the need to add anything.

---

Hollis Varne arrived on the last day of the Debates. Satchel of papers. A very good coat that had seen better years.

He came up from the plateau on the mail cart. Before he said a word to anyone, he helped the carter unload it. He gave Pip, who'd come down from her tree to stare at him, a honey stick out of his own pocket.

After that he found the market master.

"Your surveyor's letter reached my family," he said. "Same week as another one."

He'd come to find out what the pledge was worth. Sixty years old. Never repaid. He'd done the arithmetic on the cart: sixty years of interest on the original sum, compounded at the plateau rate.

He said the figure out loud in the market square.

The market master sat down on the edge of the fountain.

The herb sellers stopped selling.

The Weaver-path elder had been under the market arcade all week, writing up the Debates for the community record. She wrote everything down. Everything. Including things nobody had asked her to.

She turned to a fresh page and started writing very fast.

"I'm sorry," Hollis said. He looked like he meant it. A young man in an old coat with a tired face. "I know how it sounds. But my family lends to caravans. A hundred years we've lent to caravans. And this last season—" He spread his hands. "The attacks. Half our loans went down with the loads. We're not ruined. We're not far off."

He took a letter out of his satchel.

"Then this came. Somebody wants to buy it. The pledge. Our old pledge on your tree. They're offering a good price. Better than good. More than I thought it could possibly be worth." He looked around the square. "Every other plateau family with an old pledge along the road line got the same letter. My neighbors. All of them. They all sold." He looked at the tree. "I'm the last. So I came to see what it was worth. Before I sell."

Ismet held out his hand.

Hollis gave him the letter.

Heavy paper. Cream-colored. Very fine. Far finer than any plateau lending house would use. Unsigned.

The undersigned would purchase the Varne family's pledge on Twilighthollow for a sum that made Ismet blink, *should the market fail to recognize its worth*. A reply left with the mail carrier would find its way.

Ismet held it up to the light.

A watermark in the grain. A small ship's lantern, the kind that hangs from a stern rail.

"Port paper," he said, to nobody in particular. "One of the harbor stationers uses that mark. Half the counting houses in Port write on it."

It was the kind of thing he knew.

He gave it back and thought nothing more of it.

The town, meanwhile, was panicking. The Weaver-path elder had filled two pages. The market master had his head in his hands. Somebody on the Highridge side had already proposed a debate on the question. Somebody on the Deepwood side had said that if the plateau wanted the tree, they could come and carry it off themselves.

Sessa stood in the middle of it. Very still.

Looking at Hollis Varne.

Who was looking at the tree.

---

That night Ismet went back to the records office with a lamp.

He read the pledge again. Word by word. In the old Highridge trade register it was written in, the one his grandfather used and nobody in his guild used anymore. Every conditional. Every obligation marker. The way he'd been trained. The way he hadn't done the first time, because the first time he'd been reading for the rule, not the words.

At the same hour, Sessa went back to the tree. She sat on her root with her hand on the bark and thought about what a tree would have to be *doing*, grammatically, for a pledge on her to be true.

They met under the tree a little after midnight.

Neither of them was surprised.

"Read it to me," said Sessa. "The pledge. Not what it means. What it *says*."

He held the lamp to his notes. Read it in the old register. Then, slowly, in trade tongue.

*The town of Twilighthollow pledges to the house of Varne, against the sum lent, the great tree at the head of the square, for as long as it stands.*

"Again," said Sessa. "The end."

"*For as long as it stands.*"

"That's what I thought you said." She took her hand off the bark. "Ismet. In my language that's not a length of time. It's something she *does*. Standing. That's her verb. The pledge is only true while she's doing it."

She turned to him.

"So what happens to the debt while she's doing it?"

Ismet looked at his notes.

He'd read *for as long as it stands* three times in the records office and heard a term. A duration. How long the pledge was good for. In the old register it was that.

It was also a condition.

And all conditions have a subject.

And there was an old plateau convention for a pledge that stood on a condition. His grandfather's generation had used it. His own had forgotten. The debt was carried, not called. It couldn't be demanded while the condition held.

It fell due when the condition ended.

"Nothing," he said slowly. "Nothing happens to it. It can't be called while she stands. Not the interest. Not the principal. Nothing. For sixty years."

He looked up.

"It comes due the day she falls. It's all waiting on the tree."

"And that," said Sessa, "is what they're buying."

It took him a second.

Then it didn't.

Whoever held the pledge was owed nothing while the tree stood. And sixty years of compound interest the day it fell. The road would fell it. A road for timber wagons, straight through the square, would take the tree at its head on the first morning.

And whoever held the pledge had the consent right. They could say yes to the works that took it down.

And get paid for saying it.

"The buyer," Ismet said, "isn't buying a debt. He's buying the day she falls."

"And the Varnes. For sixty years—"

"For sixty years the Varnes have held the only legal power there is to stop anyone felling her." He laughed. Once. Very quietly, in the dark. "Nobody could touch this tree without their consent. Not the town. Not the district. Not a road. A plateau lending house has held the only power to stop anyone felling a Deepwood tree for sixty years, with every reason to let it fall."

He shook his head.

"And nobody on either side knew."

Sessa looked up into the canopy. Black against the stars.

"The man who lent the money," she said. "He thought she'd fall first. Didn't he? Looked at a tree that old and thought, she'll come down in a storm in a year or two, and I'll have my money with interest."

"I expect so."

"He was wrong."

She said the fourth word for old. The one she hadn't translated.

This time he didn't need her to.

---

They told Hollis in the records office in the morning. Two days before the fair.

Ismet read the pledge in the old register, then the new. Sessa said what it meant in hers. The Weaver-path elder wrote down every word.

Hollis listened to all of it without speaking.

He took the buyer's letter out of his satchel and looked at it for a long time.

"If I sell this, whoever buys it wants the road."

"Whoever buys it profits when the tree comes down," Ismet said. "And holds the right to say yes to whatever brings it down."

"My neighbors sold theirs." Hollis turned the letter over. "All along the line. Every old plateau pledge on every stand of forest from here to the uplands. I thought they were lucky." Quiet. "I suppose the road's coming anyway."

"Maybe," said Sessa.

A pause.

"But not through her."

Hollis tore the letter in half.

Then in half again.

He put the pieces in the records office stove.

The Weaver-path elder wrote that down too.

---

The town settled the rest by lunch.

The Highridge side proposed, in proper Debates form, that the original sum be repaid. The small principal only. Sixty years late. No interest. The Deepwood side proposed it be repaid as a gift, not a debt, since nothing had ever been owed.

Everyone agreed on the amount, which was small.

They argued about the word, which was large.

Until the market master made them write both words into the register side by side.

Hollis took the money. He looked like a man who'd expected to be rich and was surprised to find he was relieved instead.

Then he did something nobody had asked him to.

He renewed the pledge.

Rewritten, in both registers, the old trade tongue and Sessa's, as a guardianship. The house of Varne would hold the tree for as long as it stood. The interest waived. The debt discharged. The consent right kept. No works on the tree or within its roots without the agreement of the house of Varne *and* the wardens of Twilighthollow. Together.

Sessa read it through twice in her own language.

The tree was the subject of every sentence.

The path was staked that afternoon. Not twelve paces from the trunk. Along the edge of the root zone Pip had been climbing over for years, where the humped ridges under the grass gave out onto smooth ground.

Ismet led Contract along it with a loaded pannier.

The mule walked the whole length, upper market to herb yards, without stopping once.

"He agrees," said the yard master, deeply satisfied.

---

The herb fair opened on time.

The carts came down the new path at dawn. Mules that didn't balk. Panniers full of leaf and root and bark and resin and flower, from both halves of the district and beyond. The herb yards filled. Sellers argued with buyers about quality. Buyers argued with each other about price. The whole town smelled of mint and smoke and something bitter from the uplands that the Deepwood sellers swore cured fevers and the Highridge buyers swore cured nothing but money.

A new board at the foot of the tree gave its name twice.

In Highridge figures: *Lot 14, held in guardianship.*

In Sessa's language, in the class of participants, with the verb for standing.

The survey was filed that morning. Co-signed, warden and surveyor, in a register that was neither of theirs. Deepwood words for ground and root and water, inside the Highridge form for obligations and conditions. Neither guild would have accepted it alone. Together, the clerk said, there wasn't a thing in it he could object to.

It had exactly one footnote.

Bottom of the second page, under the route description, in Ismet's small precise hand:

*¹ The route follows the root edge as identified by Warden S. Alderwater from ground evidence, with assistance from a resident (Pip) and a mule (Contract). The surveyor concurs in every particular.*

Sessa read it at the counter.

Twice.

Then she looked at Ismet, who was very busy pretending to check a column of figures.

"*Concurs in every particular.*"

"It's a standard form."

"It is not."

"No," Ismet agreed.

He kept checking his figures.

After a moment, she took the pen and initialed the footnote next to his.

That wasn't a standard form either.

She sent a fair copy of the survey up into the forest that afternoon, by the warden's post, to Naruin Mossglade. No covering note. She figured it said everything she could have said, in both languages, which was more than she could have managed herself.

---

By the end of the fair's first day, Hollis Varne had decided not to go back to the plateau.

He had the principal. Nobody to lend it to. A family that could manage without him for a season.

And there was a very good spot, he pointed out, right at the bend of the new path. Just past the root edge. Every herb seller and buyer in the district would walk past it twice a day, hot, thirsty and arguing.

"A tea stall," he told the market master.

The market master looked at the spot. Deepwood side. In sight of the Highridge half. On a path that had taken two guilds, a mule, a child and a sixty-year-old debt to agree on.

"You'll need a permit," he said wearily. "From both halves."

"Of course."

"They'll debate it."

Hollis smiled.

"I'd expect nothing less."
