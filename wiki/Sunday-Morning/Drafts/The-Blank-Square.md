## The Blank Square

The river had come up in the night, which Ruan Mott had known it would, because it always did this time of year. He'd crossed it anyway, because the bundle was due.

The ford below Larchmere was a good ford. It had a gravel bottom and a line of old stakes across it to hold on to, and in summer you could walk it with your boots round your neck and not wet your knees. But it was autumn now, the rains had been in the hills for three days, and when Ruan came down to it at first light the water was brown and loud and up to the second notch on the marker stake, which meant thigh-deep and pulling.

He stood on the bank and looked at it for a while.

"Well," he told the river, "you're in a mood."

The bundle was on his back, in its oilcloth, strapped tight. It was Twilighthollow's year: the witness book the Weaver house at Twilighthollow kept, everything their people had seen with their own eyes since last Gathering Quiet, written down in their own hands or taken down from their own mouths by the keeper there. Every year the houses sent their books round to each other before Gathering Quiet, so each house could stitch its neighbors into the border of its cloth. Every year Ruan carried Twilighthollow's book to Larchmere. It was a day's walk and a river.

He'd never once got it wet.

He took a breath, and got hold of the first stake, and went in.

It was all right as far as the middle. It was cold enough to stop your heart and it pulled at his legs like a dog with a rope, but he'd crossed worse. He went stake to stake, the way you do, never letting go of one before he had the next. And then, at the middle stake, his foot went off a stone that hadn't been there in the summer, and he went down to the waist and then to the chest, and the river got hold of the bundle on his back and swung him half round.

He didn't let go of the stake. That was the main thing. He hung on to it with both arms and the water going over his shoulders, and got his feet back under him, and stood up. It took a while.

When he got to the far bank he sat down on it, streaming, and got the bundle off his back.

The oilcloth was dark all through. Water ran out of the corners.

He didn't open it. There was no point. He couldn't read a word of what was in it anyway. He sat with it on his knees in the cold, and looked at it, and then he got up and carried it on to Larchmere in his arms instead of on his back, as if carrying it differently now would make any difference.

---

Keeper Wilda unwrapped it on the big table in the stitching room, with the whole year-cloth on its frame behind her and the morning light coming in through the long windows.

She was a careful woman, Wilda. Careful hands, careful voice, careful about every single thing that went onto that cloth. She unwrapped the oilcloth one fold at a time and laid it back, and took out the book inside, and opened it.

The pages were brown. All of them. The ink had run into the paper in long soft stains, from edge to edge, page after page, so that what had been writing was now just a sort of weather. Here and there you could see where a line had been. That was all you could see.

She turned every page. Ruan stood by the door, still damp, and watched her do it, and every page she turned was the same.

"It's my fault," he said. "The ford. I went down at the middle stake."

"You're alive," said Wilda, without looking up. "I'd rather have you than the book."

"You'd rather have both."

"I would," she agreed. She turned the last page. It was as brown as the first. "But I've got you."

The year-cloth hung behind her on its frame. It was nearly done. Every Gathering Quiet the house stitched one, and every year it was different: a cloth of squares, a square for every thing somebody in the parish had seen with their own eyes that year and come and told the keeper. A birth. A flood. The big oak that came down in the spring storm. A fox that had walked into the Mabbs' kitchen and eaten a whole pie and walked out again. The first stork in ten years. And this year, a lot of squares about roads.

The middle of the cloth was done. The border was what was left: the squares from the neighbor houses, from their witness books, stitched round the edge so that Larchmere's year was held in by everybody else's. The north side was done, and the east, from the river houses. The west side was Twilighthollow's.

It was empty.

"Can't you stitch it from what's left?" Ruan said. "Some of it?"

Wilda looked at the brown pages. "Stitch what? A stain?"

"From what you remember? From last year's?"

"Last year's isn't this year's." She closed the book. "And I didn't see any of it, Ruan. Nobody here did. That's the rule. A witness's own account, or nothing. That's what the cloth is."

"I know the rule," said Ruan. "I'm only the carrier."

He said it the way he always said it, with a sort of grin, as if it was a joke. He'd said it all his life. *I'm only the carrier.* He couldn't read, he'd never learned, and in Larchmere everybody knew and nobody minded, and Ruan had decided a long time ago that it meant nothing he did would ever belong on that cloth. He carried other people's years. He'd never stitched a square of his own.

Wilda gave him a long look, and took the book over to the hearth to dry.

---

There were two guests in the house for Gathering Quiet that year, and at supper that first night they both had ideas.

The first was Gideon, a pilgrim of the Compass, who'd come in off the shrine roads three days before with his staff and his badge and his boots worn through, the way Compass pilgrims did. He was a big, warm, talkative man with a beard like a hedge, and he'd walked every road between here and the plateau, and he carried more news than a market.

"Twilighthollow!" he said, delighted, when he heard. "I was through Twilighthollow at midsummer. For the debates. I can tell you all about Twilighthollow."

And he did. There'd been a tree, he said, a great tree, in the square, that nearly came down. Or nearly got cut down. There'd been a plateau family mixed up in it, rich people, with a claim of some sort. There'd been a fight at the summer debates, a real one, people shouting, and somebody's mule had got loose in the middle of it. There'd been a wedding. Or nearly a wedding. He wasn't sure about the wedding.

"There," said Gideon, beaming. "You could stitch that. A tree, a fight, a wedding. That's a good border."

Ruan had the bottom of the table, and ate his bread. The Twilighthollow keeper had told him, when she handed him the bundle, what was in it. Not much. She'd been in a hurry. *The tree's in there, and the road, and the summer's debates*, she'd said. *Mind it at the ford.* Ruan didn't know what the tree was or what had happened to it. But he knew it wasn't a fight and a wedding and a mule.

"Was that what you saw?" said Wilda to Gideon. "With your own eyes?"

Gideon's face fell, a bit. "Well. I heard it. On the road. From people who were there." He brightened. "Mostly."

"Then it's yours to tell," said Wilda, "and you tell it very well. But it's not Twilighthollow's square."

The second guest was a copyist of the Illuminated Circle, a thin, polite man called Nils Orvay, with ink on his fingers and a little traveling desk that he carried everywhere and set up on every table he came to. He collected accounts that didn't agree with each other. That was what the Illuminated did, he'd explained to Ruan, the first evening: you went round and copied down every record you could find of the same thing, and then you laid them side by side and saw where they disagreed, and you learned a great deal from the disagreeing.

"It could be restored," Nils said, quite gently. "The Twilighthollow book. Not exactly. But I have other accounts of this year, from the plateau houses and the road clerks. I've copied a good many. If I laid them beside what's left of the pages, I could give you a reasonable reconstruction. I've done it before."

"From other people's accounts," said Wilda.

"From several. Carefully compared."

"But not from the people who were there."

Nils thought about that. He was a fair man, Ruan thought. He didn't pretend.

"No," he said. "From mine. From my comparing."

Wilda nodded. "Then that's yours too," she said. "And I'm sure it's very careful. But it's not a witness."

---

On the table by the door, where the house kept letters, there were two statements, and they'd been there a week.

One was from the forest wardens' circle, and it was against the plateau road: the new road the plateau wanted to cut through the forest from the Twilighthollow side, which everybody in Deepwood had been arguing about all year. It said the road would ruin the forest from end to end, and it asked every Weaver house in Deepwood to put its mark to it. It had a long row of marks on it already.

The other had come a much longer way. It had started in Greenvale, in the winter, from the reform circles there, and it had been passed from village to village and house to house all year, and it had got to Larchmere at last, a bit dog-eared, in the same week as the wardens'. It said the buying-up of farms all along the Greenvale rivers, after the bad harvest, was no accident. It called it coordinated theft. It asked everyone who read it to put their mark to it too. It had a lot of marks on it, from a lot of places Ruan had never heard of. Some of the villages it had gone through hadn't signed.

Half the stitchers wanted to sign both.

"The road's real," said Sorrel Mabb, that first night after the guests had gone up, banging the table. "I've seen the survey stakes. *Seen* them. They'll cut right through the beeches above the mill."

"Then stitch the stakes," said Wilda. "You did. It's on the cloth. Third row from the top."

"That's not the same as signing."

"No. It's not." Wilda didn't raise her voice. "The cloth says what we saw. You saw stakes in the beeches above the mill. That's what Larchmere saw of the road, and anyone who wants to know can come and see. The statement says the road will ruin the whole forest from end to end. Did you see that?"

Sorrel had nothing to say to that.

"It might be true," said Wilda. "I'm not saying it isn't. But it's not ours to say."

---

That second supper, the three of them got onto the year.

It was a thin supper. Mushroom broth, mostly broth: the blight had been at the forest mushrooms two summers running, and what was in the pot was what Wilda had been able to find, which wasn't much. Chestnut cakes, which were better. The kitchen fire, and the dark coming down early outside, and the cloth on its frame through the doorway, nearly done.

Nils started it. He said, half to his bowl, that it had been a strange year for his comparing. That the numbers didn't agree. The mill's book and the house's tithe-book, the same grain, the same week, and the figures all over the place. He'd never seen so many records disagree.

"Clerks," said Gideon comfortably. "Careless clerks. Bad year for it."

"Perhaps," said Nils.

Then Gideon said that it had been a strange year on the roads, too. More strangers than he'd ever seen. More people moving, with their whole households on carts. More shrines with every bed full. Everybody going somewhere.

"It's a restless year," he said. "Some years are. The roads get restless."

"Perhaps," said Nils again.

And Wilda said, looking through the door at the cloth, that it had been a strange year for the cloth. That she'd never had so many squares about roads. Roads and strangers and carts and survey stakes and people passing through. Usually it was weather and births and foxes.

"It's the moon," said Gideon. "Bad moon this autumn. It unsettles things."

"It's the weather," said a stitcher.

"It's the price of salt," said someone else.

They went round it for a bit like that, the three of them and the stitchers, each with their own reason. Ruan ate his chestnut cake. He didn't have a reason. He was only the carrier.

Then somebody's child spilled the broth, and everybody got up, and that was the end of that.

---

On the evening of the second day he went back down to the ford.

He didn't have a reason for that either. His feet took him. The rain had stopped in the hills, and the river had gone down as fast as it had come up, the way it did, and by evening it was back to the first notch, quick and clear and cold over the gravel, as if nothing had happened.

He sat on the bank where he'd sat the day before. The light was going green under the trees. A heron came down the river very slowly, one leg at a time, and stood in the shallows below the stakes, studying the water, and Ruan watched it, and it didn't catch anything, and after a while it went away again.

He thought about the middle stake. He thought about the river taking the bundle on his back and turning him round. He could feel it still, the pull of it, the weight going sideways, the book on his back gone heavy as a stone.

He'd seen it. He'd been the only one there. Nobody else in the world had been standing in the middle of the Larchmere ford at first light, hanging on to a stake, when the river took Twilighthollow's year. Just him.

Back at the house, the brown pages were drying on the rack by the hearth. They'd stopped running when they stopped being wet. They were just stained paper now. They'd stay that way.

He sat on the bank till it was dark.

---

On the last evening of Gathering Quiet, the stitchers finished the border. North side, east side, the river houses, all done. The west side, Twilighthollow's, was empty.

They'd talked about it all day. Some of them wanted Gideon's tree and fight. Some of them thought Nils could be trusted to put something reasonable together. One of the Mabbs wanted to leave a gap and fill it next year when the new book came. In the end they'd all gone home to their suppers without deciding, and left the cloth on its frame in the stitching room with the lamps lit and the empty edge, and Wilda had stayed to put the needles away.

Ruan stayed too.

He stood by the frame for a while. Wilda put the needles in their case, slowly, and didn't hurry him.

"She told me what was in it," he said at last. "The keeper at Twilighthollow. When she gave it me." He swallowed. "The tree. The road. The summer's debates. That's all. That's all I know."

"That's not enough to stitch," said Wilda.

"No." He'd known she'd say that. He looked at the empty border. "But I was there when it went. At the ford. I'm the only one who was." He made himself say the rest. "Can a carrier put a square on the cloth? If what he saw was a thing being lost?"

Wilda didn't answer straight away. She looked at the cloth, and then at him, for a long moment.

Then she took a needle back out of the case, and threaded it, and held it out to him.

"There," she said. She touched the cloth: one square at the head of the west border, plain undyed linen, the same as the backing. "Leave the middle. Stitch round it. Same as any other square."

He'd never held a needle in his life, not for this. His hands were big and rough from straps and fords, and the thread kept slipping, and Wilda didn't help, and didn't watch either. She just sat by the lamp with her own work and let him. It took him most of an hour. The stitches were too long, and some of them were crooked, and one side of the square was a little wider than the other.

When he'd done it there was a square at the head of Twilighthollow's border with a ring of brown stitching round it and nothing inside at all.

"That's yours," said Wilda.

---

The cloth went up the next morning, on the wall of the stitching room where every year's cloth went, next to last year's and the year before's. The whole parish came to look at it, same as every year.

Gideon stayed by it a good while, and then went off down the shrine roads that afternoon with a new story, about a house that left a square blank because nobody there had seen what belonged in it. It was the first story he'd carried all year that was true from start to finish.

Nils Orvay copied the cloth into his book before he left, square by square, the way he copied everything. When he got to the empty square at the head of the west border, he drew it exactly, a ring of crooked brown stitches with nothing inside, and wrote nothing in it.

The two statements left for the next house in the morning, unsigned, with the next carrier. Ruan watched them go.

At the end of the day, when the parish had gone home, the Mabbs' youngest came back on her own and planted herself under the cloth with her thumb in her mouth, looking at the corner.

"What's that one for?" she said. "There's nothing in it."

Ruan had been sweeping the stitching room. He leaned on his broom.

"I saw it go," he said. "That's what's in it."
