## The First Fish at Snowbreaker Quay

The smokehouse was cold, and it had no right to be.

Kirsa Lowe stood in the doorway at first light with her hands in her armpits and looked down the length of it: forty racks, empty; the long fire-trench down the middle, swept and laid and not lit; the vents in the roof shut; the oak and alder stacked against the back wall where she'd stacked them in the autumn, waiting. It was the end of spring. By now the place should have been so thick you couldn't see the far wall, and she should have been black to the elbows and happy.

"Smoke lies down, boats stay up," she said to the trench. It was what you said over a fire you'd laid well, so it'd draw low and long and keep the fish. There wasn't any fish. There wasn't any smoke either. She said it anyway. You didn't stop saying a thing just because there was no point.

Down on the slip, the boats were waiting.

They'd been waiting for nine days. Every boat in Snowbreaker Quay was down there, hauled up and loaded and ready, nets folded, crews sitting on the gunwales with their hands between their knees. Every morning they went out to the mouth of the bay to look, and every morning they came back in, and nobody went further, because there was nothing to go further for.

The run had moved.

Nobody knew why. The fish came every spring, up the coast and into the Quay's water, the bay and the long reach outside it, as far back as anyone had kept count, which was a long way. This year they'd come up the coast as usual and turned the wrong way at the headland, and now they were running thick as porridge in Skerrimouth's water, on the far side, where the Quay had no business fishing.

And the Quay couldn't sell a fish until the first fish was in.

That was the custom. The first fish of the spring had to come out of the Quay's own water, landed on the Quay's own slip, and go into the Quay's own smokehouse before a single fish was sold. Some people said it was for luck. Kirsa's grandmother had said it was so nobody could sneak off to market with half a catch before the price was set. Kirsa said it proved the fires. Either way it had always been done, and the advances the crews had taken over the winter, against the season's catch, came due at the first sale. No first fish, no sale. No sale, and by midsummer the Quay would owe the whole of the coast and have nothing on its racks.

The elders came down the shingle to the smokehouse once the tide had turned: three of them, in their good coats, which was a bad sign.

"Kirsa," said Old Ulric.

"No," said Kirsa.

"You don't know what we're going to say."

"You're going to say somebody should go over to Skerrimouth and talk to them about the water. And it can't be you, because if the elders go it's a clan matter and then it's a whole thing. So it's a smokehouse matter, and that's me." She shut the smokehouse door behind her. "No."

"There's nobody else."

"There's anybody else. There's the whole slip. Send a boat."

"You can't send a boat into somebody's water to ask if you can send a boat into their water," said Old Ulric reasonably. "That's what boats in the water are. The question."

"Then send someone who can talk."

The elders looked at each other.

"Kirsa," said Old Ulric, "there's a letter."

---

It was from Maris Bleakshore, which meant everybody had read it.

Last autumn she'd written to every cove on the coast, asking each to name the coves it would sail with in convoy next season. Coves it could trust, she'd said. Coves whose waters were clean. The Quay had sent its list back before the ice, the way everyone had, and on the list, the way it always had been, was Skerrimouth.

Now she'd written again. The first summer convoy would sail at midsummer. Before it did, every cove was to confirm its list.

"And the crews," said the youngest elder, "are saying Skerrimouth's had an offer."

"What sort of offer?"

"From Frostbay. A buyer. Coin now, for the whole of Skerrimouth's offshore water, this season. His boats fish it. Nobody else does." She spread her hands. "If they take it, they've sold their water to a stranger. The crews say a cove that's sold its water to a stranger isn't clean."

"The crews say a lot of things."

"They want Skerrimouth off the list."

Kirsa looked past them at the boats. Then she looked at the smokehouse, cold, with its door shut.

"I've never left it," she said. "Not in a run season. Not once since I took it."

"There isn't a run season," said Old Ulric. "That's the trouble."

---

The headland path went up behind the Quay and out along the cliffs, half a day's walk with the wind coming straight off the sea and into your teeth. Kirsa had a bag over her shoulder with bread and dried fish in it and a flask, and a coat that had been her mother's, and a temper.

She talked to herself the whole way, which was what she did. Mostly she talked to chimneys. There weren't many out here, just the odd shepherd's hut tucked into the heather, but every one she passed she looked at and said something about.

"Wet wood," she told one. "You'll be coughing by noon."

"That's climbing," she told another, where the smoke went straight up and broke off. "Smoke that climbs is smoke that lies. You think you're warm. You're not."

The huts didn't answer. She didn't need them to.

By the time she came down off the headland into Skerrimouth the afternoon was half gone, and her face had gone stiff with wind, and she'd decided on several things she was going to say and thrown all of them away.

Skerrimouth was smaller than the Quay. Kirsa had known that the way you know things about places you've never been: a fact, not a picture. In the flesh it was a single street of stone houses with turf on the roofs, a stone jetty, a strip of shingle, and boats. A lot of boats. Their boats were out, which was the first thing that went into her like a splinter. Every one of them was out, beyond the jetty, low in the water and working.

And on the shingle, by the jetty, there was a shed.

It was a smokehouse, of a kind. A family one: stone walls, a turf roof, a single fire-pit and room for maybe four racks if you were cruel about it. There was smoke coming out of the roof-hole, and Kirsa, without meaning to, without being able to help it, looked at the smoke and opened her mouth.

"That's never going to keep," she said. "You're cooking it, not smoking it."

"I know," said a woman's voice behind her. "Thank you. I'd been wondering."

---

Edda Kjeld was the headwoman of Skerrimouth, and she was not, Kirsa thought afterward, at all what she'd expected. Kirsa had expected someone grand, or hard, or at least someone who looked like they'd had an offer from Frostbay. Edda was a small dry woman with gray hair in a knot and salt-cracked hands, and she looked like someone who'd been gutting fish since dawn, because she had.

"You're the Quay's smokehouse," she said.

"Kirsa Lowe."

"I know who you are. Everybody on this coast knows who you are." Edda wiped her hands on a cloth. "You've come about the water."

"I've come because I was sent."

"Same thing." Edda turned and walked up the shingle toward the houses, and after a moment Kirsa followed her. "Sit down, then. You've walked a long way to insult my shed."

The headwoman's house had a table, and on the table there was a letter, and the letter had a Frostbay merchant's seal on it, a good one, red wax. Edda didn't hide it. She sat down with it in front of her and folded her hands on top of it.

"You know what's in it," she said.

"I've heard."

"Then you know what's in it." Edda looked at her. "Coin. Now. Real coin, enough to see us through the winter and the one after. For the offshore water, one season. They fish it, we don't."

"And nobody else does either."

"That's what sole means."

"The run's in your water," said Kirsa. "The whole run. All of it."

"I'd noticed."

"So the Quay can't take its first fish."

"I'd noticed that too." Edda's voice didn't change at all. "Your crews have been sitting on that slip for nine days telling each other we stole your fish. As if we went out in the night and turned them round."

Kirsa opened her mouth to say she'd never said that, and shut it again, because she'd thought it, on about the third day, standing in her cold smokehouse.

"I've got fish," Edda said. "I've got more fish than I've had in my life. I can't smoke them in that shed, so I sell them fresh to anybody who'll come for them, which isn't many out here, or I salt them, which is cheap. And I've got a man from Frostbay offering me coin." She put one finger on the letter. "And what have you come to offer me, smokehouse?"

Kirsa sat there.

"Nothing," she said. "I don't bargain."

"No," said Edda. "I'd heard that too."

---

It was too late to walk back over the headland. Edda gave her a bed in the loft over the net-store and, at dark, a bowl of stew from the big hearth pot that the whole street ate out of when the boats came in. Small fish and kelp and roots and a lot of pepper. Kirsa ate it at the end of the long table with the Skerrimouth crews, who looked at her the way you look at weather coming, and talked over her head about the catch.

It was good stew. She'd have said so, but nobody asked her.

Edda sat at the other end. Halfway through the meal one of the Skerrimouth men said something to her, low, and Edda answered him, not low at all.

"I know about the convoy. I can read."

"She'll take us off," the man said. "Bleakshore. If the Quay tells her we're not clean."

"Then the Quay can tell her." Edda didn't look down the table. "And we'll sail on our own, and see how they like the summer without us."

The man said, "We've never sailed on our own."

"No," said Edda. "We've never had a man from Frostbay offering us coin, either."

Kirsa finished her stew. She went up the ladder to the loft over the net-store and lay down in her mother's coat and listened to the sea, and to the wind, and to her own head telling her that it had known all along this would happen, and that she should have said no to Old Ulric and stayed with her fires.

---

In the morning she got up before light and went out, because she always got up before light, and her feet didn't know there was no smokehouse to go to.

The path went up out of Skerrimouth onto the headland, and at the top of the headland there was a cairn. She'd passed it in the dark the day before without seeing it. It was an old one, a big heap of gray stones taller than a man, with lichen on it, and from beside it you could see both waters at once: the Quay's bay on one side, long and gray and empty; Skerrimouth's on the other, dark with boats.

She sat down with her back to the cairn.

After a while somebody came up the path and sat down a little way off. It was Edda. She had a cup in each hand and handed one over, and Kirsa took it, and it was hot and had something in it that wasn't only tea.

They sat. The sun came up behind the cloud somewhere and made it lighter without making it any brighter. The wind dropped. Down below, on both sides, the morning fires were being lit: a few chimneys in Skerrimouth, and on the far side, small with distance, a few in the Quay. In the still air the smoke from both towns went up a little way and then lay down flat on the water, gray on gray, and spread out toward the headland from both sides, till you could hardly tell whose was whose.

Kirsa watched it for a while.

"Smoke lies down," she said, "boats stay up."

Edda didn't look at her. "We say it the other way round," she said. "Boats stay up when the smoke lies down."

"That's the same."

"It is not," said Edda. "We put the boats first."

That was all. They finished their cups and went back down, Edda to her fish and Kirsa to her bag and the long walk home, which she didn't take.

---

She meant to. She had the bag on her shoulder. But the path went past the jetty, and the Skerrimouth boats had come in early with the tide, and they were landing.

She stopped and watched them. She couldn't not.

It was a good catch. It was a great catch. The fish came up over the side in baskets, silver and slapping, more fish than Kirsa had seen land on the Quay's slip in three seasons put together. And they took it up the shingle to the gutting tables, and gutted it, fast and good, Edda in the middle of them with her sleeves up, and then they took it to the salting troughs.

Salt. All of it. Fish like that, fat and silver and just out of the water, the best fish she'd seen in years, and they were packing it down in salt in barrels like it was any old thing. And what went to the shed went to that one fire-pit, that turf roof, four racks, too much heat, cooked through and dried hard and fit for nothing but soup.

Kirsa stood on the shingle with her bag on her shoulder, and something in her chest hurt that had nothing to do with the walk.

Forty racks. Forty racks and a fire-trench the length of a ship, and a winter's wood stacked against the wall, and nothing on any of it.

She could see it whole: the Quay's boats in Skerrimouth's water, and the fish going up the shingle not to the troughs but over the headland. Her fires. Her racks. All of it, both towns' fish, smoking low and long the way it should, and selling at smoked prices, which were three times salt.

She could see it, and she couldn't think of a single way to say it that wasn't asking for something.

She went up to the cairn and sat there all afternoon trying.

---

Edda found her there at dusk.

"You're still here," Edda said.

"I'm still here."

Edda stood with her hands in her apron. "Your elders will think I've drowned you."

"They'll think I got lost talking to a chimney." Kirsa looked down at the two bays, both going dark. "Edda."

"What."

Kirsa didn't know how people did this. She'd never done it. Fish came in, she smoked them, the Quay sold them, and the price was the price. Smokehouses don't haggle.

So she didn't haggle. She just said it.

"Let our boats fish your water this season. Under your mark. You take a share of everything they bring in." She took a breath. "And bring me your fish. All of it. Over the headland, by cart, by boat, however you like. I'll smoke it. No charge. Forty racks. It'll sell smoked, and smoked's three times salt, and that's three times salt every week of the season, not one lot of coin now and nothing after."

Edda was quiet.

"I've got the fires," said Kirsa. "That's what I've got. That's all I've got."

The wind came up a little off the sea. Below them somebody in Skerrimouth was calling a dog.

"Whose fish goes on the racks first?" said Edda.

"What?"

"When the boats come in. Yours and mine. There's forty racks and there'll be more fish than forty racks some days. Whose goes on first?"

Kirsa understood her then. It wasn't a question about fish.

"Yours," she said. She said it fast, before the part of her that had kept the Quay's smokehouse for twenty years could stand up and object. "Yours first."

Edda looked at her for a long moment.

"The Frostbay man," she said at last, "wanted to know whose water it was. You're the first one who's asked whose fish." She took her hands out of her apron. "All right."

---

The first fish came in two days later, on the morning tide, in a Quay boat that had been out in Skerrimouth's water under Skerrimouth's mark: a big silver one, nearly as long as Kirsa's arm, landed on the Quay's slip at first light with half of both towns standing on the shingle to see it.

The elders had argued about it for a whole night. The custom said the first fish came from the Quay's own water. In the end Old Ulric, who was tired and wanted his breakfast, said that this season the Quay's own water was the water the two towns shared, which is what elders are for, and nobody argued with him, because they wanted their breakfasts too.

So the first fish came up the slip in a basket, and into the smokehouse, and Kirsa lit the trench.

She lit it the way she'd been taught: low, oak first, then the alder on top, and the vents open just a crack. The fire caught and settled and began to draw, and the smoke came up off it and spread along under the roof, thicker and thicker, gray and sweet, until you couldn't see the far wall and Kirsa was black to the elbows and couldn't stop smiling.

By noon the carts were coming over the headland from Skerrimouth, and the racks were filling, and Edda's fish went on first.

That afternoon the youngest elder brought Maris Bleakshore's letter down to the smokehouse, and Kirsa wiped her hands and wrote at the bottom of the Quay's list, next to Skerrimouth, *confirmed*, and put her mark to it. Over the headland, Edda was doing the same.

The first fish came out of the smoke at dusk, gold and stiff and smelling of oak, and by custom it belonged to the keeper of the smokehouse, to eat on the slip in front of everybody, so the whole Quay could see the fires were good.

Kirsa carried it out onto the slip. The crews were there, and the elders, and a lot of Skerrimouth, who'd come over with the carts and stayed. Edda stood at the edge of them, arms crossed tight against the wind.

Kirsa looked down at the fish. Then she crossed the shingle and held it out to Edda.

"Smoke lies down," she said.

Edda took it.

"Boats stay up," she said.
