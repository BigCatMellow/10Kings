## Inspected, Not Guaranteed

Tamsin Rake's shop was the only one on Stonefield's upper lane with its door open, and the only one with a cold hearth that looked as though it had been swept that morning, and so Wurdren went in.

Every tool on her walls hung inside its own painted outline. Tongs in tong shapes, hammers in hammer shapes, a row of files graded by size, and one empty outline shaped like a small punch, which somebody was going to hear about. The hearth was black and clean. The anvil had a cloth over it, the way people covered a birdcage at night.

"We're cold," said the smith, without looking up from the bench. She was an old woman in a leather apron, sharpening a chisel with a stone and a great deal of patience. "Everybody's cold. Fires come back at Forge Reawakening. Six days."

"I heard." Wurdren unslung the sword and laid it on the bench beside her, still in its scabbard. "I was hoping for a new grip. The old one's rotten."

She put down the stone, drew the sword, turned it in the gray light from the door and looked at the hilt, and then, without asking, knocked the pommel loose with the heel of her hand and slid the grip off the tang.

The tang was cracked. It was a fine dark line where the blade narrowed into the handle, and it ran most of the way through.

"That's not a grip," said Tamsin Rake. "That's a weld. And a weld wants a fire." She looked at him properly for the first time. "You've been swinging this?"

"Not lately."

"Good. You'd have lost the blade into somebody's hedge." She laid it down on the bench as though it were a patient. "Six days. I'm not putting a cold patch on a tang. It'd be attempted, not done, and nothing leaves this shop attempted."

Ironcrest speech made you say which kind of done a thing was: attempted, completed, inspected by someone who knew, or guaranteed by someone who would answer for it. Wurdren had heard Ironcrest people apply the distinction to horseshoes, contracts, marriages, and once, memorably, to a pie. He had never quite heard it said with the weight Tamsin Rake put on *attempted*, as though it were a word for something that had died.

"Six days," he said.

"There's an inn. There's a tavern. The Quench." She picked up her stone. "Tavern's better. Inn's run by a Greenvale woman who makes you take your boots off."

He left the sword with her. On his way out, he saw her pick it up again and look along the blade toward the light, with an expression he could not read.

* * *

The Quench was warm and loud, and it was warm and loud in the particular way of a tavern full of forge crews with nothing to do. They clustered along the fire end of the room out of habit, the way men who worked beside heat all day always did, and talked over one another, also out of habit, the way men did who were used to being heard over hammers. Half of them were on short hours. Wurdren gathered this within a quarter of an hour, because it was all anybody talked about.

The farms down the valley had stopped buying tools. Last autumn the Greenvale grain had gone for nothing, and farms that couldn't sell grain couldn't pay for plowshares, so the forges were idle. A forge that relit at Reawakening would light for half-days, and some crews were talking about not coming back at all until the masters paid full hours.

"Strike," said a big man at the fire end, as though the word had a taste.

"You'll strike," said the woman beside him, "and eat what?"

"Same as now. Nothing, half the week."

"The Slatehallow lad didn't strike," said somebody else. "He got a patron."

A few of them laughed, and a few didn't. Wurdren, who was on his second cup and had not been asked, asked.

"Up north," said the keeper, who was wiping the same cup he'd been wiping since Wurdren came in. He was a thin, weathered man who had kept The Quench for thirty years and, by his own account, had heard every forge story in Ironcrest and believed none of them. "Lad called Orin Slatehallow. Good at something new, and the guild wouldn't have it. Then somebody nobody's ever seen sent him money to do it anyway. Now he's got a workshop, and he's got the patron, and nobody knows who that is." He set the cup down. "Lucky lad. Or not. Depends who you ask."

"I heard he's doing well," said the woman.

"I heard he doesn't answer his letters from home," said the big man.

Wurdren had been in enough taverns to know that this was the kind of story that would be told a dozen ways before morning, and he let it go. It was, after all, a story about a stranger a long way off.

It was sometime after this that the district guildmaster came in, and sometime after that that Wurdren found himself, to his own surprise, a judge.

Master Oswin Vey was a narrow, upright man with a ledger under his arm, and he came in shaking rain off his coat and saying to the room at large that the road from the upper district had washed out. The unbound judge would not be coming. The judgments would have to be deferred.

The room went quiet in a way Wurdren recognized.

"Deferred till when?" said the woman at the fire end.

"Midsummer, if the road's mended. Autumn if not."

"You can't defer Col," said somebody. "He's been waiting two years."

"Nor Marta."

"The rule is the rule," said Master Vey, and he opened his ledger on the bar as if to show them the rule, though what the ledger actually held, Wurdren later learned, was forty-one pages of complaints about smoke and water from the farms, which Vey was losing one page at a time. "Three judges. Two from the guild, one bound to no apprentice by blood, craft or debt. You'll forgive me, but in this town every soul I know is somebody's aunt."

"He's not," said the keeper, and pointed at Wurdren with the cup.

Master Vey turned and looked at him, thoroughly. "You're a stranger."

"Passing through," Wurdren said. "My sword's cracked. I'm waiting on the fires."

"You've no kin in Stonefield?"

"None."

"No craft? No guild?"

"No."

"You owe no one here money?"

Wurdren thought. "I owe the keeper for two cups."

"That's all right," said the keeper. "I'll allow it."

Master Vey looked at him for a long moment, and Wurdren could see him deciding that a stranger was an insult to the craft, and then that a stranger was at least neutral, and then that neutral was what the rule required, and then — this was the part Wurdren would think about later — that he was very likely going to regret it.

"Three days," said Master Vey. "The guild hall. The first viewing is at noon."

"I don't know anything about smithing."

"The rule doesn't ask you to," said Master Vey. "It asks you to be nobody's aunt."

Wurdren, who had come for a grip and got a weld and now a judgeship, did what he generally did when the world handed him something he hadn't asked for. He accepted it and told the room about his sword.

"It's thirty years old," he said. "The smith who made it swore it was the finest steel she'd ever worked. Guaranteed it. Put her mark on it. She said, if this blade ever fails its bearer—"

"Is this a long story?" said the woman at the fire end.

"It's a medium story."

"It's a long story," said the keeper, who had heard it forty minutes before, "and he's not even got to the bit with himself in it yet."

* * *

Nell Haskett sold eggs from a table by her farmyard gate, and Wurdren went to buy some on the second morning because the inn made you take your boots off and the tavern did not do breakfast.

He came away with a dozen eggs, a slice of bread with butter he hadn't asked for, and a complete census of everything broken in the lower valley.

Nell was a broad, cheerful woman with a farm on the flat below the forges, and she talked the way water ran downhill. Her gate hinge had broken in the winter, and Col Barrowfield had mended it. Her neighbor's plow had cracked its share, and Col had welded it in the autumn before the fires went out. The Pellows' pump had seized, and Col had freed it. The mill's sluice-gate winch had stripped a gear, and Col had cut a new one from scrap. She could name them all, down the valley and back, like a woman reciting her grandchildren.

"Col's the one presenting?" said Wurdren. "The apprentice?"

"Col's the one who fixes things," said Nell. "Presenting's what the guild calls it."

"And Marta?"

"Marta makes swords. Lovely ones. Nobody down here needs a sword." She saw his face and laughed. "Well. Almost nobody."

She walked him to the end of the yard to show him the ditch. It ran along the bottom of her land, the boundary with the next farm, and carried water down from the foothills to the fields. It was gray and scummed, and a line of dead grass along its edge showed where it had risen in the winter.

"Quench water," said Nell. "From the forges up the lane. When they're working, they cool the iron, and the water comes down the hill and into my ditch, and then it's in my beans." She kicked a clod into the scum. "Every farm on the flat's got a page in Oswin Vey's ledger about it. It's the smoke they shout about. It's the water that does the harm."

"Isn't there anything to be done?"

"There's Col." She pointed up the hill, to a forge whose chimney Wurdren could just see above the hedges. "The Bracket forge put one of his troughs in, before the winter. Cools the water, settles the muck out of it, and pumps it back into their own cistern to use again. The ditch below the Bracket forge ran clear all autumn." She looked at her own ditch. "The rest of them won't have it. It's plumbing, they say."

Wurdren put the eggs in his coat very carefully. "Who says?"

"The guild," said Nell. "Who else?"

* * *

The guild hall stood at the top of the town, and it had a long, cold room with three judges' benches at one end and, along the whole length of the wall opposite the windows, the memorial: row on row of names cut into slate, forge workers dead on the job or of it, going back further than the town's oldest house. At Forge Reawakening each year, Wurdren had been told, the guild read every name aloud before the fires were relit. It took most of an afternoon. Nobody had ever suggested shortening it.

Marta Dunnock presented first, and her blade was good.

Wurdren knew that much. He knew what a good sword felt like in the hand, and when she passed it to him along the bench it came up light and alive, and balanced just forward of the guard, and the edge caught the window light in one clean line from heel to point. The other two judges, both guild masters with arms like hams, passed it back and forth and said *inspected* to each other in the tone Ironcrest people used for *very, very good.* Marta stood very straight and did not smile, and Wurdren liked her.

Col Barrowfield presented second, and brought a pump.

It stood on a low cart: an iron settling trough the length of a man, baffled inside, with a hand pump at one end and a pipe coiled at the other, and a smaller tank with a lid. It was beautifully made. Even Wurdren could see that: the joins were close and even, and the pump handle moved like a door on good hinges. Col was a tall, awkward young man with a Greenvale mother's round face and an Ironcrest father's shoulders, and he explained it in a rush. The trough cooled the quench water, the baffles made the scale and muck settle out, and the pump sent the clean water back into the forge's own cistern, so the forge used less and let none of it run down the hill.

The two masters looked at it. One of them opened the lid of the small tank and closed it again.

"Plumbing," said Master Vey.

Col's face went red.

"It's good plumbing," Vey said, and he did not say it cruelly. "I'll not pretend otherwise. But a masterwork is a smith's work, Barrowfield, and this is a tinker's. If the guild passes this, then next year every lad who can patch a kettle comes up for his mastery, and then every patched kettle's a master's work, and a master's rate goes to a tinker's rate." He looked out of the long window, toward the lane and the cold chimneys. "Half my members are on short hours. I will not tell them their rates are halved as well."

Nobody said anything. Wurdren had not expected Vey to have a reason, and he found he did not like the reason any less for being fair.

"What does the rule say?" he said.

The two masters and Vey all turned to look at him, and he realized he had spoken.

"The rule for a masterwork," he said. "What does it actually say it must be?"

Master Vey opened the guild's book, not his ledger. It was very old and bound in leather gone soft. He read it in the formal register, precisely. "*A masterwork shall be the apprentice's own work, completed, and inspected by three judges before it is passed.*"

"Inspected," said Wurdren.

"Inspected," said Vey.

"Not *guaranteed*?"

"A masterwork is never guaranteed. The apprentice isn't a master yet. There's no one to answer for it." Vey closed the book. "Why?"

Wurdren did not know why. He had asked because he wanted to know what the words said, and because he was a stranger and the only way he knew to be useful in a room of people who knew more than he did was to ask what they meant by things. He said so. Vey looked at him with an expression that was not quite suspicion and not quite respect.

At the end of the viewing, in the cold hall, while the masters argued over Marta's blade, Wurdren told the second of the two masters the story of his sword. He got as far as the smith's guarantee before the master remembered an appointment.

The next morning a letter came for Col.

Wurdren heard about it at breakfast, because everybody did. A carrier had brought it up from the valley road, and it was addressed in a clear, educated hand to *Col Barrowfield, apprentice, Stonefield Forge*, and Col had opened it in the lane in front of half the upper street. It was unsigned. It said that his methods had come to the attention of one who valued useful work. It said that, should the guild fail to recognize them, a sum sufficient to set him up in his own workshop was available, with no condition except that he continue. It said that a reply left with the carrier would find its way.

"Good paper," said the keeper, who had not seen it and had heard all about it. "They say it's good paper. Better than anything in Stonefield."

"It's a kind offer," said the woman from the fire end.

"The Slatehallow lad had a kind offer," said the big man, and drank his tea.

* * *

The charcoal for the relighting came down from the hill forests on carts, along a road that was, in early spring, less a road than a long brown argument between two ditches. The carts were late. The carters had sent word from the top of the hill that they were stuck, and everyone with a strong back and nothing to do went up to help.

Wurdren had a strong back and nothing to do. Col Barrowfield came too, and walked beside Wurdren's borrowed horse up the charcoal road with his hands in his pockets.

It was a long, cold, filthy morning. The carts had sunk to their axles in a stretch of road below the woods where the spring runoff crossed it, and they had to be unloaded, dug out, pushed, reloaded and pushed again. At the top of the stretch, where the road came out of the trees and looked down over the valley, a group of farmers from the flat stood watching with their arms folded. Nell Haskett was not among them. Several of her neighbors were.

"You're a judge," one of them said to Wurdren, as he went past with a sack of charcoal on each shoulder.

"This week."

"Then you can tell the guild." The farmer nodded down the valley, toward the gray thread of the ditches running across the flat. "Relighting day, we'll be here. In the road. Their fires don't light till our ditches run clean."

"That's not— I only—" Wurdren started, and then stopped, because it was not his quarrel, and because the man was not being unreasonable, only tired.

"Tell them," the farmer said, and went back to standing with his arms folded.

They got three carts out of the worst of it by evening, as far as the washout below the woods, where there was nothing to do but wait for the fourth. The fourth was stuck fast, and would have to wait for the ground to dry, and none of the charcoal was going anywhere without it. On the ride back down, with the dusk coming on and his coat heavy with mud, Wurdren was so tired that it was a while before he noticed that Col had stopped talking.

"I had a letter," Col said at last.

"I heard."

Col took it out of his coat. It was folded small and a little damp at the edges, and he unfolded it and read part of it aloud, the part that mattered, in a careful voice, as if he were testing whether the words would hold his weight.

"'Should the guild fail to recognize them.'" He looked at the letter. "'Should the guild fail to recognize them.'" He read it twice. "They will, you know. Fail. Vey won't pass plumbing."

"May I?"

Col handed it up to him. Wurdren held it up against the last of the light in the west, the way you held up a coin to see if it was clipped. It was heavy paper, cream-colored, far too good for Stonefield. In the light he could see a watermark in the grain: a small ship's lantern, the kind that hung from a stern rail. He didn't know what it meant. He supposed it was the mark of whoever had made the paper, somewhere with ships.

He gave it back.

"Take it," he said.

Col looked at him.

"Take it," Wurdren said again. He said it gently, and he meant it, and he could hear in his own voice how much he had wanted someone to say that to him. "Somebody's seen your work. Somebody thinks it's worth something. You can wait a long time for that." He looked out over the dark flat, where the ditches were. "If the guild won't see it, let somebody else."

Col folded the letter very small and put it back in his coat, and they rode the rest of the way down without talking.

* * *

The next morning Tamsin Rake sent for him.

She had the sword on the bench under the good light from the door, stripped to the bare blade, and she had a glass on a stand, and a file, and a little dish of acid with a feather in it, and she did not look up when he came in.

"Sit," she said.

He sat.

"This was sold to you," said Tamsin, "as something special."

"The smith said it was the finest steel she'd ever—"

"I know what the smith said. I can read it." She turned the blade and pointed with the file to a mark near the guard, very worn, that Wurdren had long ago stopped seeing: a maker's stamp, and beside it a second, smaller stamp in the old formal script. "That's a guarantee mark. That smith *guaranteed* this. Put her name to it. Said she'd answer for it." Tamsin set the file down. "It's ordinary steel."

Wurdren said nothing.

"Good ordinary steel," said Tamsin. "Decent. Nothing wrong with it. The kind of steel you'd get from any honest shop in the lowlands for a fair price. But it's not what she guaranteed, and she knew it wasn't, and she stamped it anyway." She touched the flat of the blade with one finger, very lightly. "That crack's thirty years of ordinary steel doing more than it was ever made for."

He found he could not look at the sword. He looked at the wall of tools instead, at the empty outline where the small punch should have been.

"Now," said Tamsin Rake, "look at the edge."

He looked.

"Look at it," she said. "Thirty years. You know how many times this has been ground? Hardly at all. You've honed it. Every week, I'd say, by the wear, and you've never once let it go dull enough to need grinding. The fuller's clean. There's not a pit of rust on it. The blade's as straight as the day she made it, and that's not the steel. That's you." She picked up the glass and put it down again. "The steel's not special. The upkeep is. That's inspected. I'd put my own mark to that."

Wurdren swallowed. "Can you mend it?"

"I can mend it. With ordinary steel." She made a face. "You'd want better for a tang, and there isn't any. You can't get good stock in Ironcrest this spring for love or coin. It's all gone to private contracts. So you'll get a weld as ordinary as the rest of it." She looked at him. "That suit you?"

"Yes," said Wurdren.

On his way out he started to tell her how he'd come by the sword, and got as far as "When I—" and heard himself, and stopped.

"Good," said Tamsin Rake, and went back to the blade.

* * *

On relighting day the whole town came out.

The fires would be lit at dusk, after the names. Before that, at noon, the judges would give their decision on the apprentices. And before that, in the middle of the morning, Wurdren went to Master Vey with his question.

"The rule says *inspected*," he said. "By three judges. Does it say where?"

Vey opened the old book again and read the rule again, very slowly, and closed it.

"No," he said.

"Then could we inspect it where it works?"

Master Vey looked at him for a long time. Then he looked out of the window, down the hill, toward the flat, where Nell Haskett was standing at the bottom of the guild hall steps with her arms full of something and her face full of purpose. She had been standing there, the porter said, for an hour.

"Is this your idea," said Vey, "or hers?"

"Hers, I think," said Wurdren. "I only asked where."

So the three judges walked down the lane.

Nell led them. She led them as though she had been planning the route all winter, and perhaps she had. She took them first to her own gate, which swung on a hinge Col Barrowfield had mended, and made each master open and close it. She took them to the Pellows', where the pump ran, and to the mill, where the sluice winch turned on a gear Col had cut from scrap, and to four more farms and a smithy and a chapel door, and at each one she named what had been broken and who had mended it and when, and what it would have cost to replace new, in a year when nobody on the flat had that kind of money.

And then she took them to the ditch below the Bracket forge.

The water was clear. It ran bright over the stones between banks where the grass was coming green, and a hundred yards along, where the ditch passed below the next forge up the lane, it turned gray and scummed and the grass along its edge was dead.

The three judges stood and looked at it for some time.

"The Bracket forge," Nell said, "put in one of Col's troughs before the winter. That's all. That's the whole difference."

"Every farm on this flat," said Master Vey slowly, "has a page in my ledger about this ditch."

"Every farm on this flat," said Nell, "is still a farm. Did you know that? Not one of us has sold. There's farms up the valley sold out this winter, when the grain went for nothing. Not us. We couldn't buy a new plow and we couldn't buy a new pump and we couldn't buy a new gate. So we had them mended." She looked at him. "And when the grain comes back, Oswin Vey, we'll buy plowshares again. From you. From your members. At a master's rate."

Master Vey said nothing for a long moment. Wurdren could see him working it through the way he worked through everything, by the book: repairs kept the farms solvent, solvent farms bought tools, and tools paid his members' wages. He had fought to protect those rates, and they had been protected all winter by the same work he had called plumbing.

"Inspected," said Vey, to the ditch. "In service."

The other two masters looked at each other and nodded.

Both apprentices passed. Marta's blade was inspected in the hall, Col's work was inspected in the valley, and the guild book recorded them both on the same page. When Vey read the decision aloud at noon, Marta walked straight across the hall and shook Col's hand. She said something to him that nobody else heard, and he laughed for the first time all week.

* * *

The charcoal carts came down the hill at the end of the afternoon, all four of them, and the farmers from the flat were pushing.

They had gone up to the road that morning to stand in it, and had heard somehow, the way news traveled in Stonefield, that the judges had walked the ditch and passed the plumbing. By the time the last cart came out of the mud they had their shoulders against its tailboard, and they pushed it all the way down to the guild hall, and nobody mentioned standing in the road again.

At dusk the guild read the names. It took most of an hour. Wurdren stood at the back of the long hall and listened to every one of them, forge workers dead on the job or of it, going back further than the town's oldest house, and nobody hurried, and nobody coughed. Then the masters carried coals from the guild hearth down the lane to every forge in Stonefield, one after another, and the chimneys began to smoke, and the town smelled, for the first time since autumn, like itself.

Tamsin Rake welded the tang that night. She did it with ordinary steel and she did it perfectly, and she fitted a new grip of plain ash, and on the ricasso, beside the old guarantee mark, she struck a new mark of her own. It was the stamp for *inspected*.

"Not guaranteed," she said, handing it back. "I won't answer for the steel. But I'll answer for what's been done to it."

Wurdren took the sword and weighed it in his hand. It was a fine, ordinary sword, and it balanced exactly as it always had.

On his way to The Quench he passed Col Barrowfield's bench, where the new masters' tools were laid out for the morning, and saw the letter lying among them. It was cream-colored and still folded small and slightly damp at the edges, and it had not been answered. Col was across the lane with Marta and half the upper street, arguing happily about something, and did not see him look.

Wurdren thought about what he had said on the charcoal road. *Take it.* He thought about the ditch, and the gates and pumps and the gear from scrap, and about how nobody had needed his advice after all. He was more glad of that than he could easily have explained.

At The Quench, the keeper gave him a bowl of forged harvest stew, barley and beef and root vegetables in a dark, smoky broth, the kind of stew forge towns and farm towns both claimed to have invented. Somebody at the fire end asked about his sword. Wurdren said it was mended. He did not tell the story. After a while the keeper looked at him with something like suspicion and said, "Well, that's a first," and gave him more stew.

He meant to leave the next morning. He stayed two more days instead, for no reason he gave anyone.

On the second morning, Nell Haskett came up the lane to find him.

"My gate's sticking," she said. "The other one. On the far field. I'd have asked Col, but he's a master now, and I can't afford his rates."
