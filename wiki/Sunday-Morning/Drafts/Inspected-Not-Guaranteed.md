## Inspected, Not Guaranteed

"Attempted," said Tamsin Rake.

She held the tang up to the gray light from the door. A fine dark line ran where the blade narrowed into the handle. Most of the way through.

"Sorry?" said Wurdren.

"If I patched that cold, it'd be attempted. Not done." She laid the sword on the bench like a patient. "Nothing leaves this shop attempted."

He'd come in for a grip. The old one was rotten. She'd knocked the pommel off with the heel of her hand before he finished the sentence, slid the grip off, and found the crack.

"That's not a grip," she said. "That's a weld. A weld wants a fire."

Wurdren looked at the hearth. Black. Clean. Swept that morning. The anvil had a cloth over it, the way you'd cover a birdcage for the night.

"We're cold," said Tamsin. "Everybody's cold. Fires come back at Forge Reawakening."

"When's that?"

"Six days."

Every tool on her walls hung inside its own painted outline. Tongs in tong shapes. Hammers in hammer shapes. A row of files, graded by size. And one empty outline shaped like a small punch.

Someone was going to hear about that.

"You been swinging this?" she said.

"Not lately."

"Good. You'd have lost the blade in somebody's hedge." She picked up her sharpening stone. "There's an inn and a tavern. Tavern's better. Inn's run by a Greenvale woman who makes you take your boots off."

He left the sword with her.

On his way out he looked back. She'd picked it up again and was sighting down the blade toward the light.

He couldn't read her face at all.

---

"Strike," said the big man at the fire end of The Quench, like the word had a taste.

"You'll strike," said the woman beside him, "and eat what?"

"Same as now. Nothing, half the week."

The Quench was warm and loud. Forge crews bunched at the fire end out of habit — men who stood next to heat all day, standing next to heat — and talked over each other, also out of habit. Men used to being heard over hammers.

Half of them were on short hours. It took Wurdren about a quarter of an hour to learn that, because it was all anybody talked about.

The farms down the valley had stopped buying tools. Last autumn the Greenvale grain had gone for nothing. Farms that couldn't sell grain couldn't buy plowshares. So when the fires relit this year, they'd relight for half days.

"The Slatehallow lad didn't strike," someone said. "He got a patron."

A few of them laughed. A few didn't.

"Who?" said Wurdren.

The keeper was wiping a cup. He'd been wiping the same cup since Wurdren walked in. Thin, weathered, thirty years behind this bar, and by his own account he'd heard every forge story in Ironcrest and believed none of them.

"Lad up north. Orin Slatehallow. Good at something new, and the guild wouldn't have it. Then somebody nobody's ever seen sent him money to do it anyway." He set the cup down. "Now he's got a workshop. And he's got the patron. And nobody knows who that is."

"I heard he's doing well," said the woman.

"I heard he doesn't answer letters from home," said the big man.

Wurdren let it go. A story about a stranger a long way off.

That was when the guildmaster came in, shaking rain off his coat.

Master Oswin Vey. Narrow, upright, a ledger under one arm. He announced to the room that the road from the upper district had washed out, the unbound judge wouldn't be coming, and the apprenticeship judgments would have to be deferred.

The room went quiet in a way Wurdren recognized.

"Deferred till when?"

"Midsummer, if the road's mended. Autumn if not."

"You can't defer Col. He's been waiting two years."

"Nor Marta."

"The rule is the rule." Vey opened his ledger on the bar, like the rule was in it. It wasn't. It was forty-one pages of farmers' complaints about smoke and water, which he was losing one page at a time. "Three judges. Two from the guild. One bound to no apprentice by blood, craft or debt." He looked around the room. "You'll forgive me. In this town, every soul I know is somebody's aunt."

"He's not," said the keeper, and pointed the cup at Wurdren.

Vey turned. Looked at him. Thoroughly.

"You're a stranger."

"Passing through. My sword's cracked. I'm waiting on the fires."

"Kin in Stonefield?"

"None."

"Craft? Guild?"

"No."

"Owe anybody here money?"

Wurdren thought about it. "I owe him for two cups."

"That's all right," said the keeper. "I'll allow it."

Wurdren watched Vey's face go through it. A stranger is an insult to the craft. A stranger is at least neutral. Neutral is what the rule requires.

And then — Wurdren saw it land — *I'm going to regret this.*

"Three days," said Vey. "The guild hall. First viewing at noon."

"I don't know anything about smithing."

"The rule doesn't ask you to. It asks you to be nobody's aunt."

He left.

Wurdren looked at his cup for a while. Then, because the room was still looking at him, he told them about his sword.

"It's thirty years old. The smith who made it swore it was the finest steel she'd ever worked. Guaranteed it. Put her mark on it. She said, if this blade ever fails its bearer—"

"Is this a long story?" said the woman at the fire end.

"It's a medium story."

"It's a long story," said the keeper, who'd already heard it once that evening. "And he hasn't even got to the part with him in it."

---

Nell Haskett sold eggs from a table by her farmyard gate.

Wurdren bought a dozen. He came away with the eggs, a slice of bread and butter he hadn't asked for, and a complete census of everything broken in the lower valley.

"Gate hinge, this winter. Col fixed it. Neighbor's plowshare cracked. Col welded it before the fires went out. Pellows' pump seized. Col. The mill's sluice winch stripped a gear. Col cut a new one out of scrap." She counted them off on her fingers like grandchildren. "I could go on."

"Col's the apprentice presenting?"

"Col's the one who fixes things. Presenting is what the guild calls it."

"And Marta?"

"Marta makes swords. Lovely ones. Nobody down here needs a sword." She caught his face. Laughed. "Well. *Almost* nobody."

She walked him to the bottom of the yard.

The ditch ran along the edge of her land, carrying water down from the foothills. It was gray. Scummed. A line of dead grass along the bank showed where it had risen over the winter.

The smell was flat and metallic. Like licking a nail.

"Quench water," Nell said. "From the forges up the lane. They cool the iron, the water comes down the hill, and then it's in my beans." She kicked a clod into it. "Every farm on the flat's got a page in Oswin Vey's ledger. It's the smoke they shout about. It's the water that does the harm."

"Isn't there anything to be done?"

"There's Col." She pointed up the hill, to one chimney above the hedges. "The Bracket forge put in one of his troughs before winter. Cools the water, settles the muck out, pumps it back to their own cistern. Ditch below the Bracket forge ran clear all autumn."

She looked at her own ditch.

"The rest won't have it. Plumbing, they say."

Wurdren put the eggs very carefully in his coat.

"Who says?"

"The guild," said Nell. "Who else?"

---

The guild hall had a long, cold room with three judges' benches at one end. The wall across from the windows was the memorial: row after row of names cut into slate. Forge workers dead on the job, or of it. Further back than the town's oldest house.

At Forge Reawakening they read every name aloud before the fires were lit. It took most of an afternoon.

Nobody had ever suggested shortening it.

Marta Dunnock presented first. Her blade was good.

Wurdren knew that much. It came up light and alive in his hand, balanced just past the guard, and the edge caught the window light in one clean line, heel to point. The two guild masters passed it back and forth and said *inspected* to each other in the voice Ironcrest people used for *very, very good.*

Marta stood very straight and didn't smile.

Wurdren liked her.

Col Barrowfield presented second.

He brought a pump.

It sat on a low cart. An iron settling trough the length of a man, baffled inside, a hand pump at one end, a coiled pipe at the other, and a smaller lidded tank. Even Wurdren could see it was beautifully made. The joins were close and even. The handle moved like a door on good hinges.

Col was tall and awkward, with his Greenvale mother's round face and his Ironcrest father's shoulders, and he explained it in a rush. The trough cooled the quench water. The baffles let the scale and the muck settle out. The pump sent clean water back to the forge's own cistern. The forge used less, and none of it ran down the hill.

One master lifted the lid on the small tank. Put it back down.

"Plumbing," said Vey.

Col went red.

"Good plumbing." Vey didn't say it cruelly. "I'll not pretend otherwise. But a masterwork is a smith's work, Barrowfield. This is a tinker's. Pass this, and next year every lad who can patch a kettle comes up for his mastery. Then a patched kettle's a master's work. And a master's rate becomes a tinker's rate."

He looked out the long window at the cold chimneys.

"Half my members are on short hours. I won't tell them their rates are halved as well."

Nobody said anything.

Wurdren hadn't expected Vey to have a reason. He found he didn't like the reason any less for being fair.

"What does the rule say?"

Three heads turned.

He realized he'd said it out loud.

"The rule for a masterwork. What does it actually say it has to be?"

Vey opened the guild book. The old one, bound in leather gone soft. He read in the formal register.

"*A masterwork shall be the apprentice's own work, completed, and inspected by three judges before it is passed.*"

"Inspected," said Wurdren.

"Inspected."

"Not guaranteed?"

"A masterwork is never guaranteed. The apprentice isn't a master yet. There's nobody to answer for it." Vey closed the book. "Why?"

Wurdren didn't know why. He'd asked because he wanted to know what the words said. He was a stranger. Asking what people meant by things was the only way he knew to be useful in a room full of people who knew more than he did.

Vey gave him a look that was not quite suspicion.

Not quite respect either.

Afterward, in the cold hall, Wurdren started telling the second guild master about his sword. He got as far as the smith's mark before the master remembered an appointment.

---

The letter came the next morning.

Everybody heard. A carrier brought it up from the valley road, addressed in a clear, educated hand to *Col Barrowfield, apprentice, Stonefield Forge*, and Col opened it in the lane in front of half the upper street.

Unsigned.

His methods had come to the attention of one who valued useful work. Should the guild fail to recognize them, a sum sufficient to set him up in his own workshop was available. No condition but that he continue. A reply left with the carrier would find its way.

"Good paper," said the keeper, who hadn't seen it. "They say it's *good* paper."

"Kind offer," said the woman from the fire end.

"The Slatehallow lad had a kind offer," said the big man, and drank his tea.

---

The charcoal road, in early spring, wasn't really a road. It was a long brown argument between two ditches.

The carts for the relighting were stuck in it. Everyone with a strong back and nothing to do went up the hill to help.

Wurdren had a strong back and nothing to do. Col came too, hands in his pockets, walking beside Wurdren's borrowed horse.

Unload. Dig. Push. Reload. Push again. Mud to the knees. Mud in his collar. Mud, somehow, in his left ear.

At the top of the worst stretch, where the road came out of the trees, a line of farmers from the flat stood watching with their arms folded.

"You're a judge," one of them said, as Wurdren went past with a sack of charcoal on each shoulder.

"This week."

"Then tell the guild." The farmer nodded down the valley at the gray threads of the ditches. "Relighting day, we'll be here. In the road. Their fires don't light until our water runs clean."

"I don't— I'm only—"

Wurdren stopped. It wasn't his quarrel. And the man wasn't being unreasonable. Just tired.

"Tell them," said the farmer, and went back to folding his arms.

By evening they had three carts out of the worst of it. As far as the washout below the woods, where there was nothing to do but wait for the fourth. The fourth was stuck fast. It would sit until the ground dried.

None of the charcoal was going anywhere without it.

On the ride back down, dusk coming on, coat heavy with mud, Wurdren was so tired it took him a while to notice Col had stopped talking.

"I got a letter," Col said.

"I heard."

Col took it out of his coat. Folded small. A little damp at the edges. He unfolded it and read the only part that mattered, carefully, as if he were testing whether the words would hold his weight.

"*Should the guild fail to recognize them.*"

He looked at it.

"*Should the guild fail to recognize them.*"

"They will, you know," he said. "Fail. Vey won't pass plumbing."

"May I?"

Col handed it up.

Wurdren held it to the last of the light in the west, the way you'd hold a coin up to see if it was clipped.

Heavy paper. Cream-colored. Far too fine for Stonefield.

A watermark in the grain. A small ship's lantern, the kind that hung from a stern rail.

He didn't know what it meant. Some papermaker somewhere with ships, he supposed.

He handed it back.

"Take it," he said.

Col looked up at him.

"Take it." He said it gently, and he meant it, and he could hear in his own voice how much he'd wanted someone to say that to him. "Somebody's seen your work. Somebody thinks it's worth something. You can wait a long time for that."

He looked out over the dark flat. The ditches.

"If the guild won't see it, let somebody else."

Col folded the letter small and put it away.

They rode the rest of the way down without talking.

---

Tamsin sent for him in the morning.

The sword was on the bench, stripped to bare blade, under the good light from the door. A glass on a stand. A file. A little dish of acid with a feather in it.

"Sit."

He sat.

"This was sold as something special," she said.

"The smith swore it was the finest steel she'd ever—"

"I know what the smith swore. I can read it." She pointed with the file at a worn mark near the guard. Beside it, a smaller stamp in old formal script. "That's a guarantee mark. That smith *guaranteed* this. Put her name to it. Said she'd answer for it."

She set the file down.

"It's ordinary steel."

Wurdren didn't say anything.

"Good ordinary steel. Decent. Any honest lowland shop would sell you the same for a fair price. But it's not what she guaranteed, and she knew it, and she stamped it anyway." She touched the flat of the blade with one finger. "That crack is thirty years of ordinary steel doing more than it was made for."

He couldn't look at the sword.

He looked at the wall instead. At the empty outline where the small punch should be.

"Now," said Tamsin. "Look at the edge."

He looked.

"*Look* at it. Thirty years. Hardly ground at all. You've honed it. Every week, I'd say, by the wear, and never once let it go dull enough to need the wheel. Fuller's clean. Not a pit of rust. Straight as the day she made it." She picked up the glass. Put it down. "That's not the steel. That's you. The steel's not special. The upkeep is."

A pause.

"That's inspected. I'd put my own mark on that."

Wurdren swallowed. "Can you mend it?"

"With ordinary steel." She made a face. "You'd want better for a tang. There isn't any. Can't get good stock in Ironcrest this spring for love or money. It's all gone to private contracts. So your weld'll be as ordinary as the rest of it." She looked at him. "That suit you?"

"Yes."

At the door, he turned back. He started to tell her how he'd come by it.

"When I—"

He heard himself. Stopped.

"Good," said Tamsin, and went back to the blade.

---

Relighting day. The whole town was out.

Names at dusk, then the fires. Before that, at noon, the judges' decision. And before *that*, mid-morning, Wurdren found Vey.

"The rule says *inspected*. By three judges." He took a breath. "Does it say where?"

Vey opened the old book. Read the rule again. Slowly.

Closed it.

"No."

"Then could we inspect it where it works?"

Vey looked at him for a long time. Then out the window, down the hill, to where Nell Haskett stood at the bottom of the guild hall steps with her arms full and her face full of purpose. She'd been standing there an hour, the porter said.

"Is this your idea," said Vey, "or hers?"

"Hers, I think. I only asked where."

---

Nell led the three judges down the lane like she'd been planning the route all winter.

Maybe she had.

Her own gate first. She made each master swing it on the hinge Col had mended. Then the Pellows' pump, which ran. The mill, where the sluice winch turned on a gear Col had cut from scrap. Four more farms. A smithy. A chapel door. At every stop she named what had broken, who'd fixed it, when, and what a new one would have cost. In a year when nobody on the flat had that kind of money.

Then she took them to the ditch below the Bracket forge.

Clear.

The water ran bright over the stones. Grass coming green on both banks.

A hundred yards on, below the next forge up the lane, it turned gray. Scum. Dead grass. That flat metal smell.

The three judges stood and looked at it for a long time.

"The Bracket forge put in one of Col's troughs before winter," Nell said. "That's all. That's the whole difference."

"Every farm on this flat," Vey said slowly, "has a page in my ledger about this ditch."

"Every farm on this flat," said Nell, "is still a farm."

She let that sit.

"Did you know that? Not one of us sold. Farms up the valley sold out this winter, when the grain went for nothing. Not us. We couldn't buy a new plow. Couldn't buy a new pump. Couldn't buy a new gate. So we had them mended." She looked right at him. "And when the grain comes back, Oswin Vey, we'll buy plowshares again. From you. From your members. At a master's rate."

Wurdren watched Vey work it through, the way Vey worked through everything. By the book. Repairs kept the farms solvent. Solvent farms bought tools. Tools paid his members.

The rates he'd fought all winter to protect had been protected all winter.

By the plumbing.

"Inspected," Vey said to the ditch. "In service."

The other two masters looked at each other. Nodded.

Both apprentices passed. Marta's blade was inspected in the hall. Col's work was inspected in the valley. The guild book recorded them on the same page.

When Vey read it out at noon, Marta walked straight across the hall and shook Col's hand. She said something only he heard. He laughed for the first time all week.

---

The charcoal carts came down the hill late in the afternoon. All four of them.

The farmers were pushing.

They'd gone up to the road that morning to stand in it. Somehow, the way news travels in Stonefield, they'd heard the judges had walked the ditch and passed the plumbing. By the time the last cart came out of the mud, they had their shoulders against its tailboard. They pushed it all the way to the guild hall.

Nobody mentioned standing in the road again.

At dusk the guild read the names.

It took most of the afternoon. Wurdren stood at the back of the long hall and listened to every one. Nobody hurried. Nobody coughed.

Then the masters carried coals from the guild hearth down the lane to every forge in Stonefield, one after another. The chimneys began to smoke. The town smelled, for the first time since autumn, like itself.

Tamsin welded the tang that night. Ordinary steel. Perfect. A new grip of plain ash. And on the flat just above the guard, beside the old guarantee mark, she struck a new mark of her own.

*Inspected.*

"Not guaranteed," she said, handing it back. "I won't answer for the steel. I'll answer for what's been done to it."

Wurdren weighed it in his hand.

A fine, ordinary sword. It balanced exactly the way it always had.

---

On the way to The Quench he passed Col's bench, where the new master's tools were laid out for the morning.

The letter was there. Cream paper, folded small, still a little damp at the edges.

Unanswered.

Col was across the lane with Marta and half the upper street, arguing happily about something. He didn't see Wurdren look.

*Take it.*

Wurdren thought about the ditch. The gates. The pumps. A gear cut from scrap.

Nobody had needed his advice after all.

He was more glad of that than he could easily have explained.

At The Quench the keeper put a bowl of forged harvest stew in front of him. Barley, beef, roots, a dark smoky broth that forge towns and farm towns both claimed to have invented.

Someone at the fire end asked about his sword.

"Mended," said Wurdren.

He ate his stew.

The keeper watched him, suspicious. Waited.

Nothing.

"Well," said the keeper. "That's a first." And gave him more stew.

---

He meant to leave the next morning.

He stayed two more days. He didn't give anyone a reason.

On the second morning, Nell Haskett came up the lane to find him.

"My gate's sticking," she said. "The other one. On the far field." She shrugged. "I'd ask Col, but he's a master now.

"Can't afford his rates."
