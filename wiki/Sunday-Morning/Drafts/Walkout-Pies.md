## Walkout Pies

By the first shift bell on the opening day of Foundry Week, there were nineteen names on Kettie Sowerby's chalkboard, and she didn't like a single one of them.

Not the people. She liked most of the people. She'd fed every one of them since they were apprentices with their voices breaking, and she knew who liked the crust thick and who picked the turnip out and who'd eat anything as long as it was hot and there was enough of it. It was the list she didn't like. The list was at the top of the board in big chalk letters, WALKOUT, underlined twice by somebody who wasn't her, and under it were nineteen names, in nineteen hands, each one an order for pies she hadn't baked yet, for a day nobody had named yet, for a picket line that didn't exist yet.

"Twenty," said Bosk Ferrin, and wrote his own name at the bottom.

"You've already got your name on it," said Kettie. "Fourth one down."

"That's for me. This is for the wife." He put the chalk back on the ledge. "She'll want one too."

"She can come and order her own."

"She's on half pay at the dye works. She can't come and do anything." He said it the way you'd say it was raining. He was a big man, Bosk, with a black beard and forearms like hams and a voice that could get across a casting floor with the hammers going. He'd been in the crews since he was twelve. He wasn't a bad man. Kettie had never thought he was a bad man. He was just very, very sure about things.

Behind him the gate was full of the morning shift going in and the night shift coming out, everybody stopping at the stall for a pie. Smoked meat and root, wrapped in a cloth, hot. Kettie had been baking since three. She had another forty in the oven and she'd sell every one by the second bell, the same as every morning, except that this was Foundry Week and she'd sell them twice as fast.

"Nobody pours hungry," somebody said at the gate, going in.

"Nobody pours hungry," said somebody else.

It was what you said, going in. It was what the crews had said at the foundry gate at Coalglen Meadows for as long as there'd been a foundry, which was longer than anybody could remember there being meadows. There weren't any meadows. It was a narrow valley full of smoke and water wheels and slag, and the only green thing in it was the moss on the north side of the chimneys. But it was called Coalglen Meadows, and nobody poured hungry, and both of those were true.

"Morning, Kettie," said Foreman Lusk, holding out his coin.

"It's not a good morning, Lusk. Your lot have had my lot on short hours since the frost."

"They're not my lot."

"You work for them."

"I work for them," Lusk agreed, and took his pie. He was a mild gray man who'd come up through the crews himself, and everybody knew he hated being foreman and was too good at it to stop. "Doesn't make them mine."

"Charcoal carts are short again," somebody said behind him. "Three days now."

"Charcoal carts are always short," said somebody else.

"Not like this."

Kettie turned back to the oven. Her box was on the shelf behind her, where it always was: an old tin cash box with a hasp, heavy. She touched the lid as she passed it, the way she did twenty times a morning. It was lighter than it had been at the frost. It was a good deal lighter than it had been at the frost. She knew to the copper what was in it, and she didn't like that either.

---

The crews' tavern was emptier than it should have been on the first night of Foundry Week, because short hours make empty taverns. But it was full enough. Kettie went because she always went the first night, and because Bosk had asked her to, and because she wanted to hear what was being said with her own ears and not get it secondhand off the gate in the morning.

What was being said was carters.

"Twelve of them," Bosk said, standing up by the fire with his cup. "Twelve teams. Up from the low valleys. They came in this afternoon and they're camped on the slag road, and nobody'll say what for."

"Charcoal," somebody said.

"Charcoal." Bosk laughed, not nicely. "Twelve teams for charcoal, in Foundry Week. Ask yourself what else you'd want twelve strong men for in Foundry Week."

The tavern went quiet.

"They're going to pour without us," said a young man by the door. "That's it, isn't it."

"That's what I'd do," said Bosk. "If I was them. If I'd had a crew on short hours since the frost and I knew they were talking. I'd bring in carters and teach them to pour, and if the crew walked, I'd have the carters there to step in." He drank. "So we don't walk out after. We walk out at the pour. On the day. At the long table. We don't eat, we don't pour, and that casting's sand. Then let them try it with carters."

There was a noise in the room that wasn't quite a cheer.

"No," said Nadia Pryor.

She was sitting in the corner with her pipe. She'd been pour-master at the great pour for as long as Kettie had been selling pies, and she was old now, gray and bent, with burn scars up both arms like a map of somewhere nobody would want to go.

"That casting's the year's order," she said. "Half the valley's work next winter is in that sand. You ruin the pour, you don't hurt the owners. The owners've got money. You hurt us."

"We're hurt already," said Bosk.

"Then don't make it worse."

It went round the room like a draft under a door. The old hands sitting with Nadia and the young ones standing with Bosk, and the ones in the middle looking at their cups.

"Greenvale's not buying," somebody said, to nobody, into the gap. "That's what it is. Half the farm villages over there have stopped taking tools. Some kind of boycott. That's why the orders went."

"And the Slatehallow lad's hiring," said the young man by the door. "Up the valley. Full pay. Some patron or other."

"Then go and work for the Slatehallow lad," said Nadia, "and see how long that lasts."

Kettie went home before the end, because she had to be up at three, and lay in bed with her eyes open thinking about twelve carters on the slag road with nothing but what they'd brought with them, and about her box.

---

On the third day of Foundry Week the list ran off the bottom of the chalkboard.

Somebody had started a second column down the side to fit the names in, and when that ran out somebody else had started writing them small, sideways, along the bottom edge. Kettie stood in front of it at first light with a cloth in her hand and didn't wipe any of it off.

Fifty-eight names. Fifty-eight orders for walkout pies.

"They're not here to replace anybody," said Foreman Lusk at the gate. He said it to the gate in general, loudly, holding his pie. "The carters. I've told you. They're not."

"What are they here for, then?" somebody shouted.

Lusk opened his mouth, and shut it again. "Hauling," he said.

"Hauling what?"

"Whatever needs hauling."

The gate laughed at him, not kindly. Lusk took his pie and went in. Kettie watched him go and thought: he doesn't know. They haven't told him. He's the foreman and they haven't told him, and he's out here telling everybody it's fine because he wants it to be.

Then Bosk Ferrin was at the stall.

"Kettie," he said. Not loud. For once. "The box."

The gate went quiet. The ones going in stopped going in, and the ones coming out stopped coming out.

"What about it?"

"It's the crews' box. It's the crews' money. It's what we put by, every week, for when the hours go." He put his big hands on the counter. "The hours have gone. We're walking out. We'll need it."

Kettie looked at him. Then she reached back and laid a hand on the lid of the box, without looking round.

She'd kept that box for nine years. She'd paid out of it for broken legs and burned hands and funerals, and all spring she'd been paying out of it for short hours, a bit here and a bit there, to whoever's children had gone thin in the face. Nobody had ever asked her for it all at once.

"When you walk out," she said, "come and ask me."

"I'm asking you now."

"You're not walking out now. You're standing at my stall with flour on your elbow." She slid a pie across to him. "Eat that."

He didn't. He looked at it, and then at her, and then he turned round and went in through the gate, and the gate started moving again.

The pie sat on the counter. After a while Kettie sold it to somebody else.

---

On the fourth day a young woman turned up at the stall in a road-dusty coat with a bladesmith's leather apron rolled under one arm, and asked for a pie and directions, in that order.

"I've come to see the pour," she said. She had a Stonefield way of talking, quick and flat. "I'm Kerra. Kerra Dunnock. I passed my mastership at Forge Reawakening." She said it as if it still surprised her. "They said at home there was nothing like the great pour at Coalglen. So I came."

"There isn't," said Kettie. "Nothing like it."

"Can I see it? The molds?"

Kettie looked at the gate. It was mid-morning, between shifts. She wiped her hands and took Kerra round to the side, where there was a crack in the wall you could see the casting floor through if you knew where to put your eye.

There it was. The pit. The great sand mold in the middle of the floor, bigger than a house, dug and rammed and shaped over weeks, the cores set in it, the runners cut, the whole thing waiting. And above it, up on the far wall, the furnaces, banked and roaring, filling all week.

Kerra kept her eye to the crack and didn't move.

"How many pour it?"

"Forty. All at once. Ladles and the long channel from the furnace. You've got to do it in one go or the metal goes cold in the sand, and then it's cracks, and then it's nothing." Kettie heard her own voice and was surprised how proud it sounded. "Nadia Pryor's pour-master. She's done it thirty-odd times. Never lost one."

"What's it for?"

"Rollers. For a mill somewhere. Half the valley's winter's in that sand."

Kerra stepped back from the crack. Her eyes went from Kettie to the gate to the chalkboard, which she could see from there, with its fifty-eight names going down the side and along the bottom.

"At home," she said, "in the spring, there was talk like that. Short hours. Everybody saying strike."

"What happened?"

"Nothing yet. They're still saying it."

"It's not my strike," said Kettie.

"No," said Kerra. "I don't suppose it is." She ate her pie. "This is very good."

"I know," said Kettie.

---

On the fifth night she baked alone.

She always did, the night before the pour. She'd bake for the long table, a pie for every one of the forty pour crew and a few over, and she'd bake them in the dark of the kitchen behind her stall with the oven door open and the whole room ticking with heat.

But this year she was baking for the list as well. Fifty-eight walkout pies. Because tomorrow at the long table the crew wasn't going to eat, and the casting was going to be sand, and then there'd be a picket line at the gate by noon, and fifty-eight people standing on it hungry, and Kettie Sowerby had never in her life let fifty-eight people stand anywhere hungry.

So she baked. Meat and root, onion, a bit of the good lard. The kitchen filled up with it. She put a tray in, and took a tray out, and put the next tray in. At some point she stopped thinking and just baked, the way you do when your hands know the job better than your head. The oven ticked. The street outside went quiet, and then went quieter, and then it was just her, and the pies, and the heat, and the smell.

At the end of it she stood with her back against the table and looked at them. A hundred pies, give or take. Forty for the long table. The rest for the line.

Out of the window, down the valley, she could see the carters' fires on the slag road. Small fires. Not much on them.

She stood there a long time.

"Kettie Sowerby," she said out loud, to the kitchen, "you are going to regret this."

Then she got the big baskets down off the hooks.

---

The carters were sitting round three small fires on the slag road with their teams picketed behind them, and when Kettie came down the road with two baskets on each arm, they all stood up at once, like men expecting to be told to move on.

"I've got pies," she said. "They're getting cold."

They looked at her, and then round at one another.

"We've got no money for pies," said the eldest of them. He was a gray, square man with a carter's shoulders and a face that had stopped expecting anything.

"I didn't ask for money. I asked if you wanted them."

He looked at the baskets. Kettie had covered them with cloths but the smell was coming out round the edges, and she watched the smell get to him, and to all of them.

"The day we came in," he said, "somebody chucked a stone at the lead cart from behind a wall. Missed. Called us scabs. We've been called scabs every day since."

"Well," said Kettie, "I'm not throwing pies at you. Sit down."

They sat down. She handed round the pies. The carters ate like men who've had nothing hot in five days: fast, and then slower, and then very slowly, as if they'd just remembered what food was for. The eldest one was called Embry, and he ate two, and somewhere in the second one he started talking.

They were from the low valleys, south. Their own works had gone on short hours in the spring, same as here. There was no hauling to be had at home because nobody was buying anything to haul. Then a man had come down with a contract: twelve teams, to Coalglen, for Foundry Week, hauling charcoal. The charcoal carts from the forest border had been short all summer, he'd said, something about the roads, and the foundry would pay well for anybody who'd fetch it.

"Charcoal," said Kettie.

"Charcoal," said Embry. "Nobody told us your crews were talking walkout. Nobody told us anything. We came up the road and got called names for it." He looked into the fire. "I've got a crew at home, missus. Same as yours. Same short hours. I'd not take another man's place. I'd not know how. I've never poured iron in my life."

She stood up.

"Long table's at first light," she said. "Outside the gate. I'll have pies."

"We're not welcome at your long table."

"No," said Kettie. "I don't suppose you are. Come anyway."

---

The long table was outside the foundry gate, a great slab of oak on trestles, and on the morning of the great pour the whole pour crew sat down at it before first light, forty of them, the way they always had. Nadia Pryor at the head. Bosk halfway down. Everybody in their leathers, scrubbed, quiet.

Kettie came out of her kitchen with the first basket, and behind her, in a line, came twelve carters.

It went off like a furnace door opening.

"*Out*," said somebody.

"What are *they* doing here?"

"Kettie, what in the—"

Bosk Ferrin stood up. He stood up so fast his end of the bench went over, and the men either side of him went with it.

"Whose places were you promised?" he said, straight across the table at Embry. "Go on. Whose? Mine? His?"

And Embry, who'd been called a scab every day for five days, and had five days of nothing hot, and no reason in the world to be polite, said it straight back.

"Nobody's. We were hired to haul charcoal, you great ox, because your charcoal carts have been short all summer. Ask your foreman. Ask anybody who's looked. We're on short hours where we come from, the same as you, and we've been called scabs all week by men who wouldn't come near enough to ask us a single question." He leaned on the table. "Well, you're near enough now. Ask."

Bosk asked.

It went on for a while. It was loud. It was very loud. Half the street came out to see what the shouting was. Bosk shouted, and Embry shouted back, and then two of the younger crew started in, and then two of the carters, and Nadia Pryor sat at the head of the table with her pipe and let them, and every so often said "Sit *down*, Bosk," in the voice she used on the casting floor, and every so often Bosk did.

Partway through, Foreman Lusk came out of the gate to see what the noise was, and Bosk swung round on him and said, "Charcoal? Is it charcoal?" and Lusk said, "How should I know? Nobody tells me anything," with such feeling that half the table laughed despite itself.

And through all of it the pies sat on the table between them. Kettie had put them down. Forty for the crew and twelve more for the carters. She just kept putting them down.

By the time the sun was properly up, the shouting had run out. The carters were still down at their end. The crew was still at theirs. Nobody had said sorry. Nobody looked like they were going to.

But nobody thought the carters were there to pour, either. Not anymore.

Bosk was standing at his place with his fists on the table. In front of him was a pie.

If Bosk didn't eat, there were twenty men down that table who wouldn't either.

Kettie stood behind him with the empty basket.

She had a great deal to say. She'd always had a great deal to say. She'd told Bosk Ferrin what she thought of him since he was twelve years old with his voice breaking, and he'd never once listened, and she'd never once stopped.

She didn't say anything.

She stood there. The whole table waited. Nadia Pryor took her pipe out of her mouth.

Bosk looked at the pie. The pie, being a pie, waited.

Then he sat down, and picked it up, and ate it.

---

The great pour went at noon.

Kettie saw what she could of it through the crack in the wall, with Kerra Dunnock's elbow in her ribs. Mostly she heard it. The whole valley heard it: the roar when they opened the furnace, and the bell, and then the long hiss as forty ladles and the long channel all went at once, and then a silence that went on so long she thought something had gone wrong, and then the crew cheering, and the cheer going out through the gate and along the rows and into the town.

They broke the sand at dusk. The casting came out of it whole. Nadia Pryor walked round it twice with a lamp and a hammer, tapping, listening, and then she put the hammer down and said, "That'll do," which from Nadia was as good as a song.

When the crew came out of the gate, Kettie was at her stall with the box on the counter.

Bosk came over. He was black from head to foot and grinning. Then he saw the box, and stopped grinning.

"It's yours," said Kettie. "It's the crews'. It always was." She pushed it across the counter to him. "You'll want it."

"Kettie—"

"You'll want it," she said again. "You're still walking out, aren't you?"

He looked at her. Then he looked down the street at the owners' house, at the end, with its lamps lit.

"The morning after the Week," he said. "With notice. Proper notice. On paper. So they've got time to answer." He put his hand on the box. "And if they don't, we go."

"Then you'll want it."

He took it in both arms like a child and carried it up the street.

Kettie got her cloth and went to the chalkboard. Fifty-eight names down the side and along the bottom. She wiped them off, all of them, until the board was black and empty.

Then she picked up the chalk.

*Walkout pies*, she wrote. *Morning after the Week. Bosk Ferrin. All of them.*
