# Craft

## Status

**Writing reference, not setting canon.** This page owns how a Sunday Morning story is *told* and how its prose should *sound*:

- the storytelling principles, adapted from the author's Pathwell notes;
- how to use the author's [voice guide](Sources/Voice-Guide.md);
- who writes what, AI or the author;
- The author's own watch-list of habits;
- the scene diagnostic run on every draft.

Scene-level texture (what the world was doing yesterday) is in [Worldbuilding Breath](../../Reference/Worldbuilding-Breath.md). Tone and stakes belong to the [Framework](Sources/Framework.md) and the [tone guardrails](Rules.md#tone-guardrails). Where craft and tone pull apart, tone wins; see the [order of authority](README.md#order-of-authority).

## Telling

**Source:** [BigCatMellow-Archive/Pathwell](https://github.com/BigCatMellow-Archive/Pathwell) at commit `085fd77` (2026-09-18), read 2026-09-28 ([D10](Decisions.md)): `Writing-Principles.md`, `Quick-Diagnostic.md`, `Story/Story_Files/forbidden_patterns.md`, `Story/Story_Files/pathwell_prose_voice.md` and `Story/insights/INS-0001-unpaid-plot-debts-must-be-paid-on-page.md`. Provenance: [Source Register](../../Reference/Source-Register.md) §11.

Pathwell is a literary urban fantasy novel, written for a longer, darker book, so each principle is marked **carries over**, **adapted** or **in tension**.

### 1. Write it like it's the sequel (carries over)

"The sequel assumes you were there." Characters don't explain their world merely for the reader. They talk from lived familiarity, and a thing is explained only when the character, relationship or situation would naturally bring it up.

In Two Sons, festivals, guilds, grammar habits, credit and ledgers are ordinary to the people who live with them. The first drafts broke this: a narrator explained Ironcrest's four kinds of *done* and Northwind's two *we*s. Later passes moved those explanations into what characters do and say. The Heavy Scale is the model. Tove's evidential piles show up in how she talks long before anyone could name them.

**Tension with the Framework, resolved: withhold lore, never clarity.** The Framework's attention test (§13) asks for "minimal dependence on hidden lore" and "understandable geography"; the sequel principle asks us to withhold. The reader may not know why the Salve house hangs vines, or what a Silent Whisper observer believes. They must always know who wants what, where everyone is standing, and what just happened. The voice guide agrees: "The reader should sometimes lack information. That is different from lacking clarity."

### 2. Therefore, but; never "and then" (carries over)

Scenes connect through consequence, complication, decision or relationship pressure. "And then" is the warning sign. In Sunday Morning, social pressure counts as much as plot. Bettany's anger at Pell is a *but*; Hobb's slate is a *therefore*.

### 3. Every scene changes something, or makes a later change matter (adapted)

A scene must cause something, change a relationship, knowledge or expectation, make a later choice believable, or give recovery after a big event.

**Adapted:** Framework §12 says quiet scenes "are not filler if they deepen attachment to the world", and Pathwell's principle 11 agrees ("breathe when the story needs possession"). **Enjoying the characters is a job a scene can do.** The Tree's Canopy Vigil changes almost nothing in the plot and is one of the best scenes in the collection.

**Deletion test:** "If this scene is removed, ______ becomes weaker." If nobody can fill the blank, not even with "the reader's pleasure in these people", cut or merge.

### 4. Show the iceberg's tip; let the world exceed the scene (carries over)

Know more than the reader needs. Depth comes from characters treating their world as real, not from mystery decoration. "One unexplained detail can help. Ten can become noise. Zero can be correct." Pathwell's Quick Diagnostic asks for one unexplained detail per chapter, but its later Writing Principles overrule that: **there is no quota.** Nobody in Port knows why it's called Three Moon, and that's enough.

### 5. The body may know before the mind (adapted)

A signature Pathwell move (the sugar-cookie scene), but one route into feeling, not a compulsory beat. People also suppress, mislabel, or react through habit and expertise. Quill notices beams before faces; Aldo reads a crack like a stave. Use the response that fits the person.

### 6. Characters want things, often more than one (carries over)

Every important person in a scene has some pressure shaping them: a preference, a fear, an obligation, face to protect, wanting to be useful. Mardin needs the file closed *and* can't lose it. Vey protects his members' rates *and* needs the charcoal. Wants can be social, not only practical.

### 7. Pressure the character's self-story (carries over)

Don't give every character one neat "Lie". Ask what story this person tells about themselves, what keeps that story working, and what this situation makes hard to ignore. Pell is retired. Aldo is a guest. Wurdren is someone whose career didn't matter.

### 8. Don't explain the metaphor before performing it (carries over)

If an image, ritual or recurrence already carries the meaning, don't translate it. The two halves of the banner don't need a sentence about unity.

### 9. Incomplete answers need a human reason (carries over)

Evasion builds worlds only when it comes from a person: they don't know, they refuse, they're protecting face or someone else, they misunderstand, or they want something else from the exchange. Mother Seral answers with food questions because the practice was never in words, not to make a mystery.

### 10. Place is functionally specific (carries over)

A place matters through what people can do, notice, know or lose there: procedure, expertise, material limits, routine, who belongs, who controls access. The arbitration hall's tea stall, the customs yard scale and the mill loft all work because of what happens in them.

### 11. Subtext must be recoverable (carries over)

Know what is said, what is meant, what can be inferred, what each person knows and wants, and what social action the line performs. Sometimes direct speech is strongest.

### 12. Promise, progress, payoff (carries over)

The opening promises an *experience*, not only plot facts. The middle changes or deepens what the reader is waiting for. The ending fulfils, transforms or deliberately leaves open (The Goat File's mothers, [D7](Decisions.md)). The story pages' promise ledgers already do this.

### 13. Theme through action, not speeches (carries over)

Author-side questions ("Whose decision is this?", "What makes something irreplaceable?") are planning tools. They should rarely become dialogue.

### 14. Earn the big moments; memory before loss (carries over, and very Sunday Morning)

"What has the reader actually experienced that makes this matter?" If a big moment fails, inspect what came before it rather than making it louder.

"Before we destroy something important, let it be ordinary for a while." In Sunday Morning the losses are small (a race, a bell, a family's old stall), so the "having" can be small too. The Kittiwake's crew is seen at the racks before the race. The stew is tasted three ways before it's defined.

### 15. Pay your debts on the page (carries over)

From Pathwell's INS-0001: if a scene creates an emotional or causal debt, let the reader feel its cost before moving on. One beat is enough: an acknowledgment, an aftermath, a small cost felt in the body. **Adapted:** in Sunday Morning the payment is usually warm, not heavy. Tove's coins go to the supper.

### 16. Humor is social action (carries over)

Humor creates or changes affiliation, permission, status, intimacy, avoidance, embarrassment, rivalry and repair. "Comedy needs a real serious side, not a mandatory waiting period." The situation must be real enough for the wrong response to be funny. This matches the Framework's "absurdity treated sincerely" (§8).

### 17. Read it aloud (carries over)

Listen for cadence, character voice, comic timing, awkward exposition, repeated sentence shapes, and dialogue no human mouth would carry. "A sentence sounding good is not proof that it belongs."

## Voice

**Sources.** The author's [voice guide](Sources/Voice-Guide.md), verbatim, is the authority on how sentences sound. Notes from two of his sample chapters (a Pathwell and Elizabeth story, shared 2026-09-27 and not stored here) are secondary evidence and give way wherever the two disagree.

**Use the guide as a sensibility, not a checklist** ([D6](Decisions.md)). Each story takes only what it already wants from it. A checklist of beats per story (a sad beat, a choice, a dropped joke, a callback) makes a collection predictable ([History](History.md#timeline), step 11).

**Rhythm.** The first voice notes, read from the samples alone, overweighted fragments and one-line paragraphs. The guide is explicit:

- **Ordinary movement:** mostly clean, medium-length sentences.
- **Fragments:** for danger and comic timing, not the default.
- **Reflection and emotion:** longer sentences that carry accumulation and memory.
- **Avoid** a manuscript made entirely of rapid fragments, and every scene ending on a joke.

**Sample-chapter habits that still hold,** because the guide agrees with them: starting in motion; dialogue carrying scenes; humor from character contrast and mundane worries; feeling through smell and taste; grief carried by objects; callbacks; a turn on the last line.

**Narration with an opinion, reconciled.** Pathwell's `forbidden_patterns.md` warns that the author's narration can go invisible ("half the wit lives in the narration's asides", of Pratchett). The guide warns against "self-consciously clever narration". Both hold: **narration may have attitude, a point of view that notices and judges, but it shouldn't compete with the characters for laughs.** Earlier passes cut narrator jokes to about one per story. That was a fair correction for AI-drafted prose, not a rule that narration has no voice. When the author writes over these drafts, the narration's attitude is his.

### Write how people talk

The author's own strength, in his words: people told him he "wrote how people talked", in narration as much as in dialogue ([D14](Decisions.md)). His example of the failure: "He meant to walk straight to the mill. He did not manage it." Nobody says "he did not manage it", so it sits on the page. Said the way a person would: "He didn't get very far."

- **The test.** Would somebody telling this story out loud, to a friend, say the sentence this way? If it sounds like writing, rewrite it (Elmore Leonard: "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it.").
- **The narrator borrows the character's words.** Close third person soaks up the idiom of whoever it's following: what Pell would call a thing, how Aldo would size up a crack. This is free indirect style, and critic Hugh Kenner named its word-level form the "Uncle Charles principle", after Joyce. It gives the narration a person's voice without a speech.
- **Contractions by default,** in narration and dialogue. Full forms ("did not", "I am not") only when someone is insisting, or as a character's formal register, such as Idris's courtyard voice.
- **Say it the everyday way.** "A lot of" over "a great deal of" (unless it's that character's phrase); "turned up" over "appeared"; "found out" over "discovered". Drop throat-clearing like "found that he", "in the particular way that", "for once". Keep the precise word when the character would use it.
- **Not folksy, not modern.** No phonetic dialect and no stacked idioms (Leonard: dialect sparingly). No modern slang that breaks the world. The goal is a person talking, not a performance of one.
- **Different people, different talk.** Borrowing the viewpoint character's words keeps the stories from sounding like one narrator: One Square should sound like Pell's Vale, The Greenvale Man like Aldo's cove.

## AI and the author: who writes what

The guide sets the division of labor ("AI Collaboration Guidance"), and it applies to every pass:

- **AI leads on** continuity, structure, motivation, pacing, action clarity, repeated beats and thematic consistency. The strongest AI contributions so far were structural: plans, ledgers, continuity and the uniqueness matrix.
- **AI is cautious with** jokes, emotional language, philosophical statements, character-defining dialogue, metaphors, narrator commentary and final sentence rhythm. Lines in these categories are scaffolding for the author and should be flagged for his review.
- **A voice problem routes to DO if it's a line, and to the author if it's taste** ([routing](Pipeline.md#stage-3--do-draft)).
- **The drafts are scaffolds for the author to write over,** not a substitute voice. Taste, humor and voice are his call, which is why L4 requires his reading ([Pipeline](Pipeline.md#stage-4--judge-review-independently)).

## The author's watch-list

From Pathwell's `forbidden_patterns.md`, which records habits flagged across that project, not generic advice. AI drafting shares several of them.

| Habit | Catch it | Status here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exposition disguised as dialogue** | Is one character telling another something they'd both already know, for the reader's benefit? | Watch. The main risk in world-heavy stories. |
| **The docent:** a character explaining how the world works to someone who just experienced it | Any passage that narrates the rules | Watch: Ismet on pledge conventions, Quill on stones. Keep each to one or two lines. |
| **Filtering** ("she saw", "he felt", "she heard", "noticed", "watched") | Search for perception verbs, cut the filter, render the thing | The checker counts these per story. |
| **Body-part cataloging** | Three body parts in one sentence: cut to one, make it specific | Watch |
| **Metronome narration:** sing-song, same-shape sentences between lively dialogue | Read the narration aloud separately | Watch; see the rhythm rules above |
| **Narration without an opinion** | Is narration only stage direction? | In tension; reconciled under Voice |
| **Telling what the scene just showed** | After an image does the work, delete the sentence naming the feeling | The most common remaining fault. Prefer cuts. |
| **Adding volume when uncertain** | If a passage feels off, cut before you add | Applies to revision passes too |
| **Pulling on resolved threads** | Build on locked decisions; don't re-derive them | Check [Decisions](Decisions.md) first |
| **Confirm new elements before building on them** | Don't treat an unconfirmed detail as established | Matches the [promotion rule](Rules.md#promotion-rule) |

## The one-line test

From Pathwell, before keeping any paragraph:

> *Did I explain something the reader could feel? Did a thing move on its own that a person should have chosen? Could this be shorter?*

And the guide's revision rule: clarify first, strengthen second, embellish last. Prefer what sounds like the story over what sounds like good writing.

## Sunday Morning scene diagnostic

Pathwell's five-question Quick Diagnostic, adapted. Run it on every scene of a draft.

1. **What does the viewpoint character want in this scene?** "Nothing" or "to understand what's happening" means passive. Give them a want, even a small one.
2. **Therefore, but, or and then?** Fix "and then".
3. **What changed from top to bottom?** A relationship, knowledge, a plan, or the reader's attachment. If nothing did, cut or merge.
4. **Did anyone explain what the story already showed?** Cut it.
5. **Would a reader brace here?** If so, add warmth, absurdity or a meal nearby ([tone guardrails](Rules.md#tone-guardrails)).
