## Order of authority

1. **The Framework** decides what the story is for and how it should feel: "You do not need to brace yourself."
2. **These principles** decide how the story is told: causality, exposition, subtext, earning.
3. **Voice** decides how the sentences sound.
4. **The Registry** keeps the collection from repeating itself.

## The principles

### 1. Write it like it's the sequel — carries over

"The sequel assumes you were there." Characters don't explain their world merely for the reader. They talk from lived familiarity, and a thing is explained only when the character, relationship or situation would naturally bring it up.

In Two Sons: festivals, guilds, grammar habits, credit and ledgers are ordinary to the people who live with them. The first drafts broke this. A narrator explained Ironcrest's four kinds of *done* and Northwind's two *we*s. Later passes moved those explanations into what characters do and say. The Heavy Scale is the model: Tove's evidential piles show up in how she talks long before anyone could name them.

**Tension with the Framework, resolved.** The Framework's attention test (§13) asks for "minimal dependence on hidden lore" and "understandable geography". The sequel principle asks us to withhold. Both hold if you **withhold lore, never clarity**. The reader may not know why the Salve house hangs vines or what a Silent Whisper observer believes. They must always know who wants what, where everyone is standing, and what just happened. (The voice guide says the same thing: "The reader should sometimes lack information. That is different from lacking clarity.")

### 2. Therefore, but — never "and then" — carries over

Scenes connect through consequence, complication, decision or relationship pressure. "And then" is the warning sign. For Sunday Morning, social pressure counts as much as plot. Bettany's anger at Pell is a *but*. Hobb's slate is a *therefore*.

### 3. Every scene changes something, or makes a later change matter — adapted

A scene must cause something, change a relationship, knowledge or expectation, make a later choice believable, or give recovery after a big event.

**Adapted:** Framework §12 says quiet scenes "are not filler if they deepen attachment to the world", and Pathwell's own principle 11 agrees ("breathe when the story needs possession"). In a Sunday Morning story, **enjoying the characters is a job a scene can do.** The Tree's Canopy Vigil changes almost nothing in the plot and is one of the best scenes in the collection.

**Deletion test:** "If this scene is removed, ______ becomes weaker." If nobody can fill the blank, not even with "the reader's pleasure in these people", cut or merge.

### 4. Show the iceberg's tip; let the world exceed the scene — carries over

Know more than the reader needs. Depth comes from characters treating their world as real, not from mystery decoration. "One unexplained detail can help. Ten can become noise. Zero can be correct."

Pathwell's Quick Diagnostic says to add one unexplained detail per chapter. Its later Writing Principles overrule that: **there is no quota.** Follow the later rule. Nobody in Port knows why it's called Three Moon, and that's enough.

### 5. The body may know before the mind — adapted

In Pathwell this is a signature move (the sugar-cookie scene). It is one route into feeling, not a compulsory beat. People also suppress, mislabel, or react through habit and expertise. Quill notices beams before faces; Aldo reads a crack like a stave. Use the response that fits the person.

### 6. Characters want things, often more than one — carries over

Every important person in a scene has some pressure shaping them: a preference, a fear, an obligation, face to protect, wanting to be useful. Mardin needs the file closed *and* can't lose it. Vey protects his members' rates *and* needs the charcoal. Wants can be social, not practical.

### 7. Pressure the character's self-story — carries over

Don't give every character one neat "Lie". Ask:
- What story does this person tell about themselves?
- What keeps that story working?
- What does this situation make hard to ignore?

Pell is retired. Aldo is a guest. Wurdren is someone whose career didn't matter.

### 8. Don't explain the metaphor before performing it — carries over

If an image, ritual or recurrence already carries the meaning, don't translate it for the reader. The two halves of the banner don't need a sentence about unity.

### 9. Incomplete answers need a human reason — carries over

Evasion builds worlds only when it comes from a person. They don't know, they refuse, they're protecting face or someone else, they misunderstand, or they want something else from the exchange. Mother Seral answers with food questions because the practice was never in words, not to make a mystery.

### 10. Place is functionally specific — carries over

A place matters through what people can do, notice, know or lose there: procedure, expertise, material limits, routine, who belongs, who controls access. The arbitration hall's tea stall, the customs yard scale and the mill loft all work because of what happens in them.

### 11. Subtext must be recoverable — carries over

Know what is said, what is meant, what can be inferred, what each person knows and wants, and what social action the line performs. Sometimes direct speech is strongest.

### 12. Promise, progress, payoff — carries over

The opening promises an *experience*, not only plot facts. The middle changes or deepens what the reader is waiting for. The ending fulfils, transforms or deliberately leaves open (The Goat File's mothers). The story pages' promise ledgers already do this.

### 13. Theme through action, not speeches — carries over

Author-side questions ("Whose decision is this?", "What makes something irreplaceable?") are planning tools. They should rarely become dialogue.

### 14. Earn the big moments; memory before loss — carries over, and very Sunday Morning

"What has the reader actually experienced that makes this matter?" If a big moment fails, inspect what came before it rather than making it louder.

**Memory-before-loss rule:** "Before we destroy something important, let it be ordinary for a while." In a Sunday Morning story the losses are small (a race, a bell, a family's old stall), so the "having" can be small too. The Kittiwake's crew is seen at the racks before the race. The stew is tasted three ways before it's defined.

### 15. Pay your debts on the page — carries over

From Pathwell's insight INS-0001: if a scene creates an emotional or causal debt, let the reader feel its cost before moving on. One beat is enough: an acknowledgment, an aftermath, a small cost felt in the body. **Adapted:** in Sunday Morning the payment is usually warm, not heavy. Tove's coins go to the supper.

### 16. Humor is social action — carries over

Humor creates or changes affiliation, permission, status, intimacy, avoidance, embarrassment, rivalry and repair. From Pathwell: "Comedy needs a real serious side, not a mandatory waiting period." The situation must be real enough for the wrong response to be funny. This matches the Framework's "absurdity treated sincerely" (§8).

### 17. Read it aloud — carries over

Listen for cadence, character voice, comic timing, awkward exposition, repeated sentence shapes, and dialogue no human mouth would carry. "A sentence sounding good is not proof that it belongs."

## James's own watch-list

These come from Pathwell's `forbidden_patterns.md`, which records habits flagged across that project, not generic advice. AI drafting shares several of them.

| Habit | Catch it | Status here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exposition disguised as dialogue** | Is one character telling another something they'd both already know, for the reader's benefit? | Watch. The main risk in world-heavy stories. |
| **The docent:** a character explaining how the world works to someone who just experienced it | Any passage that narrates the rules | Watch: Ismet on pledge conventions, Quill on stones. Keep each to one or two lines. |
| **Filtering** ("she saw", "he felt", "she heard", "noticed", "watched") | Search for perception verbs, cut the filter, render the thing | The checker counts these per story. |
| **Body-part cataloging** | Three body parts in one sentence: cut to one, make it specific | Watch |
| **Metronome narration**: sing-song, same-shape sentences between lively dialogue | Read the narration aloud separately | Watch; ties to Voice's rhythm rules |
| **Narration without an opinion** | Is narration only stage direction? | **In tension**, see below |
| **Telling what the scene just showed** | After an image does the work, delete the sentence naming the feeling | Cut several times already ("understood she'd been answering all along") |
| **Adding volume when uncertain** | If a passage feels off, cut before you add | Applies to revision passes too |
| **Pulling on resolved threads** | Build on locked decisions; don't re-derive them | Respect recorded decisions (the mothers stay open) |
| **Confirm new elements before building on them** | Don't treat an unconfirmed detail as established | Matches the promotion rule |

**Narration with an opinion, reconciled.** Pathwell's `forbidden_patterns.md` warns that James's narration can go invisible, and that in Pratchett "half the wit lives in the narration's asides". His voice guide warns against "self-consciously clever narration". Both are true. **Narration may have attitude, a point of view that notices and judges, but it shouldn't compete with the characters for laughs.** Earlier passes here cut narrator jokes down to about one per story. That was a fair correction for AI-drafted prose, but it shouldn't become a rule that narration has no voice at all. When James writes over these drafts, the narration's attitude is his.

## The one-line test

From Pathwell, before keeping any paragraph:

> *Did I explain something the reader could feel? Did a thing move on its own that a person should have chosen? Could this be shorter?*

## Sunday Morning scene diagnostic

Pathwell's five-question Quick Diagnostic, adapted. Run it on each scene of a draft.

1. **What does the viewpoint character want in this scene?** "Nothing" or "to understand what's happening" means passive. Give them a want, even a small one.
2. **Therefore, but, or and then?** Fix "and then".
3. **What changed from top to bottom?** It could be a relationship, knowledge, a plan, or the reader's attachment. If nothing did, cut or merge.
4. **Did anyone explain what the story already showed?** Cut it.
5. **Would a reader brace here?** If so, add warmth, absurdity or a meal nearby. *(Sunday Morning addition, from the [guardrails](Collection-Registry.md#sunday-morning-guardrails).)*
