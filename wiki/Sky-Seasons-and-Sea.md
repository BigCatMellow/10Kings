## The suns

*Lean:* **a close pair.** A bright golden sun and a smaller, redder one circle each other every few weeks, and the world circles both. Real planets like this exist; Kepler-16b is one. The pair is never more than a few hand-widths apart in the sky, so it warms the world like one sun and gives ordinary seasons. Day to day, it looks like this:

- **Two shadows.** Everything casts a sharp shadow and a fainter, reddish one, which close into one when the suns line up.
- **A second dusk.** On some days the red sun sets after the gold one, and evening ends in red light. On others it rises first.
- **The crossing.** Twice in each turn of the pair, one sun passes in front of the other and the daylight dims for an hour or two. It's the most regular thing in the sky after the day itself. That makes it a candidate for the calendar's open "month" or "week" if bt 7274 wants one; it is not settled.
- **Auroras farther south.** The small red sun is restless, and its flares light auroras farther south than Earth's. That is why Northwind sees Aurora Nights as far south as it does.

The other option is a distant second sun. It would sit in the day sky half the year and the night sky the other half, and would bring a season of bright nights. That would change farming, calendars and faith everywhere, and nothing in the lore shows it.

## The year and the light

*Lean:*
- the world's tilt is like Earth's, which gives the solstices the stories use;
- the year is about as long as ours, so the festival rhythm works as written (the exact count of days stays [open](Calendar-and-Timekeeping.md#open));
- the known world runs from about **56° north** at Northwind's coast to about **44°** at the Sunplains tip.

That span asks for a map scale of about 1.8 miles to the draft's pixel ([Realism Check](Reference/Realism-Check-2026-11.md#1-the-maps-scale)). It's what gives Northwind its auroras and Sunplains a Mediterranean summer.

| | Gullhaven, north coast (~56°) | Port (~49°) | Solenne, south tip (~44°) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daylight at midsummer | about 17½ hours, and the nights never get fully dark | about 16 hours | about 15 hours |
| Daylight at midwinter | about 6½ hours; the noon suns barely clear the hills | about 8 hours | about 9 hours |

Aurora Nights falls in late summer because that's when Northwind's nights get dark enough to see the lights again.

## The three moons

**Fixed by bt 7274** ([D52](Sunday-Morning/Notes/Decisions.md#the-authors-decisions)): the sky has three moons, and Port throws [Three Moon](Culture/Three-Moons-Festival.md) every seven years, when all three are visible from the city.

**How the sky does that** (*lean*). Three moons on three different cycles:
- a small, quick moon, full about every **10 days**;
- a middle moon, full about every **30 days**, the one most calendars count by;
- a slow moon, full about every **67 days**.

Their cycles fit the seven-year span exactly: 255, 86 and 38 turns. So **all three are full on the same night once every seven years, and on the same date each time**. That keeps Three Moon in early autumn.

A check run over those periods found no other night in the seven years when all three come within half a day of full. It did find a near miss or two each cycle, within a day or so, when two are full and the third is close. Those are "false Three Moons", and the almanac-makers argue about them ([Religions](Politics/Religions.md#the-faiths-and-the-sky)).

What three moons do:
- **Nights are seldom fully dark.** Some moon is up most nights, so a truly dark night is an event. That is part of why Northwind names its Aurora Nights.
- **Tides follow all three.** The times shift every day in a pattern only a table can follow. That is why the Tide Road has its tide post *(fixed by the lore)*: the keepers work out the tides, and travellers who misread the post get caught.
- **The great tides.** The biggest tides of the year come near the equinoxes, when the suns and moons pull most nearly in line. That is Tide Return in early spring.
- **Three Moon night brings the highest tide in seven years.** With all three moons full, the suns and moons pull in one line, and the tide runs higher than in any other year. The Narrows runs its hardest race, and Port's low quays go under at high water. A Three Moon festival week is also a week of flood-tide watches, pilots' fees and sandbags.
- **Eclipses are common**, of the suns by a moon and of a moon by the world's shadow. Each faith reads them its own way.

**The myth.** In the creation myth the three continents came together under the Mother, and her three Sons were in the sky ([Religions](Politics/Religions.md#the-creation-myth)). *Lean:* the Sons are the three moons.

## Wind, rain and storms

The [rain model](Reference/Realism-Check-2026-11.md#2-where-the-rain-comes-from) found that wet weather from the **north-west** fits the regions best. *Lean:*

- **The north-west brings the weather**, strongest from late autumn to early spring. That is the gale season, and it is why Northwind's Last Sail falls in late autumn and Ice Breaking and Tide Return in early spring. Long voyages stop in between, as the Mediterranean's sea "closed" every winter in the ancient world.
- **Northwind** gets the most rain and wind: summer fog on the Grey Banks, winter gales, and snow inland.
- **Greenvale** gets rain in every season, with mild winters on the western shores.
- **Highridge** gets snow on its passes and cold, clear winters; its summers are dry, hot by day and cold at night.
  - The lower passes open in early spring (Pass Opening).
  - The high ones, Icestep among them, open later, which fits the Caravan Blessing in late spring.
- **Ironcrest's uplands** have hard winters and snow. Their snowmelt feeds the rivers that keep Deepwood wet.
- **Deepwood floods in late spring**, as the snowmelt comes down. That is the Alderfens' flood season, when the [Canopy Road](Places/Places-of-Interest.md#the-canopy-road) is the way across.
- **Sunplains** gets its rain in winter and has hot, dry summers, a Mediterranean pattern. Blossom Markets in early spring (as almond trees bloom), the Wine Crush in early autumn, and its [drought anxiety](Story/Current-Events.md#drought-anxiety) all follow from it.

## Currents and ice

- **The White Current** *(fixed by the lore)* brings cold water south along the north-west coast. *Lean:*
  - the Grey Banks lie where it wells up over shallow ground, which is why they hold fish and fog;
  - boats bound north along the Salted Coast fight it all the way, so that run is a summer passage.
- **Warmer water along the east and south coasts** (*lean*, unnamed). It is one more reason Sunplains is warm and the east coast is mild.
- **Ice** *(lean)*: northern harbors and the head of the North Gut freeze in most winters, and the open coast only in hard ones. Ice Breaking is the first week the harbor mouths are clear.

## Tides and waves

Measured on the map draft. A wave's size depends on how much open water the wind crosses before it reaches a shore:

| Coast | Under the north-west gales | Harbors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **North and west coasts**, facing the open ocean | full ocean swell; storm seas are dangerous | Gullhaven, Greyquay, Gannet Sound, Kettle Cove, Loamford, Frostbay Way |
| **The Inner Sea**, enclosed, about 70 to 120 miles of open water to windward | short, steep chop in a gale, never ocean swell | **Port**: the sheltered harbor its page asks for |
| **East and south-east coasts**, out of the wind | calm, except in the rarer easterly storms | Accord, Solenne, Cindermouth, Rowanford, Stormwick |
| **The Long Channel and Footwater** | sheltered water | Goodwater, Gleaning |

Notes, all *leans*:
- **Gullhaven faces straight into the gales**, yet "the fleets winter here" ([Gazetteer](Places/Gazetteer.md#northwind)). So its harbor must lie behind a great stone breakwater or deep in a sheltered sound. That suits "stone breakwaters" on [Northwind](Regions/Northwind.md#cities).
- **The Narrows is a tidal race.** The Inner Sea fills and empties through one strait, so the water pours through four times a day. Ships time the Narrows by the tide and take pilots, which is a Port trade and a lever over who gets in.
- **Tides are small inside the Inner Sea** and larger on the open coasts. They are biggest on wide, shallow northern beaches like the Tide Road's.

## The ground

- **The Spine** *(fixed by the lore)* is young enough to shake. *Lean:* earthquakes are a fact of life in the Spine and the Underpass and are rare elsewhere, and there are no active volcanoes.
- **Ironcrest's ores** come from old mountain-building, the same way the world's real ore belts do. Its hot, black water is from the foundries, not the ground.
