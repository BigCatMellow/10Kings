Yes. That actually makes the fantasy version much richer, because you no longer have a cult. You have an entire religious/social ecosystem with two neighboring movements that grew up together, exchanged people and traditions, but developed very different cultures.

Historically, that parallel has some basis. Ron & Fez and Opie & Anthony shared the WNEW environment, appeared together in the "AFRO" shows, and their online communities overlapped. O&A's particularly committed listeners—the "Pests" or "O&A Army"—became known for coordinated actions against rival broadcasts, message boards and other targets; some even adopted ranks such as "General." 

For the fantasy version, I would make the difference deeper than simply nice cult vs. crazy cult.

Two related faiths
The Ron & Fez-derived movement is fundamentally about belonging.

Its people accumulate history together. They know stories about one another. They have strange old phrases nobody remembers the origin of. Minor characters become legends simply because they've been around for twenty years. The religion grows inward.

Its fundamental statement is:

"You had to be there."

The O&A-derived movement is fundamentally about participation and action.

Membership means doing something. Defending the group. Mocking its enemies. Appearing somewhere unexpected. Disrupting another ceremony. Carrying the group's symbols into forbidden places. Taking orders from charismatic figures—or deciding amongst themselves what the leaders surely would have wanted.

Its fundamental statement is:

"What did you do for us?"

That's a very useful distinction.

R&F-derived cult	O&A-derived cult
Community	Army
Belonging	Mobilization
Lore	Campaigns
Relationships	Enemies
Inside jokes	Battle cries
Membership number	Rank
Town square	War room
Civilian becomes character	Follower becomes operative
Gossip becomes story	Conflict becomes mission
Remembers everything	Attacks everything
"Were you there?"	"Did you participate?"
And neither needs to be completely sinister.

That's important.

The militant group might genuinely produce extraordinary loyalty. If one member is in trouble, hundreds show up. If an institution abuses someone they consider one of theirs, they descend on it. Their willingness to mobilize can occasionally accomplish something genuinely good.

But the same machinery can become frightening incredibly quickly.

A charismatic leader says:

"Wouldn't it be funny if somebody put our banner on the Duke's palace?"

Nobody explicitly commands anything.

Three days later there's a banner hanging from the palace.

The leaders laugh.

Now everyone knows what gets rewarded.

That is much more believable than a fantasy leader constantly issuing secret evil orders.

And they should share people
This may be where the concept becomes unusually convincing.

Don't neatly assign every character to one cult.

Have:

People who belong to both.

People who started in one and became important in the other.

People each side claims as one of theirs.

People who are beloved by one movement and despised by the other.

People who can walk freely between the two when almost nobody else can.

And especially:

people whose identities mean different things in the two traditions.

Suppose there was an enormously important jester named Tallow.

To the first movement, Tallow is remembered because of hundreds of conversations, friendships, embarrassments and little stories accumulated over twenty years.

To the second movement, Tallow is remembered for The Night of the Western Gate, when he somehow got himself into the royal coronation and shouted their slogan.

Same man.

Completely different saint.

Five hundred years later, scholars might not initially realize they're reading about the same person.

Their founders can have a similarly complicated relationship
They don't have to be rival prophets.

They might actually like each other.

Their followers make the rivalry much more important than the founders do.

That's interesting because it produces situations where the supposedly hostile religions periodically have what are effectively joint services.

Four founders appear together.

Thousands travel to see it.

For one night the division disappears.

Those events become legendary.

Centuries later theologians argue:

"If the First Speakers considered the Western Brotherhood heretics, why did they perform the Fourfold Gathering together?"

Another school replies:

"They weren't worshipping together. Contemporary accounts clearly describe it as entertainment."

And the orthodox response is:

"That's exactly what a Western apologist would say."

Now you're getting genuine religious-history texture.

The second group also gives the first group definition
Without the neighboring movement, outsiders might just see both as weird devotees of entertainers.

But put them beside each other and their differences become culturally significant.

An R&F-type follower might say:

"Those people don't listen. They just wait to be told who to hate."

An O&A-type follower might say:

"Those people sit around repeating forty-year-old jokes and talking about people nobody remembers."

And they're both partly correct.

That is much better worldbuilding than deciding which group understands the founders properly.

Eventually you could have an entire family tree
These aren't isolated cults anymore.

They came from the same strange cultural explosion.

Perhaps there was originally a new magical technology that allowed voices from the capital to be heard hundreds of miles away. Suddenly ordinary people were experiencing the same personalities and stories every night.

From that emerges:

The First Audience

↓

The two great communities

↙︎ ↘︎

the lore/community tradition
the militant/participatory tradition

↓ ↓

message-house factions, splinter sects, personality cults, rival orders, archivist movements, reform movements

And then the original speakers disappear.

Now nobody is steering any of it.

That's where things could get truly strange.

One descendant group might believe every ridiculous performance literally happened.

Another believes nothing literally happened and that recognizing the performance is the highest form of wisdom.

Another venerates one minor supporting figure whom the founders themselves barely cared about.

One militant splinter still conducts "raids" three hundred years after anyone remembers what the original joke was.

And somewhere an order of obsessive archivist-monks is saying:

"Actually, we recovered a recording from Year 14, and you've all misunderstood this."

That feels like the larger idea to me: not two fantasy versions of radio fandoms, but the birth of religions from participatory communities—and then watching culturally related religions diverge because the communities rewarded different kinds of devotion.

The R&F-inspired side produces mythology through accumulated intimacy.

The O&A-inspired side produces mythology through collective action.

Their proximity, crossover and occasional hostility would make both considerably more believable.

Not to the same depth.

For the fantasy-story purpose, a targeted O&A deep dive would probably be more useful than trying to build another RonFez-scale historical archive. You already have the R&F side doing the heavy lifting for the broader ecosystem. What you need from O&A is enough evidence to understand what made its community structurally different, where the two communities overlapped, and how those differences would translate into a second fantasy movement.

I would focus the research on six things:

Identity and belonging: Pests, O&A Army, ranks, slogans, symbols, terminology.

Collective action: raids, coordinated calls, public stunts, attacks on rival shows/sites, cases where listeners acted without explicit instructions.

Leader–audience relationship: how Opie, Anthony, Jim Norton, staff, and recurring personalities encouraged, restrained, mocked, or lost control of the audience.

Internal culture: hierarchy, cruelty, loyalty, humor, status, gatekeeping, factions, message boards.

R&F crossover: shared staff, callers, events, boards, AFRO shows, people who moved between communities, and how each fandom viewed the other.

Afterlife and fracture: what happened when the show broke apart, how fans chose sides, whether loyalty transferred to individual personalities, and how the community behaved once there was no longer one central institution.

That would let us answer the question that matters for the novel:

Why did two closely related communities, exposed to many of the same people and influences, develop such different forms of devotion?

That's much more valuable than documenting every bit, guest, producer, and date.

I would probably make an O&A research branch inside the RonFez project or a neighboring project, explicitly framed as comparative research rather than another complete history. Then we could build a document like:

R&F vs O&A — Comparative Fandom Sociology

with sections for recruitment, status, ritual, language, leadership, violence/aggression, humor, collective memory, schism, crossover, and post-show survival.

If the research starts uncovering something unexpectedly rich, we can deepen individual branches. But I would not begin by committing to another enormous archive. The danger is spending months reconstructing O&A history when the story only needs the social mechanics.
